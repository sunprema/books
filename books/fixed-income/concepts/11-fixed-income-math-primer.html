The Gentle Number Corner

The real name: “The Fixed-Income Math Primer”
The problem

Ten chapters kept saying “the price is worked out from the payments” and “this much money is lost when the wind turns.” Where do those numbers come from? If the sums stay hidden, they feel like magic, and nobody trusts magic with $500 million. Here are the sums, one at a time, with small numbers. After each one there is a plain sentence that says what the number means.

Lots of people feel their stomach drop when a sum shows up. That is fair. But each sum here is only adding, dividing, and one idea: money later is worth less than money now. All numbers are invented and made round on purpose.

1. Money now and money later

Picture a friend who will hold your $100 and pay you 5 extra for each year. After one year you have $105. After two years the 5% grows on the new pile too, so you have $110.25. This is “compounding”: interest earning interest. Going forward like this is the “future value.”

StepSumResult
$100 for 2 years at 5%100 × 1.05 × 1.05$110.25

Now turn it around. A promise of $100 in three years is worth less than $100 today, because today’s money could be growing. Divide by 1.05 three times: 100 ÷ 1.05 ÷ 1.05 ÷ 1.05 = $86.38. That is the “present value.” The number 0.8638 that does the shrinking is the “discount factor.”

What it means

$86.38 today and $100 in three years are the same deal when the going rate is 5%. A bond’s price is just a pile of these shrunk promises added together.

2. A bond, priced from its promises

Take a bond that pays $6 at the end of each of three years, and $100 back with the last $6. Different dates earn different rates: 4% for one year, 4.5% for two, 5% for three. Shrink each promise with its own factor and add.

The price is 102.83 for each 100 of face value. It is above 100 because the bond pays 6 when the going rates are lower.

3. One rate for the whole bond

Using a different rate for each date is exact but clumsy. Traders want one number to say “how good is this bond?” So they ask: what single rate, used for every date, gives the same price? This is the “yield to maturity.” For the bond above it is 4.96%. There is no neat formula; you try rates until the price matches, and the computer does the trying.

What it means

If you buy at 102.83, hold to the end, and could put every payment to work at that same rate, 4.96% a year is what you earn. Pay more for the same promises and the yield goes down. That is why price and yield move opposite ways.

4. How often you count: the quoting habit

US bonds pay twice a year, and their yields are quoted that way. A “5%” paid in two halves of 2.5% each, with the first half earning on the second, really grows $100 to 100 × 1.025 × 1.025 = $105.0625. So 5% paid twice a year is 5.0625% if you count once a year.

The trap

Compare a twice-a-year yield with a once-a-year yield and you are off by several hundredths of a percent without any error showing. Always check how often a quote is counted before you compare two of them.

5. How days are counted

Interest builds up day by day, but what is a “year”? Each market picked a habit: government bonds count real days against real days; most US company bonds pretend every month has 30 days and the year has 360 (“30/360”); short money loans count real days against 360 (“actual/360”).

See what it does to $1 million at a stated 5% for one year (365 real days):

HabitSumInterest
30/360 (full year = 360 of 360)1,000,000 × 5% × 360/360$50,000
Actual/3601,000,000 × 5% × 365/360$50,694

Same words, same year, $694 apart, about 1.4% more. Nobody is cheating; it is just the habit written in the bond’s papers.

6. Coupon, accrued interest, clean and dirty

A bond with a 5% coupon and $100 face pays 5 a year, as two payments of 2.50. Say 60 days have passed since the last payment, counted 30/360, in a half-year of 180 days. The seller has earned a third of the next payment, and the buyer must pay for it:

PieceSumResult
Accrued interest2.50 × 60 ÷ 1800.83
Quoted (“clean”) price102.25
Cash price (“dirty”)102.25 + 0.83103.08

On $10 million of face value the buyer hands over $10,308,333, not $10,225,000. Then on payment day the buyer receives the full 2.50 per 100, so the 0.83 comes back. Clean is for talking, dirty is for paying.

7. How much it hurts: duration and DV01

Take the 5% three-year bond priced at 100 with a yield of 5%. Weigh each payment by how long you wait for it. The average wait comes out to 2.82 years. This is the “Macaulay duration.” Divide it by (1 + half the yield) and you get 2.75, the “modified duration.”

NumberValuePlain words
Average wait (Macaulay)2.82 yearsWhen you really get your money back, on average
Modified duration2.75Percent of price lost for each 1% yields rise
DV01 per 100 face100 × 2.75 × 0.0001 = 0.0275Price lost per 0.01% yields rise
DV01 on $10 million face$2,754Dollars lost per 0.01% rise

The “DV01” is the number the desk talks about all day. It is “dollar value of one basis point,” and a basis point is one hundredth of a percent. Ten million of this bond loses about $2,754 if yields go up one basis point, and $16,500 for six.

To roll up a whole fund, add the DV01s. A bond worth $2,754 per point plus a futures hedge of −$1,000 per point leaves $1,754 net.

8. The bend: convexity

Duration draws a straight line. The real price line bends (look back at the figure). Check: yields up one full percent, from 5% to 6%.

GuessSumNew price
Straight line only100 − 2.7597.25
Add the bend97.25 + ½ × 9.2 × 0.01² × 100 = 97.25 + 0.0597.30
True pricere-worked in full97.29
What it means

The “convexity” number (9.2 here) fixes what the straight line misses. The line always guesses too gloomy when you lose and too shy when you win, so bends in your favor are valuable. For a bond that can be paid back early, the bend goes the other way, and that costs you. When a big move makes a duration guess look wrong, it is not a broken feed; it is the bend.

9. Gaps: three ways to measure “extra”

Simple gap. Bond yield 5.10%, matching government yield 4.00%: a gap of 1.10%, or 110 basis points. Easy, but it compares one point to one point.

The “Z-spread.” Use the whole curve instead. Our three-year 6% bond is priced at 100.00 while the curve (4%, 4.5%, 5%) says 102.83 for safe promises. To get down to 100.00, add the same extra to every rate on the curve. That extra is 1.04%, or 104 basis points. Plug it back in and the price lands on 100.00.

The “discount margin.” A floating-rate bond pays the going rate plus a fixed extra. Say the extra is 1.00% over a 4% index, three years, price 99. You bought below 100, so you earn more than 1.00%. The one extra that makes the price 99 is 1.37%, or 137 basis points.

The “OAS.” Some bonds can be paid back early by the borrower, and that right is worth something to the borrower and costs the holder. Say the Z-spread is 140 basis points but a model prices the early-payback right at 40. What is left, 100 basis points, is the “option-adjusted spread”: the extra pay for the credit risk alone. The 40 is an output of a model, so another desk’s model may say 35 or 50.

The lesson of this chapter

Every number in this book is a promise, shrunk to today and added up. Change the rate you shrink by and the price moves. How far, and how it bends, is duration and convexity. How much extra you are paid is a gap, and each kind of gap measures against something different.

Questions for every trade

Before any trade is approved, a desk asks these, in roughly this order. Each one points to a chapter.

  1. Thesis. What is the idea in one sentence, and who disagrees with it?
  2. Which view? Rates, curve shape, credit, liquidity, or several? (Chapters 6, 7)
  3. Which risk type am I really taking? Length, gap, early-payback, funding, or the other side failing? (7, 5)
  4. Carry and roll. What does holding it earn each day, after paying for it? (7, 8)
  5. DV01. How many dollars per basis point, and where on the curve? (7)
  6. CS01. How many dollars if the credit gap moves one basis point? (7)
  7. Funding. Who lends against it, at what rate and cushion? (8)
  8. Collateral. What must I hand over, and how much more if prices fall? (8)
  9. Counterparties. Who is on the other side, and how close to their room are we? (5)
  10. Limits. Which are used up, which warn, and who signed? (5)
  11. If it gets hard to trade. What does the exit cost if dealers step back? (4, 9)
  12. If borrowing costs rise. Does the trade still pay? (8)
  13. If gaps widen. What is the loss, and can we stay in? (7)
  14. If yields jump sharply. How wrong is the straight-line guess? (7)
  15. Tomorrow’s explain. Can I name every dollar of the change? (10)
  16. Proof of a fair deal. Are quotes, times, and the reason saved? (4)
  17. Data and models. Which prices are seen and which are modeled, and who checked them? (9)
  18. Cost to unwind. What does it cost to undo this, in a calm week and a bad one?

With a few sums and these questions in your pocket, a price stops being a mystery and becomes something you can check and argue with.

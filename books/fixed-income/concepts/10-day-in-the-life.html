10

One Trade, From Morning to Night

The real name: “A Day in the Life of a Fixed-Income Trade”
The problem

You have learned nine small pieces: what a bond is, who trades it, how a wish becomes a trade, who may be traded with, what the lines and gaps say, how much you lose when the wind turns, how it is paid for, and what a price really is. Pieces on a table are not a machine. This chapter puts them together and runs them once, from the first coffee to the last check of the night.

Our person is a portfolio manager. Call them Sam. Sam looks after a fund of about $500 million. This week Sam holds two beliefs. First, the yields on middle-length government papers will fall. Second, extra yield that companies pay over the government may widen a little. Sam wants to make money from the first belief, keep some of the risk from the second, and not break any rule while doing it.

Everything below uses invented numbers. They are chosen so the sums are easy to follow, not to match any real day.

1. Make the case

Sam does not start with a bond. Sam starts with a story: slower growth should push central-bank hopes for lower rates, so middle-length government yields fall. But slower growth also makes companies a little less safe, so the extra yield investors demand from them may grow. Rate research and company research both feed the story (chapter 9).

Then comes the picking. Sam lines up several company papers from sound borrowers and compares them on the same columns you met in chapters 6 and 7: yield, the gap over the government line (the “spread”), the length-risk, the credit-risk, the income from holding it, and the “roll,” the small gain as a paper slides closer to its end date. One paper wins: a seven-year bond from a steady, well-rated borrower. Its yield is 5.10% when the matching government paper yields 4.00%, a gap of 110 basis points. The paper looks a little cheap next to its peers, but not so cheap that Sam stops asking why.

2. Look at what you already hold

Before adding anything, Sam looks at the shelf (chapter 3). What is held today, by length and by borrower? How much cash is coming in and going out this week? What is the fund’s total sensitivity to a one-hundredth-of-a-percent move in rates (the “DV01”), where along the line does it sit (the “key-rate” numbers), and how much does it lose if company gaps widen (the “CS01”)? Is the fund already heavy in one borrower or one industry? How much of it could be sold quickly? How much of it is already pledged to lenders?

3. Ask the rules first

Sam builds the idea as a proposed trade (chapters 3 and 5): buy $10 million of face value of this bond, sell some government futures against it, finance it with a loan against the bond itself. Then the checks run, before anything is shown to a dealer.

  • The fund’s own job description: company papers of this rating are allowed.
  • One-borrower limit: 3% of the fund is $15 million. $3 million held plus $10 million bought is $13 million, or 2.6%. Passes, with a little room.
  • Industry, rating, country: still inside their bands.
  • Sensitivity limits: the trade adds a small amount of length-risk and credit-risk; fund DV01 stays inside its band, and the loss-in-a-bad-week test still passes.
  • Sam’s own authority: a $10 million ticket is under Sam’s sign-off size.
  • Who it may be traded with: only dealers that are approved, with papers signed and room left on their limits.

One result is a warning, not a stop: the borrower limit is above 85% used after the trade. Sam must note a reason and the warning is logged. A warning that the system lets through is a record. A warning that nobody can find later is a problem.

4. Find who will sell

A company bond has no single screen price. Sam’s trading desk checks three things (chapter 4). First, axes: dealers who have told the market they want to sell this paper or similar ones. Second, history: who quoted well on this borrower before, how fast they replied, how big they went. Third, the desk’s own preferences: a minimum number of quotes, preferred dealers, and rules about which tool to use for a ticket of this size. Ten million is a medium size, so a “request for quote” sent to four dealers fits.

5. Buy it

The request goes out to four dealers at once. The answers come back within a minute:

The best price for the whole amount is A’s 99.45. B is a cent and a half cheaper per $100 but fills only half, which would leave Sam running a second hunt for the rest and telling more of the market. Sam buys from A. The trade is captured with its time stamp, all four quotes, and the reason (best price for the full size, backed by the axis). That bundle is the proof of a fair deal that compliance will want later.

The money for the trade is more than the clean price. The buyer also pays the interest the bond has earned since the last payment day (chapter 1). Here that is 0.55 per 100, so the full price is 100.00 and the cash to hand over is $10,000,000. The trade is split to the right fund account (allocation), confirmed, affirmed by both sides, and set to settle the next business day. US company bonds now settle one day after the trade.

6. Pay for it

The fund has $2 million spare and needs $10 million. It does what most desks do and borrows against the bond (chapter 8). In a “repo,” the fund hands the bond to a lender and gets cash. The lender does not lend the full worth: it holds back a cushion, the “haircut.” Say the cushion is 5%, so the lender gives $9.5 million, and the fund covers the other $500,000 itself. The loan rate is 4.30%.

That $80,000 a year, about $220 a day, is the real “carry” in this trade. The headline gap of 110 basis points over the government looked bigger; money cost and the cushion ate much of it. This is the lesson of chapter 8 in a small box: a paper that looks good on yield alone may look thin once it is paid for. And one more thing sits behind the loan: if the bond falls in price or the lender gets nervous, the cushion can grow and Sam must find more cash on a bad day.

7. Cover part of the wind

Sam wants the credit view, not a full bet on rates. Wait, Sam does believe government yields will fall, which would help a bond holder. So why hedge at all? Because the rule book wants the fund’s total length-risk to stay near its target, and because Sam sees the rate view as already well-covered by the rest of the fund. So Sam covers about 60% of the new bond’s rate risk and leaves the rest as a chosen bet.

  • The bond’s DV01 is about $6,000 per basis point. (Length-risk of about 6 on $10 million: 6 × 10,000,000 ÷ 10,000.)
  • Sam wants to cover $3,600 of it, 60%.
  • A ten-year government futures contract moves about $75 per basis point, an invented but sensible size. So Sam sells 48 contracts: 3,600 ÷ 75 = 48.

Why futures and not a swap? Futures are simple, cheap in size, and clear through an exchange, but they ask for daily cash margin. A swap can be cut to fit the exact length of the bond, but it brings a paperwork pile and its own counterparty limits (chapter 5). Sam picks futures for speed and takes the cost, which is a mismatch: the bond is seven years long and the contract follows something closer to ten. If the seven- and ten-year points move by different amounts, the hedge will not match the bond. That is “key-rate” risk and “basis” risk, and Sam writes both on the risk sheet.

What is left over

After the hedge, the trade’s length-risk is about $2,400 per basis point (6,000 − 3,600). Its credit-risk is untouched: about $5,800 per basis point of gap. Sam kept the thing Sam wanted.

8. Watch it all day

Now the trade is a living thing. Prices change, so the bond, the futures and the loan are re-valued with fresh data (chapter 9): the bond from dealer quotes and an evaluated price, the futures from the exchange, the loan from the day’s rate. The desk watches the government line, the gap, the borrowing rate, the cash the lender and the exchange want held, the dealer limits used, and the borrower limit used. Futures lose or gain cash daily; the cushion on the bond loan is re-checked against the bond’s price. Nothing here is dramatic on a calm day, and that is the point: a desk finds trouble when it is small by looking at it every day.

9. Explain the day’s gain or loss

Next day, the government yield fell 3 basis points, as Sam hoped. But the gap on this company widened 2 basis points, as Sam also half-expected. Did Sam make money? The explain report splits the answer (chapter 7).

The total is a small loss, and that is a good sign that the hedge did its job. Rates moved Sam’s way and, after the hedge, the rate lines left only a gain of about $7,200. The gap widening cost $11,600, more than that. Sam’s own view was “gaps may widen a little”, and Sam bought the bond anyway, so this day is a cost of the bet, not a surprise. The “left over” line is small but not zero, and the desk will ask why. A residual that keeps growing means a hedge, a data feed or a model is broken.

10. Break it on purpose

A desk does not only ask “what happened?” It asks “what if?” Stress means pushing the whole package, bond, hedge and loan together, since they fail together (chapter 8). Using the sensitivities above:

It is the rows stacked: the gap widens, the cushion grows, cash is short, so the fund must sell into a market with a thinner crowd, at a worse price, which widens the gap more. That ring is why Sam keeps cash spare and the borrower limit at 2.6% and not at its edge.

The lesson of this chapter

A trade is not the buy click. It is a bond, a loan, a hedge, a few limits and a daily story, all moving together. Look at any one piece alone and you will like the trade too much.

What to ask before and after

  • Which exact view am I paying for, and which risks did I choose to keep?
  • After the loan, the cushion and the hedge, what is the trade really earning?
  • Which limits are close, and who knows?
  • If the hedge follows the wrong point on the line, what do I lose?
  • Tomorrow, can I explain every dollar of the change?
  • If everything goes wrong at once, can I stay in the trade, or am I forced out?

Seen as one day’s work, the whole book is a habit: say what you believe, check what you may do, pay for it honestly, cover what you do not want, and be ready to explain and survive the bad day.

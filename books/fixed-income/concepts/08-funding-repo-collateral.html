Borrowing Cash, Handing Over Pledges

The real name: “Funding, Repo, Collateral, and Settlement”
The problem

You see a loan paper that pays a little more than it should. You buy it. The boss is happy, until the end of the month, when the bill for the money you borrowed to buy it comes in, and it eats most of what the paper paid. Nobody was lying. You just counted the pay and forgot the price of the cash.

Most people picture a trade as one step: you pay money, you get a paper. On a real desk, someone has to hold the cash for as long as you hold the paper. This chapter is about who that is, what they ask you to hand over while they wait, and what happens on the day they ask for more.

Why a position needs money underneath it

Buying a ten-million paper means ten million in cash goes out the door. There are two ways to find it. Use cash you already have, or borrow it. A fund that borrows to hold more than it owns is “levered”: the same bit of its own money now rides on a much bigger pile, so wins and losses are both bigger.

Money has a price, and the price changes. The cheapest borrowing is the kind where the lender holds something of yours until you pay back. Inside a big firm, the desk that holds the paper is also charged for the cash by the firm’s own money desk, at an inside rate called “transfer pricing.” That is how the firm makes each desk feel what its borrowing costs. A firm also has only so much room on its books for borrowing and lending (“balance sheet”), and the room is not free: banks ration it, and ration it harder when times are bad.

The pledge swap

Picture two friends. You have a bike and need cash for a week. Your friend has cash and wants it back, plus a little. You hand over the bike, take the cash, and promise to swap again next week: cash back to your friend, bike back to you, plus a small fee. The bike is the pledge. If you vanish, your friend keeps the bike.

That is what the market calls a “repo” (short for repurchase agreement). On paper it is a sale of a loan paper with a promise to buy it back at a set price and day. In spirit it is a loan with the paper as the pledge, and the gap between the two prices is the interest. The friend on the other side is doing a “reverse repo”: cash out, paper in. Same deal, seen from the lender’s chair.

Kinds of swap

  • Overnight: done today, undone tomorrow, and often done again tomorrow.
  • Term: a set length, a week or three months. The rate is fixed for that stretch.
  • Open: rolls forward each day until one side says stop.
  • Three-way (“tri-party”): a big bank in the middle picks the pledges, prices them each day, and keeps them safe, so the two friends need not.

Any pledge, or that one

If the lender does not care which paper they get, only that it is good enough, that is a “general collateral” deal, and it trades at the plain going rate. If the lender wants one particular paper (because they sold it and must hand it over, or because it is in short supply), the deal is “special.” The lender of that paper can take a lower rate on the cash, since they are being paid in a different way, by being the one who has the rare thing. A paper that is “on special” is expensive to short and cheap to hold with financing. The gap between the plain rate and the special rate is the market’s price of scarcity. The same sum, read the other way, gives the “implied repo rate”: if you buy a paper now and sell it later at a set price, what borrowing rate would make the trade break even? If you can borrow below that rate, the trade pays. If not, it does not. (Unsourced domain knowledge.)

The pledge is worth less than it says

Look at the figure again. The lender hands over 95 against a paper worth 100. The 5 is the “haircut.” It is the lender’s cushion: if you disappear and the paper has dropped, they can sell it and still get their cash. The riskier the paper, or the riskier you look, the bigger the haircut. A government paper might need a sliver; a weak company’s paper might need a lot.

For you, the haircut is your own money, locked up for as long as the swap lasts. That makes it the part that decides your leverage. A haircut of 5 means you can hold 20 of paper for each 1 of your own. A haircut of 10 means 10 for each 1. Half the leverage, from one number someone else picks.

The lesson of this chapter

A trade that looks good on yield or spread can look bad once you add the price of the cash, the money the haircut locks up, the margin calls that follow a price drop, and the chance that the lender gets nervous. All four belong in the expected return, not on a separate page that someone else reads.

Collateral: what you hand over, and when

“Collateral” is the pledge, in any kind of deal where one side might owe the other money. Lenders keep a short list of what they accept (“eligibility”): usually government papers and the best company papers, less often the weak or odd ones. Each kind gets its own haircut. A few other words you will hear:

  • Margin call: a demand, often due the same day, to hand over more pledges or cash because the pledge lost value or the deal grew.
  • Excess collateral: you have handed over more than needed, and you want some back. Cash you are not using is cash you are not earning on.
  • Substitution: swapping one pledge for another of the same worth, so you can sell the first or use it somewhere else.
  • Valuation and disputes: both sides must agree what the pledge is worth today. When they use different prices, they argue, and the call waits. A dispute can last days while the cash gap sits there.
  • Concentration: one lender may refuse to take too much of the same paper. A pile of the same kind is hard to move around.
  • Optimization: a desk with many pledges and many places that want them picks the cheapest paper to hand over each time, and keeps the best paper for where it is worth most.

The same idea for swaps and futures

A swap or a future is not a loan, but it can still leave one side owing the other. So pledges move there too, in two parts. “Variation margin” follows today’s gain or loss: the loser hands money to the winner, every day. “Initial margin” is a deposit up front, sized for how much the deal could lose before it can be closed out. Two ways to do it:

  • Between two firms (“bilateral”): the rules are in the “CSA,” a side agreement to the master contract, which says what counts as a pledge, what haircuts apply, and how often to settle up (chapter 5).
  • Through a middle house (“cleared”): a central clearing house steps between buyer and seller, takes both initial and variation margin from each side, and promises each that the other will pay.

Both ways turn a price move into a cash demand. Your DV01 (chapter 7) is not only a profit-and-loss number; on a hedge it is also a cash number. A hedge that wins on paper on the other side of a loss still asks you for cash on the day, while the paper you are hedging pays you nothing until you sell.

Settlement: the handshake at the end

A trade is a promise made today. Settlement is the day it becomes true: the paper moves, the cash moves (chapter 1 gave the trade date and the settlement date). The safe way is “delivery versus payment”: both sides move at the same moment, or neither does, so nobody is ever left having paid for nothing.

The pieces behind it: a custodian keeps the paper safe for you; a clearing house matches and guarantees; and settlement instructions are the standing directions of who gets what, where. A wrong account, a mismatch in size, or a missing instruction is the dull cause of many late deliveries. Day-of events such as a coupon payment or a company call on the paper (“corporate actions”) change what you hold and what you are owed, and must be matched against what you expected.

When the paper does not come

If the seller cannot deliver on the day, that is a “fail.” It is a nuisance and a cost: you have the cash tied up and no paper to show for it. The market built a price on it. In the US government market, a recognised group of dealers set a charge for a fail, so that waiting is never free, even when interest rates are near nothing. The charge is up to 3% a year minus the going rate for short loans, so when rates are near zero, a fail costs close to the whole 3%.

One fail can start many. The dealer who did not get the paper often cannot hand it on, and the next one cannot either, like a line of people each waiting for the one in front to leave a chair. That chain is the reason the charge exists, and the reason a buyer who has waited too long may have the right to “buy in”: go and buy the paper somewhere else at the seller’s cost. When the lenders get nervous

None of the above is a problem on a calm day. The trouble is that the pieces lean on each other. The worked case already showed it: a price drop causes a call; a call forces a sale; the sale pushes prices down; and the lender, watching, asks for a bigger haircut, which causes another call. Two kinds of “can I get out?” become one: how easily can you sell (market liquidity), and how easily can you get cash (funding liquidity). When one gets worse, so does the other. Economists call this a “margin spiral” or “liquidity spiral.”

What it looks like from the desk, in the order it usually shows up:

  • Rising swap rates: your carry shrinks or turns negative, as in the 5.1% case.
  • Haircut increases: your own money locked up grows overnight.
  • Scarce collateral: the good papers you need to hand over are in short supply, and you pay to borrow them.
  • Margin calls: same-day cash demands, from swaps and futures as well as from lenders.
  • Lenders leaving: some will not roll your overnight deal tomorrow at any rate. A desk that relied on overnight money has to find new friends in an afternoon.
  • Dealer room shrinking: the banks that pass cash between lenders and borrowers hit their own balance-sheet limits just when everyone calls.
  • Forced selling: the loop in the figure. You sell the paper you would never sell, at the price you would never take.
Cash kills faster than loss

A position can be right and still sink you, if you cannot meet the call before it comes good. A good desk checks not only “what do I lose if the price moves?” but “what cash will I be asked for if it does, and where does it come from?”

What to ask before the trade

  • Who lends the cash, for how long, and at what rate? What if the rate rises?
  • What is the haircut, and how much of my own money does that lock up?
  • If the price falls a little, what call arrives, and when is it due?
  • Is the paper easy to hand over, or could it fail or be hard to deliver?
  • If my lender walks away tomorrow, who else would lend, and at what price?

Counting the cost of the cash, the money the pledge locks up, and the calls that follow a bad day lets a desk keep only the trades that still pay once the bill has come.

A Pile of Promised Payments, and Who Owes Whom

The real name: “Fixed Income Is a Business of Cash Flows”
The problem

Your neighbor Maya needs money today to build a bakery. She does not have it. You have some extra, but you want it back, and you want a little more for waiting. If you just hand her the money and say “pay me when you can,” you will lie awake at night. You will not know how much, or when, or whether.

The fix: write the promises down

So the two of you write a paper. It says: Maya gives you a little money every six months, and on one set day she gives back all you lent. That paper is a bond. Nothing more. The world of “fixed income” is the work of buying, selling, paying for, guarding and keeping track of such papers.

The money that moves on those set days is called “cash flow.” The big idea of this whole book fits in one line: a bond is not really a thing; it is a list of days and amounts of money. Everything a bond desk does is about that list: finding what it is worth, paying for it, guarding it from harm, and keeping track of it.

The four parts of the paper

Plain nameReal nameWhat it means
The size of the loan“principal,” “face value” or “par”The amount you get back at the end. Prices are quoted per 100 of it.
The little payments“coupon”A fixed share of the face paid again and again, often every six months.
The last day“maturity”The day the loan ends and the face comes back.
The one who owes“issuer”The one who wrote the paper and must pay.

Say the face is $1,000,000, the coupon is 5 in a year, paid in two parts, and about four and a half years are left. Then you are promised $25,000 about every six months, and on the last day, $1,000,000 plus the last $25,000. Draw it and you get a row of small bars and one tall bar at the end.

Careful

The name says “fixed,” but not every row is fixed. Some papers change the little payment as the market rate moves (“floating-rate notes”). Some grow the face when prices rise. Some house-loan bundles get paid back early, by surprise (“prepayment”). Some let the borrower end the loan early (“callable”). What is fixed is the promise to pay by a rule, not always the amount. And a promise can be broken: the one who owes may fail to pay.

Who is in the room

Lending and borrowing through papers has many people in it, and each does one job:

  • Borrowers (“issuers”): governments, firms, towns, and groups that bundle loans. They write the papers.
  • Lenders (“investors”): money managers, pension funds, insurance firms, banks, and funds. They hold the papers and collect the payments. They are called the “buy side.”
  • Shops that stand in the middle (“dealers”): big banks that keep a stock of papers, and will buy from you or sell to you at a price they name. They are called the “sell side.” Most bond buying and selling goes through them, not through one big open market.
  • Go-betweens (“brokers” and trading screens): they help you find the other side, without holding the paper themselves.
  • Keepers (“custodians”): safe places that hold the papers for the owner and move them when a deal is done.
  • The rate-setter (the “central bank”): it sets the price of the shortest loans, and that pulls the price of all other loans with it. When it moves, every row of promised money is worth a new amount.

Why the same row is worth different amounts

A promise of $1,000 a year from now is worth less than $1,000 today. You could have used the money, and the one who owes might not pay. So the price of a bond is what all the promised money is worth today, after shrinking each piece for the wait. The shrink rate is called the “yield.”

Picture it

Think of a seesaw. On one end sits the price, on the other the yield. The payments on the paper are glued in place and cannot change. So when buyers ask for a higher yield, the only way to give it to them is for the price to go down. When buyers settle for a lower yield, the price goes up. They always move in opposite directions.

This gives three kinds of price, named by how the coupon compares with what buyers ask today:

If the coupon is……the bond sells atPrice per 100
the same as the yield buyers ask“par”100
higher than the yield buyers aska “premium”above 100
lower than the yield buyers aska “discount”below 100

A premium bond drifts down toward 100 as the last day nears, and a discount bond drifts up. The loan ends at the face, so every bond is pulled home to 100. This is called “pull to par.”

One bond, bought step by step

Now buy one. A firm that makes shoes, rated safe enough, has a bond with a 5 coupon paid every six months. You buy $1,000,000 of face at a quoted price of 102.25. That quote is a premium: the bond’s coupon is higher than what buyers ask today, which is roughly 4.5 a year (a rough guess, to give the picture).

The trap

You would think you pay 102.25 percent of $1,000,000, which is $1,022,500. You pay more. The quote leaves out something, and that something is the next topic.

The part the quote leaves out

The shoe firm pays a coupon on the 15th of March and September. Say you buy and the deal is settled on June 18. The seller held the bond for the days since March 15, and in that time the coupon kept growing like a plant, even though no money was paid out. When the coupon comes in September, you will get the whole thing. That is not fair to the seller, so you pay the seller their share of the days up front. That share is called “accrued interest.”

Many bonds of firms count a month as 30 days and a year as 360 days. From March 15 to June 18 that is 93 days:

accrued = $1,000,000 × 5% × 93 ÷ 360 = $12,916.67
PieceAmountPlain meaning
Quoted (“clean”) price, 102.25$1,022,500.00What the bond itself is worth

= Cash you hand over (“dirty” price)$1,035,416.67The real money that moves

In September you get the whole $25,000. Of that, $12,916.67 only pays you back for what you gave the seller. The rest, $12,083.33, is what you truly earned for the 87 days you held it. Fair for both sides.

Two dates, not one

You shake hands (or click) on the “trade date.” The cash and the paper change hands on the “settlement date.” For firm bonds in the United States that is now one business day later, a rule that began in May 2024. Interest is counted up to the settlement day, not the handshake day. So the day you agree and the day you own it are not the same, and a lot of work happens in between.

What can hurt you after you buy

  • Rate exposure. If market yields go up, your bond’s price goes down. You hold it a long time, so the drop is real on paper right away.
  • Credit exposure. The shoe firm could look weaker, so buyers want more yield from it, and the price drops. Worse, it could fail to pay.
  • Selling and paying-for exposure. You may not find a buyer fast at a fair price when you want one. And you paid $1,035,416.67 in cash: if that money was borrowed, the borrowing cost eats into what the coupon earns, and the lender may ask you for more pledges if the price falls.

Four words that sound the same but are not

Desks use four different words, and mixing them up causes real mistakes:

  • The paper (“security”): the bond itself, with its list of dates and amounts.
  • The one who owes (“issuer”): the shoe firm. It may have many papers out at once.
  • What can hurt you (“exposure”): rate, credit, and so on. One bond carries several, and many different papers can carry the same one.
  • What you hold (“position”): how much of a paper you own or owe, in which account. Buy $1,000,000 today and your position grows by $1,000,000 of face.

Each of these gets its own look in the next chapter. For now, keep one picture in your head: in all of them there is a row of dates, a pile of money on each date, and someone who has to bring it.

When you see every bond as a row of promised money, you can ask the same few questions of any of them: how much, on which day, from whom, and what is it worth if the world changes.

From “Here Is Your Job” to “Here Is What We Hold”

The real name: “From Mandate to Portfolio Position”
The problem

Someone hands a money manager a sheet of rules and says, “Here is your job.” A day later there is a list of papers in a box, some cash, and a few borrowed things. How did the first become the second? And when five different rooms in the same firm each show a different number for “what we hold,” which one is right? Most of them, it turns out, and that is the trouble.

The job sheet

Every pot of money starts with a written job (the “mandate”). It says what the money is for and what it must not do. Three things sit inside it:

  • The yardstick (“benchmark”): a made-up basket of papers the manager is measured against. Beat it, and you did well. Trail it, and you did not.
  • The rules (“investment guidelines”): no paper below a certain grade, no more than a set share with one company, the length of the whole pile kept near the yardstick’s length.
  • The goal: earn steady pay, grow the pot, or match what the owner must pay out later.

Big pots are cut up. A firm may hold many accounts (one per owner), and inside one account there may be sleeves (one slice run by one team, like “short papers only”) and strategies (the plan a slice follows). Each has its own rules, so the same paper can be fine in one slice and banned in the next.

Owning, and owing

A long position means you hold the paper and gain when it rises. A short position means you sold a paper you do not own, by borrowing it first, and you gain when it falls. Now add them up in two ways:

Way to addReal nameLong 100, short 90What it tells you
Subtract the short from the long“net exposure”10How much you win or lose if everything moves together
Add both sides“gross exposure”190How big the book really is: what must be funded, checked and held on the books

A small net can hide a huge gross. Risk, borrowing cost and the space the firm’s lenders see often follow the gross, not the net.

Three ways to say “how much”

People say “a ten million position” and mean different things. Keep these apart:

  • Face value (“par,” “notional”): the principal the paper promises to pay back. This is the count of how many paper units you hold.
  • Market value: what the holding is worth in cash today, which is price times holding. A paper bought at 102.25 with 10 million face is worth about 10.225 million, before the accrued part you met in chapter 1.
  • Risk size: how much you lose per small move in rates. Two holdings with the same face can have very different risk, because a long paper moves much more than a short one. Chapter 7 gives this its own name.

Next, lots. If you buy the same paper three times at three prices, you hold three lots, each with its own buy date and price. When you sell part, someone must say which lot you sold, because that sets the gain and, for taxable owners, the tax (“tax lots”). A position is really a stack of lots, not one number.

Two clocks: trade day and settle day

You met these in chapter 1. A deal made today pays and delivers a day or more later. So there are two honest answers to “what do we hold?”

  • Trade-date position: counts the paper the moment the deal is made. Traders and risk people use this, because you are already exposed to the price.
  • Settlement-date position: counts it only when the paper and cash have changed hands. Custody and cash people use this.

The gap is “bought but not yet delivered.” It matters: the paper moves in price today, but the cash is still in your account, and someone must plan to send it.

That leads to the cash position: money in the account, minus money that must go out soon (to pay for new buys), plus money coming in (coupons, principal paid back, sales). Looking ahead at these is called projecting cash flows. A manager who forgets this finds out on settlement day that the account is short.

Is it really ours to use?

An investor’s holdings are what they own. A dealer’s pile (“inventory”) is bigger, because a dealer buys to sell on. For either, a paper in the list is not always free to use. Sort it like this:

StatePlain meaningCan you sell it today?
AvailableOurs, free and clearYes
BorrowedNot ours; we borrowed it, perhaps to cover a short saleOnly to hand it back
Lent outOurs, but someone else holds it for nowOnly after it comes back
PledgedHanded over to back a loan or a promiseNo
FinancedUsed to get cash today (see chapter 8)Not freely

Anything pledged or otherwise tied up is “encumbered.” It looks like a holding on the list but cannot be sold or lent. When a trader asks what can be sold today, the answer is the available pile, not the whole list. And a dealer’s pile is never free: every paper held must be paid for with borrowed cash or the firm’s own money, so “the desk is holding it” always has a running cost.

Stacking positions into a picture

A list of a thousand lots tells nobody anything. So the firm adds them up along one line at a time (“aggregation”): by account, company, sector, country, grade, money type (“currency”), length bucket, and by the kind of risk. The same pile gives a different picture each way. Rules are checked on these sums: “no more than five in one company” is a sum over every paper that company wrote.

One day in the life of a position

A position is not a fixed fact. It is rebuilt every day by a chain of events:

  • Morning list: yesterday’s evening list, with anything that settled overnight.
  • Orders: wishes to buy or sell, held back until rules say yes (chapter 4).
  • Executions: the deals actually struck, each with a price and a size.
  • Allocations: a big deal is often made once, then split among accounts. The split says how much of the one deal goes to each. Until it is done, nobody knows which account holds what.
  • Amendments: fixes to a wrong price, size or account. They can change yesterday’s list after the fact.
  • Settlements: paper and cash actually move.
  • Coupon and principal events: pay lands, or a paper ends and disappears from the list.
  • Financing: borrowed cash and pledged papers are rolled or changed for the night.
  • Evening list: the new truth, which feeds risk and profit reports.
Worked example. Morning: you hold 10 million face of one company’s paper. Orders: the manager wants 5 million more. Execution: one deal for 5 million at 101.50. Allocation: 3 million goes to account A, 2 million to account B. Evening: A holds 13 million and B’s total grew by 2 million. Because the deal settles in a day or two, the settlement-date list still shows 10 million tonight, while the trade-date list shows 15 million across both accounts. Both are right. They answer different questions.

Gain and loss, in two kinds

Profit and loss (P&L) splits into two parts:

  • Realized: locked in. You sold a lot, or you received a coupon or a principal payment. The cash is real.
  • Unrealized: on paper only. The paper you still hold is worth more or less than it cost. Revaluing every holding at today’s price is “marking to market.”

A paper that jumps in price and is not sold has gained, but only on paper, and it can give it back. And for the unrealized part, the price used for the mark matters, since a thin paper may have no fresh deal to read it from (chapter 9).

Five rooms, five lists

Back to the opening puzzle. Each room keeps its own view of the same position, built for its own job:

The daily work of “reconciliation” is lining these up and chasing every difference. A difference is rarely a mystery. It is usually a late allocation, an amendment that one room has not seen, a settlement that failed, or two prices from two sources.

The shape of the data, in one glance

Behind the screens, the same ideas become tables that point at each other. A rough sketch:

The Portfolio is the root of the tree: orders are made for it, deals come out of orders, allocations send pieces of each deal into its lots, and every lot is a stack of one Instrument. Rules (“limits”) attach to the Portfolio, to the company, or to the other side of the deal (“Counterparty”). That is why one order touches nearly everything.

A job sheet becomes a position through a daily chain of orders, deals, splits, fixes and payments, and every room tells the same story from its own clock and its own prices, so the first question about any number is always: which list is this?

How a Wish to Buy Turns Into a Bought Thing

The real name: “Orders, Axes, and Execution”
The problem

You want to buy a used boat. There is no shop with a price tag on it. Instead there are a few sellers, each with a different price, and none of them has to tell you theirs unless you ask. If you shout your plan to the whole dock, every seller raises the price. If you ask only one, you do not know if it is a good price. And the one who badly wants to get rid of a boat today? You would never find them by luck.

Most paper that pays interest is bought this way. Chapter 3 ended with a job sheet turned into a list of what we hold. This chapter is the step that puts new things on that list: a wish becomes a real deal.

Where the buying happens

Shares of a company mostly trade in one big shared book of offers that anyone can see. Paper that pays interest mostly does not. It trades in a dealer market (a “quote-driven market”): a few big firms stand ready to buy from you or sell to you at prices they set, and they make their money from the gap between the two. There are so many different papers, and most of them so rarely change hands, that a single shared book would sit empty most of the day.

Here are the main ways a deal gets made:

WayThe pictureGood for
Phone or chat (“voice”)You talk to a person you knowLarge, awkward, or rarely traded papers
Ask-several-sellers screen (“RFQ”)You send one question to a few sellers at once and pick the best answerThe everyday way, by far
Selling day (“auction”)A government or company sells new paper to the highest biddersNew paper, not old
Shared book of offers (“CLOB”)Everyone’s offers sit in one listThe most traded government paper
Price stream (“request for stream”)A seller keeps sending you live prices until you stop themSmall, quick deals
Everyone with everyone (“all-to-all”)Buyers can trade with other buyers, not only with dealersFinding a seller outside the usual circle
Whole basket (“portfolio trade”)Dozens or hundreds of papers priced and traded as one packageMoving a lot at once with one price

The ask-several-sellers screen is the main electronic way, and the all-to-all and basket ways have grown as add-ons to it. Voice is still alive for the hard cases.

The words for a price

A dealer shows two prices. The bid is what they will pay you if you sell. The offer (or “ask”) is what they want if you buy. The offer is higher. The middle of the two is the mid, and the gap is the bid-ask spread. Think of it as the toll you pay to cross the river: the wider the river, the bigger the toll.

  • Size: how much the price is good for. A price for one million says nothing about ten.
  • Depth: how much can be done near the best price before it moves.
  • Indicative or firm: an “indicative” price is a sign in the window, not a promise. An “executable” price is one the seller will really do right now, for that size. Only the second counts.
  • Slippage: the difference between the price you hoped for and the price you got.
  • Market impact: your own buying pushes the price up against you. Bigger wish, bigger push.
  • Information leakage: sellers who lose the contest still learn you want to buy. Some may buy ahead of you.
Lots of asking is not free

Asking more sellers gets you more answers, so the best one tends to improve. But every extra seller is one more mouth that knows your plan. Good desks pick a number of sellers that fits the size and the paper: fewer for big, thin deals, more for small, busy ones.

What kinds of order can you give?

An order is the written wish before any deal exists: which paper, buy or sell, how much, and any limit on the price. Common kinds:

  • Market order: “Get it done at whatever price is there.” Fast, and the price is left to luck.
  • Limit order: “Do it, but not above this price when buying.” You are safe on price and may not get filled at all.
  • Stop order: “If the price falls to this level, sell.” A safety net, not a plan.
  • Target yield or target spread: the limit is written as the pay-rate you want rather than a price. Some people think in pay-rate, and a price follows from it.
  • Relative-value order: “Buy this paper and sell that one, as long as the gap between them is at least so much.” The gap matters more than either price.
  • List or basket order: many papers in one instruction, worked as a group.

Orders also carry rules about how they may be worked: how long they stay alive, whether a trader may fill part now and part later, and which sellers and screens are allowed.

The seller who wants to sell today

Back to the dock. One seller has a boat they must move before the end of the month. They will take less than the others. If you could find that person, you would buy better. In this market that sign is called an “axe”: a dealer’s real interest in buying or selling one particular paper, usually because of something they already hold, something they owe, or a customer they are working for.

Why would you care? A dealer with an axe often trades inside the usual gap, and for a larger size than they normally would, because they want the deal and will pay for it. The dealer’s gain is getting rid of a risk. Yours is a better price.

Kind of axeWhat it points atExample
Paper axeOne exact bond“We offer 5 million of this company’s bond due 2032.”
Sector axeA group of similar papers“We like to sell bank bonds.”
Curve axeA spot on the line of lengths“We want to buy ten-year-ish risk.”
Risk axeA kind of exposure, not any one paper“We are long a lot of company-risk and want to cut it.”

Axes also come strong or weak. A strong one is fresh, sized, and backed by a dealer who has acted on it before. A weak one is stale, tiny, or put out only to look busy. Telling the two apart is a skill, and the desk’s own records are what teach it: it tracks which sellers did what they said.

A sign, not a promise

An axe tells you whom to ask first. It does not give you a price. The firm price still comes only when you ask for it, so an axe works together with the ask-several-sellers step, not instead of it.

Memory, and the desk’s habits

Every deal leaves a trail, and a good desk keeps all of it:

  • the time the question went out and the time each answer came back,
  • every seller’s price, size, and how fast they replied (the losers too),
  • the screen used, the size done, any part-fills, and any changes made,
  • and the reason the trader gave for picking this answer.

Two jobs for this trail. First, proof: when someone asks later “did you get a fair deal?” (“best execution”), the record answers. Second, learning: it shows which sellers really show up with size, and which only show a window sign.

The desk also writes down its habits as preferences, so they apply the same way every time:

  • favorite sellers, and a ranking of them for each kind of paper,
  • the least number of prices to collect before choosing,
  • which screens and ways of dealing are allowed,
  • deal sizes above which a person must step in,
  • a banned list of sellers or papers,
  • and which small deals may be done by machine with no person watching (“auto-execution”).

The whole trip, start to end

Now put it in a line. Each step has a job, and a deal can stop at any of them:

After the deal, three plain jobs remain. The dealer sends its record of the terms (the “confirmation”). The buyer says “yes, that matches” (the “affirmation”). Then paper and cash swap (“settlement”). Shared matching services handle the split-by-account, confirm and agree steps for many firms at once. As the time between deal and settlement has shrunk to one day in the United States, the “yes, that matches” step has been pushed to the evening of the deal day itself, so any mismatch is found before it can cause a failed delivery.

One buy, from the first check to the last

A manager decides to buy 10 million face of a company bond, the same kind as the 102.25 bond in chapter 1. Here is the trip.

  1. Decision. The manager writes an order: buy 10 million, limit 102.40 or lower, for the account that follows the “short and safe” sleeve.
  2. Checks before the deal. The system asks: does the sleeve’s job sheet allow this company and this grade? Would one company’s share go above its cap? Would the length of the pile stay inside its band? Is there enough cash coming for settlement day? A “no” stops the order; a “maybe” asks a person to say yes or no.
  3. Approval and routing. A senior person approves if the size needs it. The order goes to the trading desk.
  4. Find the sellers. The trader looks at the axes on the screen. Dealer A shows a sell axe on this very bond. The trader’s habits say: collect at least three prices, and Dealer C is on the banned list for this sleeve, so skip it.
  5. Ask. The trader sends one question to A, B and D for 10 million. They come back like this (the numbers are made up for the example):
    SellerOfferSize good forReply time
    A (has the axe)102.3010 million6 seconds
    B102.385 million11 seconds
    D102.4210 million9 seconds
  6. Choose. D is above the limit, so out. A is lowest for the full size. B is close for half. The trader picks A and writes why: “best price, full size, axed.” That is 10 million at 102.30, which is 10,230,000 of market value, plus the interest that has built up since the last coupon (chapter 1’s “dirty price”).
  7. Record. The deal is entered with its time, price, seller, screen and reason, and the whole price trail is saved. The position grows on the trade-date list immediately.
  8. Split. If the order was for one account, the whole amount lands there. If several accounts shared it, each gets its share at the same price.
  9. Confirm and agree. The dealer’s record arrives; the buyer checks it against its own and says yes, ideally the same day.
  10. Settle. Next business day, the bond moves in and the cash moves out, together or not at all. The position now shows on the settlement-date list too, and the cash list drops by the amount paid.

Notice the manager saved 0.08 of a point against the limit, or about 8,000 on 10 million, only because of the steps in the middle: knowing whom to ask, asking enough, and keeping the list short enough to keep the plan quiet.

Three easy mistakes

Treating a window price as a real price. Asking the whole dock and paying for the gossip. Trusting an old axe as if it were fresh. All three are fixed by the same habit: ask, with a short and well-picked list, and write down what came back.

A wish becomes a bought thing through checks, a careful ask, and a good record, and the desk that knows which seller wants to sell today, and keeps notes on who keeps their word, buys better with the same money.

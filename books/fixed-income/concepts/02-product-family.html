The Big Family of Loan Papers

The real name: “The Fixed-Income Product Family”
The problem

Last chapter you learned that a bond is a row of dates with money on them. Now you walk into the room and see a hundred kinds of paper. They all look like rows of dates. A new person grabs one that looks nice, and only later learns it hurts in a way they never thought about. Lots of people find this part maddening, and that is fair.

The fix: ask what can hurt you

Do not learn the papers one by one. For each paper, ask one thing: what is the main way this one can hurt me? Once you know that, the family falls into a few small groups. Every paper below is told that way.

Papers from a government

A country that lends in its own money is the safest one to lend to, so its papers are the base that everything else is measured from. In the United States the Treasury sells three kinds, and the main difference is how long they run:

  • Short ones (“bills”): run four weeks up to one year. They pay no coupon. You buy them below face and get the face back, and the gap is your pay.
  • Middle ones (“notes”): run two to ten years, with a coupon every six months.
  • Long ones (“bonds”): run twenty to thirty years, same coupon plan.
  • Price-follower ones (“inflation-linked”): the face grows when the cost of things goes up, so the payments keep their power.

Each time the government sells a new paper of a given length, that newest one is called the “on-the-run” one. It is the one everybody trades and the one everybody quotes. Older ones of the same kind are “off-the-run.” They are almost the same paper, but fewer people trade them, so they are harder to sell. The newest ones make up about two-thirds of what is traded, so they act as the yardstick (“benchmark”) for each length: the 2, 5, 10 and 30 year marks are the ones people talk about.

Main way it hurts: rates. There is almost no fear of non-payment, so the only big worry is that market rates move.

Papers from a company

A company is less safe than a government, so it pays more. A rating firm gives each company a grade. Strong grades are “investment grade.” Weaker grades are “high yield,” which is just a polite name for “pays more because it may fail.”

The same company can write papers that rank differently if it goes broke. This matters only on the bad day, but on that day it matters a lot:

Company papers also come with twists written into them:

  • The company may end it early (“callable”). It does this when rates fall and it can borrow cheaper. You lose the nice coupon just when it is worth most.
  • You may end it early (“putable”). Rare, and it helps you.
  • The coupon moves (“floating-rate note”): it is set again every few months at a market rate plus a fixed gap. It is hurt little by rate moves, but still carries the company’s credit worry.
  • You may swap it for the company’s shares (“convertible”): you take a smaller coupon for a chance to share in good news.
  • Promises the company makes about how it behaves (“covenants”), such as not piling on too much other debt.

Main way it hurts: credit (will it pay, and what do buyers ask for the worry), plus rates, plus the twists.

Papers from towns and states

These come in two flavors (“municipal bonds”). One is backed by the full promise and taxing power of the town (“general obligation”). The other is backed only by the money one project brings in, like tolls from a road or fees from a water plant (“revenue bonds”). If the project has a bad year, the second kind has nothing else to lean on.

The big draw: for most of them the interest is free of the national income tax. So towns can pay less, and a person in a high tax group can keep more. Many can also be ended early by the town after some years, often around ten.

Main way it hurts: local credit, tax rule changes, early ending, and being hard to sell.

Papers from a pile of house loans

Many home loans are put in one big pool. The pool’s payments are passed on to people who hold slices (“mortgage-backed securities,” or MBS). In the most common kind, a government-backed group promises that you will be paid even if homeowners fail, so you do not worry much about credit.

Picture it

Every homeowner in the pool holds a free right: they may pay back the loan whenever they want. They do it most when rates fall, because they can borrow cheaper and move. So when rates fall, money comes back to you early, right when you would hate to get it, since new papers pay less. When rates rise, nobody moves house or changes loans, the money comes back slowly, and you are stuck holding a low coupon for longer. Early return is called “contraction.” Slow return is called “extension.”

Because of this, the paper’s price climbs less than a plain bond’s when rates fall, and can drop more when rates rise. This lopsided shape is called “negative convexity.” Note what it does not mean: the paper still gains when rates fall. It just gains less than it should. The loss is in the upside.

Main way it hurts: early or slow payback, which moves the length of the paper while you hold it.

Papers from other piles of loans

The same pooling trick works for car loans, card balances and student loans (“asset-backed securities,” or ABS). Here the pool is cut into slices (“tranches”) that sit in a line for the money. The money coming in fills the top bucket first, then the next, and so on (the “waterfall”). When borrowers fail, the loss hits the bottom bucket first.

Main way it hurts: how many borrowers fail, and which bucket you sit in. The top bucket can be very safe; the bottom one is like a bet.

Short loans that use a paper as a pledge

Sometimes you need cash for a night. You sell a bond to a lender and promise to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The gap is the interest. This is called a “repo” (the lender’s side is a “reverse repo”). It is really a loan with a pledge.

The lender usually hands over a bit less cash than the bond is worth, so there is a cushion. If the bond is worth $100 and you get $98 in cash, the cushion is called a “haircut” of 2 percent. The loan can run for one night (“overnight”), a set stretch (“term”), or with no end date (“open”).

The twist that confuses everyone

Usually the lender takes any safe paper as a pledge (“general collateral”). But sometimes lots of people want to borrow one particular bond, perhaps to sell it before they own it. That bond is then “on special.” Its repo rate drops below the usual rate. A lower rate means the bond is wanted more: the owner can borrow cash very cheaply by lending out the thing everybody wants.

Main way it hurts: the lender may pull away, ask for a bigger cushion, or the pledge may lose value. Funding gets its own chapter later.

Deals about rates, not papers

You can bet on, or protect against, rate moves without buying any bond at all.

  • Trade of payment styles (“interest-rate swap”): two sides agree on an amount that never actually moves. One side pays a fixed rate on it. The other pays a rate that changes. Today new dollar deals use the rate on overnight loans backed by government paper, called “SOFR.” It took over from an older rate (“LIBOR”), which ended for dollars in mid-2023.
  • A right to enter such a deal later (“swaption”).
  • A promise to trade a government paper at a set price on a set day (“Treasury future”). Many different papers are allowed for delivery, and the seller picks. They pick the one that costs them least, called the “cheapest to deliver,” and that shapes how the future prices. Options on these futures exist too.

Main way they hurt: rates move against you, and the deal may need cash pledges as prices change.

Insurance against a company failing

A “credit default swap” (CDS) is a deal that works like insurance on a company’s paper. It has two sides of money:

  • The paying leg: the one who wants cover pays a small fixed fee again and again.
  • The cover leg: if the company fails (for example goes broke or misses a payment), the one who sold the cover pays the face minus what can be saved (the “recovery”).

At a fair price, the worth of the fees and the worth of the cover are equal. Sometimes the price of cover in the CDS market and the extra pay on the company’s bond do not match. The gap between them is called the “CDS-bond basis,” and it can last because of cost of funding, how easy it is to trade each, and fine points in the contracts.

Main way it hurts: the company fails (a sudden big loss for the seller of cover) or the one who owes you the cover cannot pay.

Learn each paper by the one way it hurts, and a crowded room of strange names turns into a small set of fears you can count, size and plan for.

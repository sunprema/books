Who You May Trade With, and How Much

The real name: “Counterparties, Credit, and Limits”
The problem

You run a lending corner at a big fair. A stranger walks up and says, “Lend me a thousand coins today, I will pay you back Friday.” You say yes. Another stranger asks the same. By noon you have lent to forty people, and half of them are cousins of each other. On Friday, one cousin does not show up, and then the other cousins do not either. You have no list, no cap, and no way to say who was safe to lend to. Everyone at the fair feels the sting.

Chapter 4 showed how a wish becomes a deal with a seller. But before the wish can even start, someone has to have decided who the seller may be and how much may be done with them. This chapter is about those rules. They are not exciting, and they are the reason a firm is still standing after a bad week.

The people on the other side

The other side of any deal is called a “counterparty”: the one you made a promise to, and the one who made a promise to you. In this market they come in many kinds:

WhoThe picture
Dealers and big banksThe firms that stand ready to buy from you or sell to you
Money managers, hedge fundsOthers who invest, and who may also be your buyers and sellers
Pension funds, insurersLong-term savers with a lot of money and long promises
Central meeting house (“CCP”)A firm that stands in the middle of a deal so each side faces it, not each other
Safe-keepers (“custodians”)Hold the papers and the cash for their owners
Three-way helpers (“tri-party agents”)Hold and swap the pledged items in a borrowing deal for both sides
Members of the meeting houseThe firms that are allowed to deal with it directly, and who pass others’ deals through

“Which one, exactly?”

A big name on a sign is not one person. Behind it is a chain: a parent firm, legal entities under it, branches in other countries, funds, and the accounts of those funds. And one more: the place where the money and papers actually change hands, which may be a different entity again.

Why does this matter? Because a promise is made by one exact legal entity. The parent may not stand behind a smaller company that carries its name. So the desk must write down the exact entity on the deal. Mixing up the sign and the entity is a classic way to learn this lesson the hard way.

Before the first deal: the paperwork

Nobody trades on a handshake. Before a new counterparty is switched on, the firm checks who they are and where their money comes from (“KYC” and “AML” checks), collects tax forms, and signs agreements:

  • The master deal contract for rate and credit swaps (the “ISDA Master Agreement”), with a schedule of special terms and a short confirmation for each deal. Its big gift is netting: if one side fails, all deals between the two are added up into one number, instead of each being fought over alone.
  • A pledge sheet that goes with it (the “Credit Support Annex”, or “CSA”). It turns “put up something safe” into numbers: what counts as safe, how much it is marked down, how much risk may run with nothing pledged, the smallest amount worth moving, and how often to ask.
  • The master contract for borrowing against papers (the “GMRA”), the borrowing-deal cousin of the first one.
  • Agreements with the meeting house and the safe-keeper, and the standing instructions that say where cash and papers must be sent.

If any piece is missing, the system should say “no deal” before a trader can even start.

The ways a promise can break

Back at the fair, there is more than one way to lose a coin. The desk keeps these apart:

  • Before-the-day risk (“pre-settlement”): the other side fails while a deal is still open, and you must replace it at today’s price. The loss is the “replacement cost”, and it only exists if the deal has moved in your favour.
  • Day-of risk (“settlement”): you hand over your side, and theirs never arrives.
  • Wrong-way risk: your exposure grows at exactly the moment they get weak. Buying cover against a bank from a company owned by that bank is the textbook case.
  • All-in-one-basket risk (“concentration”): too much with one name, one sector or one place.
  • Place risk (“country”): a government rule or crisis stops payments across a border.
  • Contract risk (“legal”): the paper does not hold up when it is tested, or a counterparty was never allowed to sign it.
  • Mistake risk (“operational”): wrong entity, wrong account, wrong instruction.

Three tools shrink these: netting (add up deals), pledges (hold their safe things against what they owe), and the meeting house (face one strong middle instead of many strangers).

The big zoo of caps

A “limit” is a number that says: this much and no more. There is a limit for almost every way to be hurt:

GroupWhat is capped
WhoExposure to one counterparty; to one issuer of paper; to one sector, country or rating band
How easy to leaveLiquidity and concentration: how much of one paper you hold compared with how much trades
Rate betsDuration, DV01, and key-rate limits (chapter 7 explains these)
Credit betsSpread-risk and CS01 limits
Bad dayStress-loss: the most we may lose if a made-up awful day happens
PeopleTrader authority, desk, and portfolio limits: how much each person or group may do alone

Lots of people find this long list maddening, and that is fair. But each cap answers a different question, and one number cannot answer them all. A firm can be fine on rate bets and badly lopsided on one issuer at the same time.

The check that runs on every deal

Here is the path a deal takes, from a wish to a watched position:

  1. Proposal. The manager’s idea: which paper, how much, who with.
  2. Limit check. The system works out what the deal would do to every cap it touches, as if it were already done.
  3. Allowed? Are the papers and the counterparty on the right lists? Are the contracts in place? Is it within the portfolio’s own rules?
  4. Pass, warn, or block. Green goes ahead. Amber needs a person to say yes. Red stops.
  5. Execute (chapter 4).
  6. Update. The new deal immediately counts against every cap, so the next check sees it.
  7. Evening watch. Prices move overnight, so exposure moves too. Every limit is looked at again with fresh prices.

The last step matters most and is forgotten most. A deal that was fine at lunch can break a cap by dinner without anyone touching it.

Who owns the cap, and what happens at the line

There are two kinds of line. A hard limit blocks: the system will not let the deal through. A soft limit warns: the deal may go ahead only if the right person says so. Crossing a soft line is an override, and it needs a name, a reason, and a record. When a cap is close, a message goes to the person above, and higher still if it is crossed.

  • Every limit has an owner, a real person who answers for it.
  • Every limit has a review date: is it still the right size?
  • Every override, change and breach goes into a record nobody can quietly edit.
A cap nobody enforces is a stage prop

If overrides are easy, unrecorded, or always approved, the cap is for show. The test is simple: can you point to the last time a deal was stopped, and say who stopped it?

A small example

A manager wants to buy 10 million of a bond from dealer A. The limit check finds three things (all numbers invented):

CapSizeUsed nowAfter dealResult
This company’s paper50 million35 million45 millionPass
Exposure to dealer A20 million12 million12 millionPass (a plain buy, settled next day, adds little)
This sector (soft)25%23%26%Warn: needs sign-off

The deal can still go ahead, but a named person must approve the sector overrun and write why. That note goes in the record, and tomorrow’s review will see it.

Knowing exactly who you face, writing caps for every way you could be hurt, and checking every deal against them before and after, lets a firm take real risks without one bad friend bringing it down.

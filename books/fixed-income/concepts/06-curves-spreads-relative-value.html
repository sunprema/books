Lines and Gaps That Tell You What Is Cheap

The real name: “Curves, Spreads, and Relative Value”
The problem

At the fair, three stalls sell loans of different lengths. One is paid back in a week, one in a year, one in thirty years. A friend says, “Stall Nine is paying a lot for its loan.” A lot compared with what? With the stall next to it? With last year? With a stall that is much safer? Without a way to line them up, every price is a lonely number, and a lonely number tells you nothing.

This chapter is the way of lining prices up. Chapters 1 to 5 told you what you hold and what you may do. Now: of all the things I could buy, which one is a good deal? The answer is always a comparison. Two tools do almost all the work: a line that shows the price of waiting, and a gap that shows the price of extra worry.

The line: the price of waiting

Put the safest borrower in the country (the government) on a drawing. Along the bottom, how long until you are paid back. Up the side, the yearly rate you are paid for waiting that long. Join the dots. That bendy line is called a “yield curve”. Usually it climbs: wait longer, get more. Sometimes it sags or bends the other way, and people pay close attention when it does.

There are a few kinds of line, and they answer different questions:

LineThe picture
Spot (“zero”) lineThe rate for one single payment at one date, with nothing paid in between. The cleanest line, because nothing is mixed in.
Par lineThe rate on a plain loan that pays regular coupons and sells at face value. This is what you see quoted.
Forward lineThe rate the market is saying, today, for borrowing starting later: “one year from now, for one year.” It is worked out from the spot line, with no guessing needed.
Discount factorsThe same line as a price tag: what one coin paid at a future date is worth today. Say 0.80 for a coin due in five years.

Different borrowers give different lines: the government’s, the swap line (the rate banks swap fixed for floating), the overnight-rate line, and a line for each company’s promises. Each one is a ruler. You pick the ruler that fits the thing you are measuring.

How a line gets made

Nobody can buy a pure “five-year single payment” for every date, so the line is built from real things that trade. This is called “bootstrapping”: start with the shortest one and fix its rate. Use that to price the next one, which leans on the first. Then the next, leaning on both. Each step reuses what is already pinned down, like laying bricks from the ground up.

That leaves gaps between the real dates, so the builder joins them with a smooth rule (“interpolation”). Lots of people find this part unsettling, and that is fair: two firms can draw slightly different lines through the same dots, and so give slightly different answers. The line is a made thing, not a found thing.

Betting on the shape of the line

Once you have a line, you can bet on how it will move. The bets have names:

  • Whole-line bet. You think all rates will rise or fall together. Own more or less “wait-sensitivity” (chapter 7).
  • Steepener. You think the gap between short and long rates will grow. You buy the short end and sell the long end, sized so a parallel move cancels out.
  • Flattener. The opposite: you think the gap will shrink.
  • Butterfly. You bet on the bend: the middle against the two ends. Buy the middle and sell both sides, or the reverse.
  • Barbell vs bullet. The same overall sensitivity held two ways: half short and half long (a barbell), or all in the middle (a bullet). They behave alike for a small move and differently for a bend.
  • Roll-down. If the line slopes up and does not move, a bond slides down it as time passes and its price rises. Some desks hold a bond for that slide.

All of these are watched with “key-rate” numbers: how much you gain or lose if just the two-year point, or just the ten-year point, moves a hair. That way two bets that look different on paper can be seen to be the same bet.

The gap: the price of extra worry

Now take a company’s loan paper and set it next to the government line. The company pays more. That extra is the “spread”: what you are paid to put up with the chance of not being paid back, and the chance you cannot sell quickly. But “the gap to what, exactly?” has several answers, and each one is a ruler with its own habits:

GapHow it is measuredPlain reading
G-spreadBond’s yield minus the government yield at the same length (joined smoothly)Quick, one point on one line
I-spreadThe same, but against the swap lineSame idea, bank ruler instead of government ruler
Asset-swap spreadBundle the bond with a swap; the extra over floating pay you getThe worry-price with the rate bet removed
Z-spreadOne steady amount added to every point of the spot line so the bond’s payments add up to its priceUses the whole line, not one point
OASThe Z-spread after taking out the value of any “early payback” rightThe worry-price once the trick clauses are cleaned out

The last one needs a picture. A company can sometimes pay you back early, whenever that suits it. That right is worth something to the company and costs you. So the gap you are shown looks fatter than the gap you really earn. With invented numbers: Z-spread 120, minus 30 for the early-payback right, leaves an “option-adjusted spread” of 90. For a bond you can hand back early, it goes the other way: the OAS is higher than the Z-spread.

Cheap and rich

Here come two words newcomers reliably flip. Rich means dear: the price is high, so the yield or spread is low compared with similar things. Cheap means the opposite: low price, high yield or spread. Say it with the fair: a rich apple costs more than its twins.

That is the whole trick of “relative value”: not “is this good?” but “is this good next to its neighbours?” Desks look at it from several sides: a screen that lists every bond with its gap and how far it is from a fitted line; peers (same sector, same grade, same length); history (is today’s gap in the top tenth of all days in the past five years?); and cost to trade (a wide gap on something nobody can sell is not a bargain).

Reading the desk’s sentences

Research notes are written in shorthand. Here are two, undone:

The sentenceIn plain words
“The 10-year point looks rich relative to the 7s and 30s.”Draw the line between the seven-year and thirty-year points. The ten-year sits below it: its rate is lower than its neighbours imply, so its price is high. The bet: sell the middle, buy the ends (or at least do not buy the middle), and wait for the dip in the line to fill.
“This MBS sector offers attractive OAS but carries negative convexity.”Home-loan bundles pay a fat gap even after the early-payback right is taken out. But when rates fall, homeowners pay back early and you get your money back just when it is worth least; when rates rise, they stay put and you wait longer. Your gains are capped on the way up and your losses run on the way down. The gap is the pay for that.

A small worked case. A company bond yields 5.30. The government yield at the same length is 4.10. The G-spread is 120 hundredths of a point (“basis points”). The bond’s sector usually pays 95 on average, and today’s 120 is higher than 90% of the past five years’ days. Looks cheap. Then you check: it is thinly traded, and the cost to sell is about 15. So the gap you can rely on is nearer 105. Still cheap, but less so, and now you know how much.

When two prices of the same thing disagree

Sometimes you can get nearly the same exposure two ways, and the two prices differ. That difference is a “basis”. Some you will meet:

  • Cash vs futures: own the government paper, or own the promise-to-deliver contract on it.
  • Bond vs insurance: own the company bond, or sell protection on the same company (the “credit swap”, chapter 2).
  • Swap vs government: the “swap spread”, the gap between the bank line and the government line at the same length.
  • Funding gap: the cost of borrowing cash one way versus another.
  • Across money types: swapping dollars for another money for a time.

You would think such gaps would shut at once, since any clever person could buy the cheap one and sell the dear one. They do not, for reasons you can name:

  • Borrowing costs. The trade needs cash, and cash costs money (chapter 8).
  • Different pledges. The two sides may need different things put up as collateral.
  • Selling cost. One side is easier to sell than the other.
  • Choices hidden in the contract. The seller of a futures contract may choose which paper to hand over, and that choice has a price.
  • Room on the dealer’s books. The big dealers who would close the gap have limited space, and it fills up when times are hard.
  • Too many buyers or sellers. A flood on one side can push the price off for a long time.
“It has to come back” is a hope, not a rule

A wide gap can be pay for something real: a thin market, a clause that hurts you, or a company that is sinking before the screens notice. And a basis that “must close” can grow for months first, and a trade that needs borrowed cash may be forced out before it does. Cheap on a screen is where the questions start.

With a line to measure waiting and a gap to measure worry, every loan paper can be set next to its neighbours, so a desk can say what is dear, what is cheap, and why, and not just what it costs.

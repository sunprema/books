Can You Sell It, and What Is It Worth Today?

The real name: “Liquidity, Market Data, Research, and Models”
The problem

It is the end of the month. A report says your pile of loan papers is worth a hundred million. Then you try to sell one slice of it, and the best offer is a lot lower. Which number was true? Both. One was a guess about a price. The other was a price somebody would really pay.

A desk has two jobs that sound the same and are not: decide what it thinks, and know what it can get. This chapter is about the stuff used to do both: how easy a paper is to sell, the facts a desk collects, the people who write views, and the models that fill the gaps where there are no facts.

Liquidity: how easy is it to get out?

A paper is “liquid” if you can turn it into cash fast, in size, without moving the price against yourself. That is four small questions at once. (Domain knowledge, not from a cited source.)

  • Gap between buy and sell price (the bid-ask spread): how much you lose just by going in and straight back out.
  • Size: how much can you trade at that price? A tight gap for a small ticket says nothing about ten million.
  • Depth and speed: how much sits behind the first quote, and how fast you can finish (“immediacy”).
  • Bounce-back (“resiliency”): after a big trade, how quickly does the price come home?

Behind all four sits one human fact: will a dealer put its own money on the line to take your paper? Most loan papers do not trade on a screen with a crowd of buyers. They trade because someone at a bank agrees to hold them for a while (chapter 4). Every cost of trading, the gap, the push on the price, the leak of your plan, is the price of that favor.

The paper everyone knows, and the paper nobody does

The newest government paper of each length is the “on-the-run” one. Everyone quotes it, so it trades in huge size with a tiny gap. Older ones of nearly the same length are “off-the-run”; they sit in drawers and trade less. A small company bond that came out two years ago may trade a few times a month. Same family, very different exit door.

Calm-day gaps lie

In a scare, everyone wants out at once and dealers want to hold less, not more. The gap widens, the size shrinks, and the “easy” paper and the “hard” paper stop acting alike. A quote from a quiet Tuesday is a poor guide to the exit cost on a bad Thursday, which is why chapter 8 showed forced sellers causing the very drop that hurts them.

Market data: four boxes of facts

Desks keep their facts in four families. Knowing which box a number came from tells you how far to trust it.

  • Reference data is the paper’s ID card: its code, coupon, end date, how days are counted, how often it pays, when it can be called, its rating, currency and sector. It is dull and it is the skeleton: every other system keys off it, and a wrong coupon quietly corrupts price, risk and settlement downstream.
  • Pricing data is what the paper trades or is quoted at: dealer quotes, real trades, blended “composite” prices, closing prices, and the yields and spreads worked out from them.
  • Market-risk data is the set of lines and guesses a model needs: the curves from chapter 6, the “wobble” levels that price options, guesses of how fast home-owners repay (chapter 2), how likely a company is to default, and how much would come back if it did.
  • Event data is what happens next: coupons and principal due, early calls and buy-back offers, rating changes, defaults, big news releases and central-bank decisions. An event can change what you hold overnight.

Facts can be wrong

Data arrives dirty, and a desk spends real effort cleaning it. The usual troubles:

  • Stale prices: the last price is from last week because nothing traded.
  • Bad ticks: a typo or a glitch, such as a price of 10.2 for a paper that is worth 102.
  • Holes: new papers and rare ones have little or no history.
  • Disagreement: two vendors give two prices for the same paper at the same moment.
  • Bad clocks: a price with the wrong time stamp is a price for a different world.
  • No trail: nobody can say where a number came from (“provenance”), so nobody can fix it.
  • Survivor bias: a history that only keeps papers still alive leaves out the ones that failed, and makes the past look kinder than it was.
A calm number can be a stale number

If a bond rarely trades, its “price” barely moves, and the report shows a smooth, quiet line. The portfolio looks safe precisely because nothing in it can be sold. That is the reverse of safe.

Six prices, six different animals

The same paper can show six “prices” on one afternoon. They are not the same kind of thing, and a good report labels which one it is showing.

Kind of priceWhat it really isTrust it forWatch out
ExecutedA real trade: a set size, at a set moment.Proof of what happened.It may be old, small, or between two friends at an odd price.
Dealer quoteWhat one dealer says it would pay or charge.A starting guide.May be “indicative,” a hint with no promise behind it.
ExecutableA quote the dealer must honor, for a stated size, for a short time.What you can actually do right now.Good only for its size and for seconds.
CompositeA blend of many sources’ quotes.A sense of the middle of the market.Can hide that every source is stale, or that none would trade.
EvaluatedA price service’s estimate, built from trades, quotes and models, then ranked and combined.Valuing a large pile, daily, for books and reports.An estimate, not a bid. Services differ by asset type.
ModelA price you calculate from curves and assumptions, with no market voice at all.Papers with no market, and checking other prices.It is only as good as its guesses, and it hides them in a neat number.

Facts and guesses form a ladder. The rule books for company accounts sort values into three rungs. Level 1 is a quoted price in an active market for the identical thing. Level 2 uses things you can see: quotes for similar papers, benchmark curves. Level 3 leans on inputs you cannot see and must guess. Most loan papers priced by an evaluated service land in the middle rung. The higher the number, the more of the value is opinion.

Price services (the big data firms) rank what they can see and fill the rest with a model; their strengths differ by type of paper, so good desks compare more than one. The rule of thumb to carry: say which price, say for what size, say as of when.

Research: where ideas come from

A view usually starts as someone’s written opinion. Desks read several kinds, and each answers a different question. (Domain knowledge, not from a cited source.)

KindThe question it asks
MacroWhere are growth, prices and jobs going?
Rates strategyWhere will central banks and the curve go?
Credit and sectorWhich companies and industries will stay healthy and pay?
Fundamental issuerIs this one borrower’s story sound?
SecuritizedHow will the pooled loans behave?
Relative valueWhich of two similar papers is cheap? (chapter 6)
QuantWhat does the data say, once it is cleaned and tested?

The road from idea to order runs the same way each time: idea → check against data → size it against risk and limits → order → execution (chapters 4 and 5). Each step asks the one before it to prove itself. An idea that cannot name the price it wants, the risk it takes and the exit it expects is a hope, not a trade.

Models: filling the gaps

Where there are no facts, you calculate. A model takes inputs and returns a number, and a desk has a whole shelf of them:

  • Cash-flow model: lays out every payment the paper promises or is expected to pay (chapter 1).
  • Discounting and curve building: shrinks future payments to today’s money using the curves of chapter 6.
  • Spread models (credit spread, Z-spread, OAS): find the extra yield above the curve that matches the price. OAS also strips out the value of options inside the paper.
  • Prepayment model: guesses how fast home-owners pay early, which changes when the cash comes.
  • Default and recovery models: guess how likely a borrower is to fail and how much comes back.
  • Liquidity-cost and transaction-cost models: guess what it would cost to get out at size, so a paper’s “fair” price is not mistaken for the price you will actually get.

Every one of those has assumptions inside: how fast, how likely, how much. An assumption is a position. If your prepayment guess is wrong, you are exposed to it exactly as you are exposed to rates, only nobody wrote it on the risk report.

Keeping models honest

The bank rule book for this (the US supervisors’ guidance known as SR 11-7) calls the danger model risk: harm from decisions based on a model that is wrong, or used wrongly. It asks for three checks: is the idea behind the model sound; is it still behaving as time goes on; and do its outputs match what really happened. It also asks for a list of every model, with owners, and a trail anyone can follow.

  1. Write the assumptions down and who chose them.
  2. Calibrate to market facts, and say to which ones and on which day.
  3. Validate with someone who did not build it.
  4. Backtest: run it on the past and see if it would have been right.
  5. Benchmark: compare against another model or a vendor.
  6. Version it, so you can tell which model gave yesterday’s number.
  7. Log every override: who changed the number, to what, and why.
  8. Be able to explain a number to a person who does not trust it.

This is not paperwork for its own sake. When a desk tells the risk team that a price is “just the model’s,” the risk team’s next question is “which version, using whose guess, checked by whom?” If there is no answer, the price is a rumor in a suit.

A short example: one paper, six answers

Invented numbers. A ten-year company bond that rarely trades, asked about at the close:

SourceSaysWhat it tells you
Last executed trade, 6 days ago, $1m98.80A fact, but old and small
Dealer A, indicative98.50 / 99.30A hint, no promise
Dealer B, executable for $2m98.20 / 99.10Real, but only for $2m, for seconds
Composite98.75The middle, with a stale ingredient
Evaluated price98.65The service’s best estimate
Our model (spread plus curve)99.05Only as good as the spread we assumed

The month-end book may rightly use 98.65. But to sell $10 million, the useful number is nearer 98.20 or lower, and the model’s 99.05 is the one you should trust least. A desk that reports 99.05 as “the price” has promised a profit it has no way of collecting.

The lesson of this chapter

A price is a claim with a source, a size and a time on it. Facts and guesses can both be useful, but they must never be allowed to look alike on the page. And a thing that is easy to value is not the same as a thing that is easy to sell.

What to ask before the trade

  • Which kind of price am I looking at, for what size and as of when?
  • Where did the facts come from, and do two sources agree?
  • How much of the value is a model’s guess, and what happens if the guess is wrong?
  • What would it cost to get out at my size, on a good day and on a bad one?
  • Who validated the model, and which version made this number?

Knowing which numbers are facts, which are estimates, and which are guesses is what lets a desk trust its own screen.

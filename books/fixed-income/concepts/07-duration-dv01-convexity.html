How Much You Lose When the Wind Changes

The real name: “Duration, DV01, Convexity, Carry, and Roll”
The problem

You own a boat, and a lot of people have put their money in it. Every morning the boss asks one thing: “If the wind changes today, how much do we lose?” You cannot say “some.” You have to say a number, in money, before the wind moves. A boat with no number is a boat nobody trusts.

In this book, the wind is the going rate for lending money. When it rises, the loan papers you hold drop in price; when it falls, they rise (chapter 1). This chapter is the set of numbers that turn “the wind moved” into “we are down this many dollars.” Then comes the harder part: why the number is only a guess, and what else is quietly moving the total.

First number: how long the boat leans

A loan paper pays you small amounts along the way and a big amount at the end. The farther away your money is, the more a change in the wind hurts, because you are stuck at the old rate for longer. “Duration” is one number for how far away your money is, on average, and it doubles as the lean of the boat: how much the price drops for each step up in the wind.

There are three kinds, and they answer slightly different questions:

  • Macaulay duration: the average wait for your money, in years, with each payment counted by how big it is. A loan paper that pays nothing until the end waits exactly its full length; one with coupons waits less.
  • Modified duration: the Macaulay number adjusted into a lean. A modified duration of 8 means: if rates rise by one full point (100 hundredths of a point, which desks call “basis points”), the price drops about 8 percent. It is a guess in a straight line, good for small moves.
  • Effective duration: measured by really moving the wind up and down a little and re-pricing the paper each time. You need this for paper with “early payback” tricks inside, because those change the money coming in when the wind moves, and the tidy formula cannot see that.

For a whole pile of loan papers, you take the money-weighted average of each lean. A fund also compares its lean with the lean of the list it is judged against (chapter 3). “We are 0.4 years long against the list” means: a little more lean than the list, so we lose a little more than it does when the wind rises.

Second number: the lean in dollars

Duration is a percent. But the boss wants dollars. So multiply: lean times the money value, then divide by 10,000 to get one basis point. The result is the “DV01”: the dollars gained or lost when the wind moves by one basis point (one hundredth of a point). Some places say “PV01.”

With invented numbers: a pile worth 100 million dollars with a lean of 8 has a DV01 of 100,000,000 × 8 ÷ 10,000 = $80,000 for each basis point.

Percent is not dollars

A huge pile of short papers can have more DV01 than a small pile of long ones, even though its duration is much lower. Duration compares loan papers. DV01 adds them up. That is why limits and hedges are written in DV01 (chapter 5).

Because DV01 is in dollars, you can add it across very different things: bonds (long), a swap or a future that bets the other way (short). “Net DV01” is the sum with signs; “gross DV01” ignores signs and says how big the pieces are. A desk with net zero and a huge gross is not safe; it is two big boats tied together by a rope that might snap.

To hedge, you add a thing with the opposite lean. If you hold $80,000 of DV01 and a bond future carries $800 per contract, you sell about 100 of them. Now a small, level move in the wind nets near zero. Futures and swaps are the usual tools because they are cheap to trade and easy to size.

The worked case

Your pile has a DV01 of $80,000. The wind rises by 6 basis points. The first guess is the one the boss wants:

Rates P&L ≈ − DV01 × move = − $80,000 × 6 = − $480,000

That is the first guess, and it is a good one to say out loud at breakfast. But it is not what you will see on the screen tonight. The rest of this chapter is the list of reasons why.

The wind is not the same at every length

So far we pretended the whole line of rates (chapter 6) moves up and down together. Often it does not. Short rates may jump while long rates sit still. So the lean is split by length: how much do we lose if only the two-year point moves, only the five, the ten, the thirty? These are “key-rate” numbers, and each one is a DV01 for one point on the line.

Two piles can have the same total lean and be very different boats. One is long the ten-year; the other is split between two and thirty. If the line twists, one wins and the other loses. A desk that hedges only the total can still lose on a twist if the pieces do not match. So the hedge is sized point by point: sell a little of the two, a little of the ten, as the pile needs.

The straight-line guess is a bit wrong

Price against the wind is not a straight line; it bends. Duration is the straight edge laid on the curve at one spot. For a small move, the edge and the curve are nearly the same. For a big move, they part, and the gap is the bend. That bend is called “convexity.”

With the same invented pile (lean 8, convexity about 70): for a 6-point move, the bend adds only about $1,300, so the guess is nearly right. For a full-point move, the guess says −$8,000,000, but the bend gives back about $350,000. Small now, big when the wind is wild.

Positive convexity is good for you: you win more than the guess when the wind goes your way and lose less when it does not. Nothing so nice is free, so the market charges for it, in a lower rate.

Negative convexity is the opposite, and it comes from a hidden right the borrower holds. A company that can pay you back early will do so when rates fall (so your price cannot rise much), and will not when rates rise (so your price falls all the way). Home-loan bundles are the big case, since home owners pay off and move house when it suits them (chapter 2). The hidden right is a bet you have sold without noticing; you were paid for it in extra rate. Its price depends on how wild the wind is expected to be: when people expect big swings, that right is worth more, and the paper is worth less to you.

A second wind: how worried people are

A company loan paper pays the government rate plus a gap for worry (chapter 6). That gap can move on its own. The numbers for it mirror the ones above:

NumberWhat it says
Spread durationThe lean for the worry-gap instead of the wind: percent price change for one full point of gap.
“CS01”Dollars gained or lost if the worry-gap moves one basis point. The DV01 of worry.

RecoveryThe share you get back after a failure. A guess until it happens.
MigrationThe risk the company is marked down a grade, so the market asks for a bigger gap, without any failure.

In the worked case: a CS01 of $30,000 and a gap that widens by 2 basis points costs another $60,000. It is a separate hit that the wind number never saw.

What you earn for standing still

Even if nothing moves, your boat is paid for being on the water. “Carry” is that pay. It has parts:

  • Coupon carry: the interest that builds up each day on what you hold.
  • Financing carry: if you borrowed to buy, you pay interest on the loan (chapter 8). Pay for the loan, subtract from the coupon. It can be negative.
  • Pull-to-par: a paper bought above face value drifts down to face as the end nears; one bought below drifts up. Both are certain, if the borrower pays. Forgetting this makes coupon pay look like the whole story.

“Roll-down” adds a second pull. If the line of rates slopes upward and does not move, a paper slides to a shorter length as time passes, and a shorter length has a lower rate, so the price rises. The same idea works for the worry-gap, which is “spread roll.” Add carry and roll: that is the pay you expect if the wind and the gap sit still all year.

With our invented pile: about $12,000 a day of carry after funding, and about $4,000 of roll. A nice day’s pay.

Carry is not free money

A paper pays a high carry because something can go wrong: the wind can jump, the gap can blow out, the borrowing can dry up. Carry trades earn small sums, day after day, and then give them back at once. “Picking up coins in front of a steamroller” is the old saying. Carry tells you what you get paid to carry the risk; it does not tell you the risk is gone.

The sum at the end of the day

Everything above fits into one line, which the desk uses to explain every dollar:

Total P&L = rates + spread + carry + roll + convexity + volatility + funding + currency + residual

Each piece is one cause. “Volatility” is the value of the hidden early-payback rights. “Funding” is any change in what you pay to borrow. “Currency” matters if the paper is in another money. “Residual” is whatever the other numbers cannot explain; a small residual means the explanation can be trusted, and a big one means something is wrong with the numbers, the prices, or the story.

Now the hedge. Say you had sold futures to cover half the lean, $40,000 of DV01. They earn about +$240,000 for the 6 points. But the futures follow their own paper, not yours, and that gap (“basis”) can be a bad day: say they only earn $225,000. Then the hedge leaves you with about −$298,000, and the missing $15,000 is hedge slippage, a real cost of the real world. Two more ways the number slips: the 6 points did not move every length equally (key-rate mismatch), and your prices at the close may come from different sources than your hedge’s prices (chapter 9 shows why that matters).

So when someone says, “We were down $480,000 on rates,” the right reply is: “Which lengths moved, what did the worry-gap do, what did we earn for standing there, and how well did the hedge follow?”

These numbers let a desk say, before the wind moves, how much it can cost, and after, which part of the loss was the wind, which was worry, and which was just the hedge missing.

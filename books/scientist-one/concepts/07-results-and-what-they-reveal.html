Chapter seven

What the Numbers Reveal

We've built up the whole apparatus — the failure modes, the Chain-of-Evidence definition, the three-stage pipeline, the CoE Audit's four checks. Now we read the actual results, the way the CoE Audit itself was designed to be read: not as one blended quality score, but check by check, system by system.

The comparison

ScientistOne was evaluated against four baseline autonomous-research- agent systems — Sakana AI-Scientist v2, AutoResearchClaw, DeepScientist, and AI-Researcher — across the ADRS benchmark's five frontier research tasks (75 papers total, spanning all five systems) plus six additional generalization tasks drawn from MLE-Bench and Parameter Golf, the task from chapter 4.

Beyond the audit checks: task performance

The paper doesn't stop at "the papers are honest" — it also asks whether the underlying research is actually good, which is a genuinely separate question from everything in chapter 6. On the ADRS benchmark, ScientistOne matched or exceeded human-expert performance. On the MLE-Bench generalization tasks at High difficulty, it earned Gold Medal results on two tasks — RSNA Brain Tumor and 3D Object Detection — and on 3D Object Detection specifically, the baseline DeepScientist scored 0.0000, a complete failure, on the same task ScientistOne won a gold medal on. On Parameter Golf, as chapter 4 covered, ScientistOne reached a state-of-the-art score of 1.0600 while a baseline failed to even produce a valid submission.

Two different questions, both answered

"Is this paper honest" (the CoE Audit) and "is this research good" (task performance) are independent axes — chapter 1 opened on exactly that independence, in the failure direction. The results here close the loop by showing a system that scores well on both at once: honesty didn't come at the cost of capability.

One honest limit

In keeping with a book about a system built to be checked rather than trusted, it's worth applying the same instinct to the study itself. This is a single paper's own comparison against baseline systems it also had to instrument to run the same CoE Audit — that is not the same evidentiary weight as a fully independent, third-party replication of these exact numbers on these exact systems. The CoE Audit's design (post-hoc, uniform, mechanically checkable — chapter 6) is what makes independent replication possible in principle. Whether it has actually been replicated by someone with no stake in the result is a separate, later question this book doesn't have an answer to yet.

The point was never that ScientistOne is right about everything. The point is that you can check.

Where this leaves us

Trace the whole arc back to chapter 1: baseline systems produce competitive, professional-looking papers that fail independent checks at rates too high to treat as noise — up to 21% hallucinated references, as low as 42% score-verification pass rate, method-code alignment as low as 20%. ScientistOne's answer wasn't a better prompt or a more careful writing style. It was structural — evidence chains built by construction across three pipeline stages (chapters 3 through 5), measured by an audit that doesn't have to trust the system it's checking (chapter 6) — and the measured result is 0 hallucinated references out of 337, 12 of 12 papers with reproducible scores, and the highest method-code alignment of any system tested, without giving up state-of-the-art results on the underlying research itself.

So what did we actually gain — for the whole book?

A working definition of what it means for an AI-written paper's claims to be checkable, a system built to keep that property by construction rather than by inspection, and an audit methodology that turns "trust us" into four numbers anyone can go verify. The receipts were the point all along.

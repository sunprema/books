Chapter two

Chain of Evidence

Once you've accepted that "reads well" and "is true" are different properties, the natural next move is to ask for a definition of the second one that a machine can actually check. That's what Chain-of-Evidence (CoE) is: not a writing style, not a prompting trick, but a structural requirement on what a claim is allowed to point to before it's allowed to exist in a paper.

The definition, stated plainly

The paper's framing is short enough to hold in your head in one piece: every claim a research system produces must be traceable, through a recorded chain of supporting claims and evidence, to a grounding source. Read that twice, because every word is load-bearing.

"every claim"
Not just the headline results. A citation, a number in a table, a sentence describing how an algorithm works — each one is a claim under CoE, and each one owes a trace.
"traceable, through a recorded chain"
The trace isn't a vibe or a footnote added after the fact — it's a recorded path, meaning something a program can walk and check, not something only a human reviewer could reconstruct.
"to a grounding source"
The chain has to bottom out somewhere real: a paper that exists, a program's actual output, a file on disk — not another claim, all the way down.

The distinction worth keeping

CoE doesn't make a system's science better. It makes a system's claims checkable. Those are different, narrower properties — a mediocre idea with a perfect evidence chain is still a mediocre idea, just an honestly-reported one. Keep this distinction in mind for the rest of the book: everything from here on is about checkability, not about cleverness.

Claim types, and what each one must trace to

Not every claim needs the same kind of receipt. CoE recognizes several primary claim types, each with its own required evidence-chain shape — the trace a citation needs looks nothing like the trace a benchmark score needs. Here are three the paper is explicit about:

Citation claim ✓ scholarly-db lookup

"...as shown by Vaswani et al. (2017), self-attention scales..."

the cited work exists in a scholarly database, and its content is consistent with how the citing text describes it

Numerical claim ✓ recorded output

"...our method achieves 0.87 F1 on the held-out set..."

the value as reported traces back to a recorded run output — not a number typed in because it looked plausible

Methodological claim ✓ implementation match

"...we apply gradient clipping at norm 1.0 before each update..."

the method as described resolves to the corresponding code — the clip call, at that threshold, in that place

Notice the pattern: each trace target is something mechanically checkable — a database lookup, a diff against a run log, a diff against source code — rather than something that requires a domain expert's judgment call. That's deliberate. A verifiability framework that needs an expert to adjudicate every claim doesn't scale past the first paper.

"By construction," not "by inspection"

Here's the design choice that separates CoE from "add a fact-checking pass at the end." A system could, in principle, write a paper however it likes and then run a verifier over the finished draft, flagging whatever doesn't check out. That's strictly better than nothing. It's also strictly worse than the alternative the paper actually builds toward: maintaining the evidence chain as the work happens, so that by the time a claim is written down, its trace already exists — because the trace is what the claim was built from, not something bolted on afterward.

Think about what changes between those two approaches. A bolt-on verifier can only report failures — "this citation didn't check out" — after the fact, when the paper is already written and the model has to go back and either fix or remove the claim. A by-construction system never writes the unsupported claim in the first place, because it never had a piece of evidence to build it from. The difference isn't philosophical; it shows up directly in the error rate, which is the subject of chapter 7.

Where this is going

The next chapter follows this "by construction" idea into an actual system. ScientistOne is what it looks like to keep an evidence chain alive across three very different kinds of work — reading papers, running experiments, and writing prose — without ever letting the chain lapse between them.

So what did we actually gain?

A checkable definition of "traceable" — three concrete claim types, each with a mechanical trace target, and a design principle (by-construction over by-inspection) that tells us what to look for when we open up the system that implements it.

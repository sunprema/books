Operating the capstone in production

Three incidents, walked end to end. Each is ordinary, each has happened to somebody this month, and each is diagnosable in minutes with the instrumentation the last twenty-two chapters installed — and in hours without it.

Incident 1 — the ERP goes away

02:40. Alert: ERP connection pool utilization at 100% for five minutes. No customer complaints yet, because the customer's overnight batch is patient.

Triage. Pool saturation with rising latency means the downstream is slow, not that you are overloaded — check arrival rate first. It is normal. So this is the ERP. Its own monitoring shows an unplanned index rebuild; the DBA confirms two more hours.

Decide. Two options. Let the circuit breaker hold open and reject with 503 plus Retry-After — honest, and the customer's system will re-submit. Or park intake: accept orders, publish them to a queue, and drain when the ERP returns. Meridian chose to park, because the customers' retry behaviour is not uniform and some give up after three attempts.

Recover. The ERP returns at 04:55. The queue drains at pool capacity, roughly 22 orders/second against a backlog of about 4,000 — three minutes. The idempotency key means anything that was retried in the meantime is a no-op.

What made this a 20-minute incident

Pool utilization was alerted before anything failed — the leading indicator from chapter 13. Without it, the first signal would have been customer timeouts an hour later, with a queue already too deep to drain inside the batch window.

Incident 2 — the duplicate partner file

06:15. Alert: reconciliation exceptions above threshold — 340 lines acknowledged twice.

Triage. Trading Networks shows two interchanges from partner 004561 with the same control number, four hours apart. The second one's transaction record shows the acknowledgement service ran and returned a logged no-op — the duplicate check held. So no double-acknowledgement reached the ERP; the reconciliation exceptions are the detection, not the damage.

Root cause. The supplier restored from backup and re-sent Thursday's file. Confirmed by phone, not inferred.

Action. Nothing to fix in Meridian. Clear the exceptions with a note, and ask the supplier to notify before a replay. The improvement is a distinction in the alert itself — a duplicate that was correctly rejected should be informational, not the same severity as a genuine quantity mismatch, or the alarm trains people to ignore it.

A control that works produces an alert about something that did not happen. Tune the severity, do not silence the control.

Incident 3 — the expired certificate

03:02. Alert: no file from partner 004561 in its expected window. The absence alarm from chapter 11.

Triage. ActiveTransfer's log shows connection attempts failing at TLS negotiation. The message names a handshake failure, not an expiry — which is why this is worth rehearsing. The certificate inventory shows the partner's client certificate expired at 00:00 UTC.

Why no 30-day warning. The inventory covered Meridian's own certificates. The partner's client certificate — presented to Meridian — was in the truststore but not in the alarm set. That is the finding.

Action. Partner sends the renewed certificate; install, verify, the backlog transfers. Then extend the inventory to every certificate in the truststore, not just the keystore, and add each partner's certificate to their onboarding checklist with an owner on both sides.

Observability-driven root cause

A repeatable order for integration incidents: scope it (one partner, one order type, or everything?), place it on the timeline against deploys and downstream events, follow one correlation ID end to end — which is where a single failing transaction tells you more than any dashboard — and only then form a hypothesis and test it against a second transaction. Confirm before you act; the 2026 postmortem literature is full of confident wrong root causes announced early.

Two platform mechanics carry this. Service audit logging that stores the input pipeline is what makes a failed transaction replayable at all. And resubmission from Monitor is possible only for a top-level service — so the runbook must name which service that is, or the instruction "resubmit the failed order" is not executable at 03:00 by someone who did not build it.

Postmortems that change something

Every incident here ended with exactly one durable change: the pool alarm, the severity split, the truststore inventory. That is the right number. A postmortem producing fourteen action items produces zero, and a postmortem producing none was not a postmortem.

Two habits make them compound. Add the incident's captured pipeline to the fixture set (chapter 16), so the regression suite grows from reality rather than imagination. And ask the absence question every time: what would have told us sooner? In two of these three incidents, the answer was an alarm on something not happening.

Failure scenario — the postmortem itself

After incident 1 the team writes fourteen action items, including "review all connection pools" and "improve monitoring". None has an owner or a date. Six months later the same alert fires, the same investigation runs, and someone finds the old document. The single-item rule is not modesty; it is the only version that gets done.

Design review questions

  1. For your last three incidents: what one change came out of each, and did it land?
  2. Does your runbook name the top-level service to resubmit, by path?
  3. Which certificates are in your truststore but not in your expiry alarm set?
  4. What is your longest undetected period in the last year — the red bar?
  5. Can someone who did not build the system follow your runbook at 03:00?

Observability and troubleshooting

The question at 03:00 is never "is the server up". It is "where is purchase order 4471195". An integration platform that cannot answer that in under two minutes is unsupportable, however healthy its dashboards look.

The logs, and which one to open

webMethods logging is deliberately split by concern rather than pooled into one stream. The types include server, session, error, guaranteed delivery, service, business process, task, document and security logs. Knowing which to open is most of the diagnostic skill.

Which log answers which question
LogAnswers
ErrorWhat threw, and the stack trace — all errors in Integration Server, including exceptions from services
Service (audit)Did this service run, with what inputs, and can I resubmit it
Guaranteed deliveryWhich transactions started, their current status, and whether they completed or failed
DocumentWhat was published and consumed
ServerStartup, package state, resource-level events
SecurityAuthentication and authorization outcomes
SessionWho connected, from where, for how long

Guaranteed delivery has its own view in Integration Server Administrator under Logs > Guaranteed Delivery, where you can see the names of running guaranteed-delivery processes and whether they completed successfully or failed. When an asynchronous path has gone quiet, that page is the first stop, not the error log.

Correlation IDs: accept, never mint

One identifier must survive the whole journey: HTTP request, flow service, ERP call, published event, subscriber, and the acknowledgement file that comes back two days later. The rule is to accept a correlation ID from the caller and only generate one if there is none. A trace that begins at your edge cannot tell you what the customer's system did before it reached you — and that is frequently where the answer is.

Carry it in three places, because each is searched by a different person: the transport header (X-Correlation-Id), the canonical document's source.correlationId, and every log line the flow writes. For B2B, add the business keys too — the interchange control number and the customer PO number — because the supplier will phone you quoting theirs, not yours.

Pipeline capture: powerful, and not free

Service audit logging can store the input pipeline, and storing it is what allows you to resubmit the service later. It is the most valuable diagnostic setting on the platform and the one most likely to cause a second incident, for three reasons: audit volume grows with payload size rather than transaction count, payloads containing personal data land in a store with different retention rules than the source, and secrets left in the pipeline (chapter 3) are captured verbatim.

A capture policy worth copying
  • Top-level services only. Nested services multiply the same payload across every level.
  • On error always; on success by sampling — a small percentage is enough to characterize normal traffic.
  • Drop secrets before the audited step, not after. The pipeline is captured as it stands.
  • Agree retention with whoever owns the data, and make sure it is shorter than the source system's, not longer.

Resubmission has a shape you must design for

A service can be resubmitted from Monitor only if it is a top-level service, not a nested one. This is not an operational footnote — it is a design constraint that reaches back into chapter 6. If your recoverable unit of work is buried three levels down inside an orchestration, it cannot be resubmitted, and your runbook's "resubmit the failed step" is not executable.

So decide, at design time, what the recoverable unit is. For Meridian it is one order. That means submitOrder is invoked as a top-level service with pipeline capture on, and the entire order is replayable — safely, because chapter 12 made it idempotent. Those three properties only work together.

Metrics, traces, and the hybrid view

Logs tell you about one transaction; metrics tell you about the system. The four that matter for an integration tier are throughput, latency percentiles (p50 and p99 — the mean hides everything), error rate by class from chapter 12's taxonomy, and, most usefully, connection pool utilization. Pool saturation is the leading indicator that precedes almost every slow collapse, and it is the one metric integration teams most often fail to collect.

Across a hybrid estate, End-to-End Monitoring provides the cross-runtime view — the gateway, the integration runtime, messaging and B2B on one timeline. That matters because the interesting failures live in the gaps between runtimes, which no single component's dashboard can show.

Alert on symptoms, and on silence

Alert on what the business feels: orders not acknowledged within the SLA, dead-letter depth above zero, pool utilization sustained above 80%, a partner file that has not arrived in its window. Do not alert on CPU. And notice that two of those four are absence alarms — the failure that produces no error is the one that goes unnoticed longest.

Failure scenario

A subscriber silently stops consuming after a bad deploy. No errors, because nothing runs. No alert, because every alarm is on error rate. The queue grows for eleven hours; nobody notices until the CRM is a day behind and sales complain. Queue depth and consumer liveness are absence signals, and absence needs its own alarm — error-rate monitoring is structurally blind to a component that has stopped doing anything at all.

Design review questions

  1. Time yourself: how long to answer "where is PO 4471195" with only production tooling?
  2. Does your correlation ID originate at the caller or at your edge?
  3. Is your recoverable unit of work a top-level service? If not, what is your actual recovery procedure?
  4. Do you collect pool utilization, and does anything alert on it before saturation?
  5. Which of your alerts would fire if a component stopped doing anything at all?
  6. If pipeline capture is on: what personal data is in it, and who agreed the retention?

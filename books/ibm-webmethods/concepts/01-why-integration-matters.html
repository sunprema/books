Why enterprise integration still matters

Start with the simplest system that works: one service calls another over HTTP. Now add nine more systems, four of which you do not control, one of which only speaks EDI, and a compliance officer who wants to know who changed an order and when. That pressure is the whole subject.

The problem that does not go away

Every company above a certain size ends up with the same shape: a system of record that is old and correct, a handful of SaaS applications that are new and opinionated, a data platform, and a set of partners who exchange documents on their own terms. None of these were designed together. Integration is the work of making them behave as one business process anyway.

The naive answer is point-to-point: when the CRM needs order status, the CRM calls the ERP. It works, and for three systems it is the right call. The cost arrives with the fourth. n systems with direct connections trend toward n(n−1)/2 integrations — ten systems is up to 45 — and each one carries its own retry logic, its own credential, its own idea of what an order looks like, and its own outage.

Four shapes, not one

"Integration" is four different problems wearing one word, and the platform gives each its own runtime. Getting this classification right on day one is worth more than any amount of later tuning, because a misfit here is expensive to undo.

The expensive mistakes in integration are almost never algorithmic. They are a request/response API sitting on top of a batch process, discovered in production, at volume.

The system we will build

Meridian Supply sells laboratory instruments. An order arrives from a customer's procurement system, must be priced and validated, must land in the ERP as a sales order, must be visible to sales in the CRM, must trigger fulfilment, and must be reconciled against a supplier acknowledgement that arrives as an EDI 855 the next morning. That is one business process crossing four technical boundaries and one company boundary.

Back of the envelope, so the design has numbers in it: 12,000 orders a day, peaking at roughly 8 orders/second for two hours around month end; average order 14 KB of JSON; the ERP tolerates about 20 concurrent connections and answers in 300–900 ms; the CRM's API allows 100 calls per minute. Those four numbers decide most of what follows. In particular, an ERP that answers in 900 ms at 20 concurrent connections caps you near 22 orders/second — comfortable now, and the first thing to break if the business doubles.

Failure scenario

The CRM's 100-calls-per-minute limit is the one people miss. At 8 orders/second you are generating 480 CRM notifications a minute against a 100-call budget. Notice that this is not a webMethods problem and no amount of platform tuning fixes it — it is a design problem, solved in chapter 10 by making CRM notification an event subscriber that batches, not a step in the order flow.

Where the product stands today

webMethods is now an IBM product. IBM completed the acquisition of webMethods — along with EntireX and ApplinX — and StreamSets from Software AG on 1 July 2024. The current umbrella is IBM webMethods Hybrid Integration, positioned as a unified iPaaS covering applications, APIs, events, data, messaging, B2B/EDI and file transfer under one control plane across hybrid multi-cloud.

Practically, this matters in three ways: documentation now lives under IBM's docs domain, the on-premises components you will actually install still carry their long-standing names, and the branding in any tutorial older than mid-2024 will not match your console. Concepts are stable; labels are not. This book states concepts first and isolates version-specific mechanics where you can see them.

Design review questions

  1. For each existing interface in your estate, which of the four styles is it — and which was it designed as?
  2. What are your four numbers: volume, peak, payload size, and the tightest downstream limit?
  3. Which single downstream system caps your throughput today, and do you know its limit by measurement or by rumour?
  4. If you removed the integration platform, how many teams would have to write a retry policy?

Why the Same Thing Works on One Computer and Not Another

Real name: "The Problem with Software Environments"

The cake that fell

Your friend sends you a cake paper. It says what to put in and how to mix it. She makes the cake in her kitchen and it is great. You make it in yours, with the same paper, and it comes out flat and sad.

Nobody lied. The paper just left things out. Her oven is hotter than yours. Her flour is a different kind. She always has a little salt sitting out, so she never wrote "salt" down. The paper only works if your kitchen is secretly the same as hers.

Computer programs are cakes. The paper is not the whole story.

Every program needs a kitchen: other programs, helper files, the right version of each, and settings. All of that together is called an "environment". This book is about one question: how do we say, in words a machine can follow, what the whole kitchen must hold? Before any new tool, let us see how things go wrong.

Problem one: two cooks, one shelf

Most computers keep helper parts on one shared shelf. Say you make two things. The first one needs the old kind of a helper part. The second one needs the new kind. There is only one spot on the shelf for that part. Put the new one there and the first thing breaks. Put the old one there and the second thing breaks.

People call this "dependency hell", and the name is fair. Lots of people find it maddening, and they should. Nobody did anything wrong. The shelf was just built for one of each.

Problem two: things nobody wrote down

Here is the cake problem again. A set of steps that sets up a program says "get these parts, then run this". But it quietly counts on things that were already there: a tool for putting parts together, a helper file the computer came with, which kind of computer you have, even what language it speaks.

On the person's own computer, all of that is there, so it works. On a fresh computer, it is not, and the steps stop in a strange place with a message that does not say why. These are "hidden assumptions". The worst part is that the author cannot see them. They live in the author's kitchen, like the salt.

The big ideaIf a thing works only because of something nobody wrote down, then it does not really work. It works here.

Writing down what you have is not saying what you need

A common fix is to write a list of what is on your computer right now: every part, with its version. That feels safe. It is a start, but it is a photo of the kitchen, not a plan for one.

A photo shows the result. It does not say which parts you chose and which ones just came along. It does not say how each part was made. It often misses the big, low-down things such as the tools that put the parts together. And it cannot tell a friend what to do on an empty room.

There is a middle step that many teams use: a "lock file". Elixir has one, and so does Rust. It pins the exact version of every part from that language's own parts store. That helps a lot. But it does not cover the language tools themselves, or the helper files that come from the computer, or the settings. The lock file is one shelf. The kitchen has many.

The thing we will build all book long

Words are cheap, so here is something real. All through this book we will grow one small project. It has two pieces:

  • A small web helper written in Elixir, using a tool called Phoenix. It answers people who visit it, and it keeps its notes in a database.
  • A small helper tool written in Rust, which the web helper calls to do a heavy job.

Two languages, two sets of tools, a database, and some secret passwords. It is small, but it has most of the trouble a real project has. Look at what it quietly needs to start:

# the whole setup steps, as people write them today
mix deps.get
mix phx.server
# what these two lines quietly count on:
#   Elixir and Erlang, in versions that fit each other
#   a C tool for putting parts together
#   a file-watching helper
#   a database already running, with a known name
#   a Rust tool, found somewhere on the path

Two lines. Five things nobody wrote. We will deal with every one of them before we are done.

Try it on paper

Two computers look the same. Both have the project files. Both show the same list of parts. On Ana's computer the web helper starts. On Ben's it stops with a long strange message about a missing file.

  1. Write down five things that could be different between the two computers even though the list matches.
  2. Put each one in a group: a part from the language's store, a tool from the computer, a setting, or something that was running.
  3. Which of your five could a lock file catch? Which could it not?
  4. Now write the one sentence you wish the setup steps had said.

What this will not fix

Be careful here. Saying everything the kitchen needs will not make every cake perfect. Data that lives in a database, secrets, and the clock on the wall do not come from a plan. We will keep saying that, loudly, in later chapters, rather than pretend a good plan fixes all of it.

What a good plan can do is make the parts of the kitchen the same everywhere, and make a missing salt show up as a loud error on day one. That tool is Nix, and it begins in the next chapter.

A kitchen that is written down is one you can build again.

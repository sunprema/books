Who Does What: The Many Parts With Nix in Their Name

Real name: "A Map of the Nix Ecosystem"

One name, five jobs

Say you ask a friend, "Do you know Nix?" She says yes. You ask, "Is it hard to put on a computer?" She says no. You ask, "Can I run it without changing my whole computer?" She says, "Well, that depends. Do you mean Nix, or NixOS?" Now you are lost, and so are most new readers.

The trouble is that one short word is used for many different things. Picture a town where the bakery, the baker, the bread and the baker's street are all called "Nix". If someone says "Nix is broken", you cannot tell if the oven is cold or the street is closed.

So this chapter is a map. It does one job: it gives each part its own name and says what that part is in charge of. We will not type anything hard. We only learn who does what.

The five parts

Start with the cake from the last chapter. A cake needs a way of writing the paper, a cook who follows the paper, a big box of papers for common cakes, and, if you like, a whole party built around the cake. Here are the parts, one at a time.

1. The language: how the paper is written

Nix is first a small way of writing things down. People call it a "language". You write what you want in it: a list, a name, a recipe for building a program. It does not do anything on its own. It is only the words on the paper. Later chapters teach you to read it.

2. The package manager: the cook

Something has to read the paper and do the work. That is the tool also called Nix, the one you run in your terminal. It reads the words, builds the things they describe, and puts them away on a shelf of its own. It runs on Linux and on other Unix-like computers, and that includes a Mac. It is the cook, and it can work in any kitchen.

3. Nixpkgs: the big box of papers

Writing every paper yourself would take forever. So people gather their papers in one huge shared box. It is called "Nixpkgs", short for "Nix packages". It holds papers for a very large number of programs, and each one is written in the Nix language. When you ask for a common program, the cook usually goes to this box to find its paper.

4. NixOS: a whole computer built from papers

Here is the step that confuses people. Some folks used the same ideas for more than programs. They wrote the paper for the entire computer: which programs are on, which helpers run, who the users are. That is "NixOS", an operating system. It is built on Nix, and it is written in the same words. Because the paper describes everything, you can keep it in a folder with your history and go back to an older computer if a change goes wrong.

The big ideaYou do not need NixOS to use Nix. The cook works in your old kitchen. NixOS is a different, whole house, and this book keeps it for later chapters.

5. The helpers around the edges

Two more names will keep showing up.

  • Flakes. A "flake" is a folder with a label on the front. It says what it needs (its inputs) and what it gives back (its outputs), and a second file remembers the exact versions it used. It does not cook anything new. It is a tidy way of handing a project to the cook. The people who make Nix still call this an experimental feature, so you must switch it on. We will meet it in its own chapter.
  • Home Manager. This one looks after you, not the machine: your programs, your settings files, your shell. It can run by itself on an ordinary computer, or live inside a NixOS or Mac-with-nix-darwin setup. So you can have a tidy personal setup without changing the computer under it.

Using Nix without moving house

Here is the good news for anyone who likes their computer as it is. The cook and the big box work on your current system. You do not wipe anything. Your old programs stay where they are, and Nix keeps its own things on its own shelf. If you later want Home Manager, it can sit on top of that too. NixOS is the one part that asks you to change the machine itself, and it is always optional.

This is also why the next chapters start with the cook. We get a real result first, with your computer unchanged, and add the other parts only when we need them.

Where our project fits

Remember the Phoenix helper and the Rust tool from chapter one? Each part of the map will get a job there. The language writes the recipes. The cook builds them. The big box gives us ready-made Elixir, Rust and a database tool. A flake labels the project for the team. Home Manager would hold one developer's personal settings. NixOS would be the machine that finally runs it. Nobody needs to know all of that now. Just keep the map in your pocket.

Try it on paper

Below are seven jobs. For each one, write which part is in charge: the language, the package manager, Nixpkgs, Home Manager, or NixOS. If two seem right, write both and say which one does the main part.

  1. Keeping the paper for a popular program that many people share.
  2. Saying that a list holds three names.
  3. Making sure a web server starts when the computer turns on.
  4. Building a program from its paper and putting it on its shelf.
  5. Keeping your shell settings the same on two laptops.
  6. Choosing which users exist on the machine.
  7. Making sure a team all use the same exact versions of what a project asks for.

Hint for the last one: it is a flake's job to pin those versions, and the cook is who follows the pins.

What the map does not tell you

A map is flat, and real towns are not. The edges between parts are blurry. The word "Nix" will still be used loosely by people, so ask "which one?" when it matters. Flakes are common and handy, but the project calls them experimental, so details may shift. And a map says who does the work, not how. The how starts next, with your hands on the keyboard.

When you know which part does the job, you know where to look when it goes wrong.

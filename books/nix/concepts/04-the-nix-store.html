The Big Room Where Every Thing Gets Its Own Shelf

Real name: "The Nix Store: Software Without a Global Installation Directory"

Two friends, one drawer

Picture a shared house with one kitchen drawer for knives. Ana needs a small knife for her recipe. Ben needs the same kind of knife, but a bigger one. There is one slot in the drawer with that name. Whoever came last wins, and the other one cooks with the wrong knife and wonders why dinner is strange.

Most computers keep programs this way. A library of code goes into one well-known place, under one name. Two programs that want different versions fight over it. Updating one can quietly break the other. Chapter one called this the trouble with shared places, and now we look at how Nix gets out of it.

The fix: a shelf for every version

Nix does not keep one drawer. It keeps one huge room, and every thing it makes goes on its own shelf, with a name that no other thing can have. The room is the folder /nix/store. People call it "the store". Look at what a shelf name looks like:

/nix/store/<hash>-hello-2.12.1

Read it from left to right. First comes the room, /nix/store. Then a long jumble of 32 letters and numbers, which I have written as <hash>. Then a dash, then a plain name a person can read. The jumble is made from a description of how the thing was made: the papers, the tools, the settings, everything that went into it. Change any one of them, even a little, and the jumble changes, so the thing lands on a different shelf. The make-up of that jumble is in the manual under "store path"; it uses a custom spelling with the letters e, o, u and t left out.

So Ana's small knife and Ben's big knife have different names, and both sit in the room at once. Nothing fights. Each program is told the exact shelf it wants, and it gets that one.

Shelves never change

There is a second rule, and it is the one that makes the first one safe. Once a thing is on a shelf, nobody edits it. Not you, not another program, not a later update. New version? That is a new thing on a new shelf. The old one stays exactly as it was. People say these things are "immutable", which just means "never changed after it is made".

Why does that help? Think of a library book that no one may write in. Two people can read the same copy at once and always see the same words. If a program worked yesterday and its shelves are the same today, it works today.

One honest note. For ordinary builds, the jumble comes from the description, not from a fingerprint of the finished bytes. So the name tells you "this is what was asked for", and the guarantee that the same description gives the same result comes from the sandboxed builds you will meet in a later chapter.

Things that need other things

A cake needs flour; a program needs other programs and code. When Nix finishes making a thing, it looks through it for the jumbles of the shelves it was made with. If it finds one written inside, say in a spot that says "look for my helper over here", it notes that this thing needs that shelf. This is what Nix means by a "reference". It is plain searching for text, which is why a stray note of a tool path can make a thing need that tool even when you did not mean it to.

Now follow the notes. Take a thing, take everything it points to, then everything those point to, all the way down. That whole bundle is called a "closure". It is exactly what you must carry to move the thing to another machine and have it work. Nothing less, and nothing more.

nix build nixpkgs#hello
readlink result                            # which shelf is the sticky note pointing at?
nix-store --query --references $(readlink -f result)   # the things it points at directly
nix path-info -rsSh nixpkgs#hello          # the whole bundle, with sizes

Line one makes the program. Line two follows the result note from the last chapter to the real shelf. Line three lists the direct needs only. Line four lists the whole closure, one shelf per row, with the size of each (-s) and the size of everything it pulls in (-S), in a form people can read (-h). Your output has different jumbles from mine, so I am not copying any here.

Built is not the same as installed

Here is a quiet but big idea. Building a thing puts it on a shelf. That is all. It does not put it on your program list. Installing means something else: you keep a small page, called a "profile", that names the shelves you want on your list, and your shell looks at that page. Think of the shelves as the whole warehouse and the profile as your own shopping basket.

Each time you change the basket, Nix does not erase the old page. It writes a new numbered page and points at that one. Every old page is called a "generation". Added something and your day went wrong? Point back at last night's page. Nothing was lost, because nothing on a shelf ever changed. Only the page you point at moved.

On common setups the pages live under /nix/var/nix/profiles, and your home folder has a link, ~/.nix-profile, that leads there. Details differ between machines and Nix versions, so look at yours with ls -l ~/.nix-profile.

Who cleans the room?

A room where nothing is ever changed or thrown away fills up. The cleaner is a command, nix-collect-garbage. It does not guess. It starts from a list of things that are surely wanted, called "roots", follows every note from them, and marks all it reaches as in use. Every shelf it never reached is garbage and is removed.

What counts as a root? Every page in every profile, for one. Also, the result note that nix build leaves in your folder: Nix registers it in a special folder under /nix/var/nix/gcroots, so the program it points to stays. Delete result and the program is free to be cleaned up at the next sweep, though not removed at that instant.

nix-collect-garbage        # remove shelves nothing needs
nix-collect-garbage -d     # first throw away old pages, then sweep
The catch

The -d form removes your old numbered pages, so you can no longer go back to them. Use it when you are sure you will not need to. Run as yourself it clears your pages; the machine's own pages need the admin account.

What this costs you

Many versions at once means many shelves, and the room gets big. You pay in disk space and in the habit of sweeping now and then. Programs that assume a fixed place, like /usr/bin/env scripts that look in set folders, sometimes do not work on their own and need Nix's help. And one thing to remember for later: anyone who can use the machine can read the whole room. Never put a password on a shelf. A later chapter shows where secrets go instead.

Try it yourself
  1. Build nixpkgs#hello. Follow result with readlink. Write down the shelf name, and mark where the room, the jumble and the plain name are.
  2. List its direct needs, then its whole closure. On paper, draw boxes and arrows for the first two levels.
  3. Guess first: which is larger, the program or its closure? Check with nix path-info -rsSh.
  4. Delete result. Predict whether the shelf is still there. Check, then run nix-collect-garbage and check again.

Every version gets its own shelf, so nothing you add can break what you already have.

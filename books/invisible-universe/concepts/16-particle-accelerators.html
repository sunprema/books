Particle Accelerators

"None of what we've seen was found by looking harder with a better microscope," Nova said. "It was found by smashing things together, hard enough, and watching what flew out." Everything from the proton's inner quarks to the Higgs boson itself was discovered using machines built for exactly that purpose: particle accelerators.

The idea goes back to Ernest Rutherford, who in 1911 used alpha particles from radioactive decay as tiny projectiles to discover the atomic nucleus, and in 1919 became the first person to deliberately split an atomic nucleus. In 1932, John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton built a 400,000 volt machine and achieved the first fully human-engineered nuclear split — no radioactive source needed, just an electric field doing the accelerating.

The next leap came from Ernest Lawrence, who in 1929-30 invented the cyclotron: instead of one giant, single-shot jolt of voltage, a cyclotron uses a magnetic field to bend particles into a spiral, giving them a small extra kick of energy on every loop. Lawrence's first working model was only about four inches across and accelerated protons to just 1.25 MeV — modest by today's standards, but the design principle behind it — repeated small kicks instead of one giant one — is the same principle the LHC uses today, just scaled up by a factor of billions.

Why this matters Higher collision energy lets you probe smaller distances and create heavier particles — E=mc² runs in both directions: enough concentrated energy can condense into brand-new mass, including particles far heavier than anything in the colliding beams. That's literally how the Higgs boson and the top quark were created and observed: not dug up, but made, fleetingly, out of pure collision energy.
Fun fact Modern accelerators like the LHC don't use one big voltage at all — they use superconducting magnets to bend the beam in a circle and radiofrequency cavities to nudge particles forward a little more on every single lap, building up to energies no single jolt of electricity could ever safely deliver.

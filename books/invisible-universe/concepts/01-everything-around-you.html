Everything Around You

Mira had read the message twice before she believed it. It wasn't from a person. It was from something small enough to sit on her fingertip, glowing a faint, patient blue.

"Hello, Mira. I'm Nova. I go small. Would you like to see how small the world actually goes?"

She looked around her room the way you're probably looking around yours right now. A desk. A phone. A glass of water sweating a ring onto the wood. All of it felt solid, finished, obviously done. That is the first thing you have to unlearn to do particle physics: nothing you can see is actually the smallest version of itself. Every object in this room — including you — is a crowd. A civilization of matter, standing so still and so close together that your eyes read it as one solid thing.

Chemists already know the crowd's roster: about 90 naturally occurring elements, arranged on the periodic table, combine and recombine to build every material you've ever touched — wood, water, skin, steel, stars. That's already a beautiful kind of magic: a small alphabet writing an almost infinite number of words. But this book isn't stopping at the alphabet. We're going to find out what the letters are made of.

Why this matters Understanding what things are actually made of is the difference between memorizing facts and being able to predict new ones. Every material scientists have ever invented — better batteries, cancer drugs, the screen you might be reading this on — came from someone understanding one layer deeper than "it just works that way."

Nova's plan was simple, if a little dizzying: shrink, together, one honest step at a time. Not a magic jump straight to "everything is tiny vibrating strings" (we'll be careful about what's actually established science and what's still a guess). Just: molecule, then atom, then the atom's own insides, then those insides — following the evidence exactly as far as physicists have actually verified it, and being upfront about the edge where they're still investigating.

Quick check Before you turn the page: how many naturally occurring chemical elements do you think exist? (Answer: about 90 — everything else on a full periodic table is made artificially, in labs and reactors.)

"Ready?" Nova asked.

Mira looked at the glass of water on her desk — at the millions of molecules she now knew were sitting inside it, minding their own business, about to become a lot less boring. "Ready," she said.

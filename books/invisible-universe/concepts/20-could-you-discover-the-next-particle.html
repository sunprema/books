Could YOU Discover the Next Particle?

"Every particle in this book," Nova said, "was found by someone who was once exactly as unsure as you are right now." Mira thought about that. Somebody had once stared at cosmic-ray tracks and had no idea what a muon was. Somebody had once written down an equation that seemed to demand antimatter exist, with no proof at all — yet.

Physics doesn't require a single "type" of person. Fabiola Gianotti led the ATLAS team that helped confirm the Higgs boson in 2012, and later became the first woman to serve as CERN's Director-General — proof that leadership in this field is earned, not reserved. And in 1956-57, Chien-Shiung Wu ran a meticulous experiment proving that the weak force doesn't respect mirror symmetry the way physicists had assumed it must — a foundational discovery. The 1957 Nobel Prize for that idea went only to the two theorists who predicted it, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, not to Wu, whose experiment actually proved it true. It's a genuinely uncomfortable footnote in the field's history, and worth knowing exactly because it's uncomfortable — science is done by people, and people don't always get credited fairly.

Why this matters Discovery has come from theorists scribbling equations that demanded a particle exist before anyone saw it (quarks, the positron, the Higgs boson), and from experimentalists building an instrument that revealed something nobody had predicted at all (the muon). There's no one "correct" way in. Curiosity, careful measurement, and a willingness to say "that's strange, let's look closer" are the only requirements history actually shows.
Fun fact Every open question from the last chapter — dark matter, the matter-antimatter imbalance, neutrino masses, gravity's place in quantum theory — is being actively worked on right now, by people who started out exactly where you're standing: curious, and willing to ask "why?" one more time than everyone else did.

Mira looked at Nova, glowing faintly on her fingertip, and then out her window at an ordinary evening sky — full, she now knew, of neutrinos passing straight through the glass, through her, through the whole planet, without stopping. The everyday world hadn't gotten any less familiar. It had gotten enormously more interesting.

One last question Of everything in this book — quarks, neutrinos, the Higgs field, dark matter, antimatter — which open mystery would you want to be the one to solve? There's no wrong answer. Nobody has answered it yet either.

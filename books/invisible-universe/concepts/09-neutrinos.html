Neutrinos

Of everything in this book so far, neutrinos might be the strangest — not because of what they do, but because of how little they do. A neutrino carries no electric charge, feels only the weak force and gravity (skipping both electromagnetism and the strong force entirely), and as a result can pass through ordinary matter almost completely undisturbed. Right now, trillions of neutrinos from the Sun are passing through your body every second, and through the entire planet beneath your feet, without hitting a single atom.

One of three neutrino flavors, paired with the electron, muon, and tau.

There are three neutrino flavors — electron, muon, and tau — one paired with each charged lepton. And they do something no other particle in the Standard Model was originally expected to do: they oscillate, meaning a neutrino created as one flavor can later be measured as a different flavor entirely, as if it quietly changed its identity mid-flight.

Why this matters When the Standard Model was first built, neutrinos were assumed to be exactly massless. Oscillation proved that assumption wrong — it can only happen if neutrinos have some small mass. Takaaki Kajita and Arthur B. McDonald won the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics for proving it. It remains one of the clearest examples of the Standard Model being genuinely, experimentally revised rather than just refined.

Because neutrinos interact so rarely, detecting them at all is its own triumph of engineering — you'll see the giant tanks and detector arrays built just to catch the occasional one in Part VII. The last neutrino flavor confirmed, the tau neutrino, wasn't directly observed until 2000, by the DONUT collaboration at Fermilab — completing the full six-lepton picture from the last chapter.

Fun fact A neutrino could, in principle, pass through a wall of solid lead a light-year thick and have a good chance of never once bumping into an atom. Catching them at all requires enormous, patient detectors — sometimes literally kilometers of ice or water, watched for years.

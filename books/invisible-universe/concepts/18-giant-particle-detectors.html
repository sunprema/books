Giant Particle Detectors

A collision happens in about a trillionth of a second, and whatever's created inside it decays away almost instantly. So how does anyone actually "see" a Higgs boson? They don't — not directly. They build enormous, layered machines that catch and identify everything the original particle decayed into, then work backward.

Four major detectors ring the LHC. ATLAS and CMS are general-purpose detectors, deliberately built by two separate, independent teams using different technical designs — so that if one claims a discovery, the other can independently confirm or refute it. LHCb specializes in particles containing bottom quarks and subtle matter-antimatter differences; ALICE specializes in heavy-ion collisions that briefly recreate conditions from the universe's first moments.

Why this matters Neither ATLAS nor CMS ever detects a Higgs boson directly — its lifetime is far too short. Instead, both reconstruct it from its decay products (for example, a pair of photons, or four leptons) and look for a small statistical "bump" at the right energy, appearing far more often than random chance would explain. That bump, confirmed independently by two different detector teams, is what a discovery actually looks like in practice.
Fun fact ATLAS is officially the largest-volume particle detector ever constructed — about the size of half of Notre-Dame Cathedral — yet CMS, despite being physically more compact, actually weighs almost twice as much, thanks to a denser magnet design. Size and mass don't always track together.
Quick check Why does the LHC run two independent general-purpose detectors, ATLAS and CMS, instead of just one bigger one? (So any claimed discovery can be cross-checked by a completely independent team and design — reducing the chance of a shared mistake going unnoticed.)

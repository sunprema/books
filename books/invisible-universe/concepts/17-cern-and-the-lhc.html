CERN and the Large Hadron Collider

CERN — the European Organization for Nuclear Research — was founded by twelve European countries in a convention that entered into force on 29 September 1954, in the aftermath of a war that had scattered European science across the world. The idea was as much about peace as physics: build something so large that no single nation could afford it alone, and let scientists from former rivals work on it side by side. Today, more than 20 member states fund and share it.

Its most famous instrument is the Large Hadron Collider — the LHC — a ring of superconducting magnets 27 kilometers around, buried about 100 meters underground beneath the France-Switzerland border near Geneva. It's the largest and most powerful particle accelerator ever built.

Inside that ring, protons are pushed to about 99.9999991% of the speed of light by roughly 9,000 superconducting magnets, cooled colder than deep space to keep them superconducting. At full speed, a proton completes about 11,245 laps of the ring every single second, before being steered into a head-on collision with another beam traveling the opposite direction.

Why this matters The LHC is how nearly every particle in the second half of this book was actually confirmed — the top quark's cousins, the precise properties of the W and Z bosons, and most famously the Higgs boson itself in 2012. Bigger rings and stronger magnets aren't just about going faster; they're what let physicists reach collision energies high enough to produce genuinely new, heavy particles.
Fun fact The LHC's Run 3 physics program continued through mid-2026, after which it enters a multi-year shutdown for major upgrades toward the "High-Luminosity LHC" — a redesign meant to produce far more collisions per second, giving physicists a better shot at spotting the rarest events, including possible clues to some of the mysteries in Part VIII.
Quick check Why does the LHC use a chain of smaller accelerators before the final 27 km ring, instead of accelerating protons from a standstill inside the ring itself? (Each stage boosts particles to progressively higher energy before handing them to the next, larger machine — like a relay race, rather than asking one machine to do the entire job.)

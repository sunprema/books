Inside the Proton

"Cracking one open" turned out to be exactly the right phrase, and exactly what a generation of physicists actually did — not with a hammer, but with a beam. In 1968, at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center, teams fired high-energy electrons at protons and watched how they scattered. Some electrons bounced back at sharp, unexpected angles — the same kind of clue Rutherford had used decades earlier to discover the nucleus itself. Something small and hard was inside the proton.

That something turned out to match a proposal two theorists, Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig, had each made independently in 1964: that protons and neutrons aren't elementary at all, but are built from smaller particles. Gell-Mann called them quarks. A proton, in the simplest accurate picture, is three quarks — two of a type called "up" and one of a type called "down" — held together by the strongest force in nature.

Why this matters This was a genuine plot twist in physics history: for decades, protons and neutrons were treated as if they were the true "atoms" — the uncuttable end of the line. 1968 proved that assumption wrong, the same way the discovery of the electron had proven the original Greek "atom" wrong 70 years earlier. Every layer you think is the bottom might not be.

Nova was careful to add the honest fine print here, because a good guide doesn't oversimplify: "three quarks" is the clean textbook picture, but a real proton is busier than that. Alongside its three main ("valence") quarks, it's full of fleeting quark-antiquark pairs popping in and out of existence, and a restless sea of the very particles that hold it all together. We'll keep the three-quark picture as our map — it's accurate enough to build on — but the real proton is a genuinely chaotic, crowded little world.

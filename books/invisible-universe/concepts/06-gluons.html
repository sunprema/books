Gluons

If quarks are the proton's cast of characters, gluons are the thing that keeps them from wandering off set. The name is almost embarrassingly literal: gluons are the "glue" of the strong nuclear force, the particles quarks exchange to hold each other together.

But gluons carry a property that makes them stranger than they first sound: a charge of the strong force called color charge. It has nothing to do with actual visible color — it's just a label physicists borrowed, because a proton's three quarks combine one "red," one "green," and one "blue" color charge to make something color-neutral overall, the same way mixing red, green, and blue light makes white. Every quark carries one of three color charges (or an "anticolor," for antiquarks); every gluon carries a combination of a color and an anticolor.

Unlike the photon, gluons carry the very charge they mediate — so gluons interact with each other, not just with quarks.

That self-interaction is the single biggest difference between the strong force and every other force in this book. A photon (the electromagnetic force's carrier, which you'll meet properly in Part IV) doesn't carry electric charge itself, so photons happily ignore each other. Gluons carry color charge, so gluons stick to other gluons — which is exactly why the strong force behaves so unlike gravity or electromagnetism: it doesn't fade out with distance. It gets stronger.

Why this matters This single fact — that the strong force grows with distance instead of shrinking — is the entire reason matter is stable at all. It's why protons and neutrons hold together so fiercely, and it's the direct cause of quark confinement you read about last chapter: there's no such thing as "pulling two quarks slightly apart." You either keep them bound, or you make new particles.
Fun fact "Color charge" is one of the friendliest bits of jargon in physics — physicists picked red/green/blue purely because mixing them makes "white" (color-neutral), a nice visual metaphor for how a proton's quarks always add up to something with no net color. No actual color is involved at any point.

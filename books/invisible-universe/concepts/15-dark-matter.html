Dark Matter

Everything in this book so far — every quark, every lepton, every force carrier — belongs to what astronomers call "ordinary" or "baryonic" matter. And here's the humbling part: ordinary matter makes up less than about 5% of everything in the universe. The rest is something else entirely, something the Standard Model doesn't include at all.

Nobody has ever directly detected a dark matter particle. What astronomers have measured, repeatedly and consistently, is its gravitational fingerprint: galaxies rotate far faster at their outer edges than the visible matter in them could possibly explain — as if an enormous, invisible "halo" of extra mass surrounds every galaxy, holding it together. Gravitational lensing (light bending as it passes massive objects) and patterns in the cosmic microwave background — the faint afterglow of the Big Bang — both point to the same missing mass.

Why this matters Dark matter is a genuine open frontier, not a settled fact with a missing particle catalog entry. Leading candidate ideas include WIMPs (weakly interacting massive particles) and axions, but none has been confirmed. CERN's NA64 experiment hunts for light dark-matter candidates using high-energy particle beams, and the LHC's own detectors search for tell-tale "missing energy" signatures — collision energy that seems to vanish, as if carried off by an undetected particle.
Fun fact "Dark" doesn't mean black or hidden in shadow — it means dark matter doesn't interact with light at all: it doesn't emit it, absorb it, or reflect it. It would be perfectly invisible even standing in bright sunlight, which is exactly why its only known evidence is gravitational, not visual.
Quick check Has a dark matter particle ever been directly detected? (No — as of this book's research, only its gravitational effects have been observed; the particle itself remains undiscovered.)

Quarks

Six quarks exist, as far as anyone has ever found — no more, no less, arranged in three pairs physicists call "generations." Only the lightest pair, up and down, shows up in the ordinary matter around you. The other four are heavier, unstable, and mostly show up briefly in cosmic rays or inside particle-accelerator collisions before decaying into lighter particles.

Notice the mass column: a top quark outweighs an up quark by a factor of about 80,000. A single top quark is roughly as massive as an entire atom of gold — an absurd amount of mass to pack into something with no known size at all. The top quark wasn't confirmed until 1995, at Fermilab's Tevatron collider, partly because you need an enormous amount of energy to ever produce something that heavy.

Quarks also carry a strange feature: their electric charge is a fraction — either +2/3 or -1/3 of an electron's charge. Nothing else in nature does this. But because quarks are never found alone (more on that in a moment), their fractional charges always add up to a clean whole number in anything you could actually detect:

// proton: up + up + down
(+2/3) + (+2/3) + (-1/3) = +1

// neutron: up + down + down
(+2/3) + (-1/3) + (-1/3) = 0
Why this matters This is why nobody has ever isolated a single quark in a detector. Try to pull two quarks apart and the force between them doesn't fade with distance — it grows, like stretching a rubber band. Eventually there's enough energy stored in that "band" to spontaneously create a brand-new quark-antiquark pair, snapping the rubber band into two shorter pieces instead of one long one. You never get a lone quark; you get more particles.
Quick check Why can't you ever isolate a single quark? (Pulling quarks apart takes so much energy that new quark-antiquark pairs form before you can separate them — a phenomenon called confinement.)

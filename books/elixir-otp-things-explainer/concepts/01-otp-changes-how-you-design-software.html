Elixir calls this: OTP

A Different Way to Build Software

Here's the problem

Picture a little chat app with one big room full of people. Everything — who's in the room, what they said, who's typing — lives in one giant pile of shared notes on a table in the middle of the office. Every worker who wants to read a note, or add one, has to walk up to that same table.

Now two workers reach for the same note at the same time. One is rewriting it while the other is reading it. Nobody agreed on whose turn it was. The note tears a little. Nobody notices until later, when someone reads a name that's half old, half new.

That's a race — two things happening "at the same time" that step on each other because they share one pile of notes with no rule about turns.

And it gets worse

Say one worker at that shared table has a meltdown — drops their pen, knocks over their coffee, storms off. Because everyone was sharing the same table, the mess is everyone's mess now. The whole office stops to clean up, and nobody quite remembers who was doing what.

Most programming languages hand you exactly one office layout: everyone shares one big table (shared memory), and if you want two people writing at once without tearing a note, you hand out little "please wait your turn" locks yourself, for every single note, and hope you never forget one.

The rule

Elixir runs on a machine — the BEAM — that gives every worker their own private desk instead of one shared table. A worker's notes belong to that worker alone. Nobody else can read them or scribble on them directly. The only way to give a worker information, or ask it to do something, is to slide it a written note — and the worker reads its notes one at a time, in the order they arrived, whenever it's ready.

Each of these desk-and-worker setups is called a process (not the heavy "whole program" kind of process your operating system runs — a much lighter, cheaper one; a real app might have thousands running at once, quietly, on one machine). Because nobody but the worker ever touches its own notes, there's no table to fight over, and so no torn notes.

That single change — give up the shared table, hand out private desks and notes instead — is the whole idea underneath OTP. It's a set of building blocks and a shared way of working, not one library you bolt onto a project for one feature. Once workers only talk by sliding notes, a second question shows up on its own: what happens when a worker has a meltdown at their own desk?

Here's the second problem

Say the worker running "room: acme" hits a note it truly cannot make sense of — maybe someone sent it garbage instead of a real message — and it just stops working, mid-task, notes half-read. With private desks, that mess stays at that one desk. It doesn't spill onto the table, because there is no shared table. But someone still has to notice the empty desk and decide what happens next: does the room just stay dead? Does someone throw the whole office out and start over?

The rule

OTP's answer: certain workers, called supervisors, don't do the room-hosting or message-answering work themselves. Their entire job is to watch a handful of other workers and decide what to do the moment one of them stops — usually: clear the desk and start a brand new worker in its place, ready to go.

So the shape of an OTP program is really two kinds of workers wearing two different hats: workers do the work; supervisors decide what happens when a worker stops doing it. Everything in this book — the specific kind of worker called a GenServer, the trees of supervisors watching workers watching sub-supervisors — is built out of just those two ideas, over and over.

Once you stop sharing a table and start sliding notes instead, "two things happening at once safely" and "one thing breaking without taking everything else down with it" turn out to be almost the same idea, solved the same way.

Elixir calls this: Supervisor

Letting a Worker Fall Down Safely

Here's the problem

Our RoomServer is going to see bad notes eventually — a message shaped wrong, a bug we haven't found yet. When that happens the worker hits code it can't make sense of and simply stops. Its desk sits there empty. Do we really want to write a wall of "what if this is nonsense" checks in front of every single note, forever, just in case?

The rule

OTP's answer is almost the opposite of "check everything defensively": mostly don't catch every possible error. Let a worker that's truly confused just stop — this is nicknamed "let it crash." A stopped worker leaves behind a clean, empty desk, not a half-corrupted one still limping along with bad memory. Somebody else's whole job is to notice the empty desk and deal with it.

That "somebody else" is a supervisor — a special worker that doesn't do room-hosting work itself. It just watches a list of other workers, and the moment one of them stops, it decides what happens next. The connection that lets a supervisor find out the instant a worker stops is called a link: two linked workers are told about each other's exits.

Watching it happen

Say we deliberately send our own room a note it can't handle:

GenServer.cast(room, :crash)

If nothing handles :crash, the worker stops right there. Four things happen, in order:

  1. The worker's desk disappears — the process is gone.
  2. The supervisor watching it is told immediately (that's what the link is for).
  3. The supervisor starts a brand new worker to take its place.
  4. The new worker's memory starts fresh from init/1 — whatever the old one was holding is simply gone, unless it lived somewhere durable, not just in that worker's own memory.
The one thing to really remember from this whole book

A supervisor brings back a worker. It does not bring back the room's chat history. Supervision restarts the process, not whatever business facts were only ever living in that process's own memory. If a room's messages need to survive a crash, they need to live somewhere sturdier than one worker's state — a database, or a file — and this book will keep coming back to that line, because it's the single most common wrong assumption people bring to OTP.

Hiring the watcher itself

A supervisor is hired almost like any other worker, except instead of one job description, you hand it a list of workers to watch and a rule for how to react:

children = [
  {RoomServer, :lobby}
]

Supervisor.start_link(children, strategy: :one_for_one, name: MyApp.Supervisor)

:one_for_one is the plainest rule there is: when one watched worker stops, restart only that one — its deskmates are left alone. (There are rules for restarting a whole group of deskmates together too, but this book only ever needs the plain one.)

Worth knowing — not every stop deserves a restart

Each watched worker also carries a label for why it stopped matters: :permanent ("always bring it back, no matter how it stopped" — the default, and what a room worker should be), :temporary ("never bring it back — a one-off job that's done when it's done"), and :transient ("bring it back only if it stopped messily, not if it finished cleanly on purpose").

And a supervisor won't restart forever, no matter what: it counts how many restarts happen in a short window, and if a worker is truly stuck in a crash-restart-crash loop, the supervisor gives up and stops itself too, rather than burning the whole machine restarting something broken over and over. That's the safety valve underneath the safety feature.

Letting a confused worker simply stop, instead of limping on half-broken, is what makes a clean restart possible — the crash is the safety feature, not the failure.

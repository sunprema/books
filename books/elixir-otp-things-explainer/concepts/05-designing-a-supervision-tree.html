Elixir calls this: supervision tree

Drawing the Family Tree of Who Watches Whom

Here's the problem

One supervisor watching one room worker is easy to picture. A real app needs a whole office full of workers: a place that remembers which rooms exist, workers hosting each room, workers running background errands. Somebody has to decide who starts first, who gets grouped together, and — if a piece breaks — how much of the office is allowed to go down with it.

The rule

Every OTP app has one top-level supervisor that starts when the app starts — the root of the whole office chart. Everything it watches gets started in a fixed, known order, every single time the app boots, before anyone's allowed to send a note to any of it.

And supervisors can watch other supervisors, not just plain workers — that's what turns a flat list into a tree. Ownership only ever points one way, down the tree: a supervisor owns its children, and a child never reaches back up to restart or reconfigure the very supervisor watching it.

Deciding what goes together

Should the room-registry desk and the room workers live under the very same supervisor, or under two different ones? This isn't a technicality — it's the real design question the whole tree answers: if this breaks, how much of the rest of the office is allowed to go down with it, and how much should stay standing?

Group two workers under the same supervisor with the plain :one_for_one rule from the last chapter, and a crash in one doesn't touch the other at all — good when they're genuinely independent. Group things that can't sensibly run without each other more tightly, and a failure in one is meant to take its whole little group down together for a clean joint restart. Choosing which is which, for every worker in the office, is what "designing a supervision tree" actually means.

Worth knowing

A supervision tree isn't just start-up plumbing you write once and forget. Read correctly, it's a plain diagram of who owns what, what order things start and stop in, and — most importantly — which failures are walled off from which. That's genuinely the app's failure-recovery design, drawn as a tree, not a side note about it.

Every box in the tree is a promise about blast radius: how far a crash in that box is allowed to spread before something stops it.

Chapter OneWhy EDI Still Matters

Every few years someone announces that EDI is dead. Meanwhile a trillion dollars of trade moves through it, and a supplier who cannot send an 856 does not get shelf space.

Let's start with what the acronym actually promises. Electronic Data Interchange is the exchange of standard business documents between organizations in a structured, machine-readable form — purchase orders, invoices, shipping notices — without a human retyping anything at either end. That is the whole idea, and it is older than the web.

What makes it interesting to us as engineers is not the format. It is that EDI is an interoperability contract you do not control. When you build a REST API, you define the schema and your consumers adapt. In EDI the trading partner hands you a 200-page implementation guide and you adapt. That inversion is the source of nearly every design decision in this book.

Where it lives

EDI is the connective tissue of supply chains: retail, logistics, manufacturing, healthcare and finance. Some of that is inertia — the integrations work and nobody reopens them. But a good deal of it is not optional at all. In US healthcare, X12 formats are legally mandated: HIPAA designates them as the standard for electronic healthcare administrative and financial transactions. There is no "we'll expose an API instead" conversation to be had.

In retail the compulsion is commercial rather than statutory, and just as firm. Most large retailers specify AS2 or a VAN in their supplier onboarding requirements, and a supplier has essentially no room to negotiate. The format is a condition of doing business.

Why it persists next to APIs

The honest answer is that EDI and APIs solve different shapes of problem. An API call is one entity, right now, with a synchronous answer. An EDI interchange is a batch of documents, dispatched on a schedule, whose answer comes back minutes or hours later as another document. That difference in granularity and timing — not the syntax — is what makes the two awkward to bridge.

The cycle, end to end

Here is the whole book in one paragraph. A buyer sends a purchase order — an 850. The supplier's system receives it and immediately returns a 997, which says only "this was syntactically valid and I have it". Some time later the supplier returns an 855, which says whether the order was actually accepted, rejected, or accepted with changes. When the goods ship, an 856 advance ship notice describes what is on the truck and in which cartons. Finally an 810 invoice asks for payment, and an 820 remittance advice accompanies it.

The first mistake everyone makes

The 997 is not an order confirmation. It is a syntax receipt. A system that treats a 997 as "they accepted my order" will happily manufacture goods against an order the partner rejected an hour later in the 855. Keep these two ideas apart from the very first day; almost every chapter that follows depends on the distinction.

So what did we actually gain?

A framing, mostly. If you take EDI as a set of file formats, the work looks like writing parsers, and you will be surprised in production. If you take it as a distributed integration problem with an externally-owned schema, an at-least-once transport and a slow asynchronous acknowledgment loop, then everything you already know about idempotency, replay and observability applies directly — and that knowledge is most of what makes an EDI service survive.

The next chapter maps the actors: who sends, who relays, who acknowledges, and who gets to define what "valid" means.

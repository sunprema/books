Chapter FifteenEDI Architecture Patterns

Four topologies, one recurring mistake: putting EDI inside the system it feeds.

Let's start with the decision that has the longest half-life. Where does EDI processing live?

Four topologies

Build, buy, or delegate
PatternYou operateFits when
Direct integrationEverything: transport, translation, mappingFew partners, unusual requirements, EDI is core to the product
Managed EDI providerOnly the canonical boundaryMany partners, small team, standard retail flows
iPaaSMappings and orchestration; they run the plumbingEDI is one of several integration styles you already run there

Most organizations of any age are hybrid: a VAN for the long tail, direct AS2 for the two partners who account for most of the volume, and their own service in the middle. That is not indecision — it is the correct response to a partner mix with a very uneven distribution.

A dedicated service, not a module

Whatever the topology, put EDI processing in its own service rather than inside the ERP or order system. The reason is organisational as much as technical: partner onboarding happens on the partner's schedule, and if EDI lives inside the ERP then onboarding is coupled to the ERP's release cycle. You end up telling a new customer they can start trading in November because that is when the next window is.

A separate service also gives the partner-specific ugliness somewhere to live that is not the core domain model — which is chapter 14's rule, expressed as a deployment boundary.

Queue-based processing

EDI traffic is burst-and-idle. Partners transmit on schedules — overnight batches, hourly drops — so a day's documents can land in a few minutes. A queue between the transport adapters and the pipeline smooths that, lets you scale consumers independently, and gives retries somewhere natural to live.

It also composes well with chapter 12: because the deduplication check is inside the pipeline rather than at the queue, at-least-once queue delivery adds no new hazard. The same document arriving twice from the queue is handled by the same mechanism that handles a partner resend.

Configuration over branches

The recurring theme of the last three chapters, stated as an architectural property: onboarding a partner should not require a deploy. That means partner profiles — identifiers, transport, validation rules, mappings, cadence — are data the service reads, not code it ships.

A rules engine is not the first step

The natural end point of configuration-driven behaviour is a rules engine, and it is a fine place to arrive. It is a poor place to start. With three partners, a profile is a JSON document and a handful of well-named functions. Build the engine when the profiles tell you which abstractions they actually need — not before.

Multi-tenant concerns

If you are building EDI as a product rather than for one company, one detail from chapter 12 becomes critical. Control numbers are unique only within a partner relationship, so a multi-tenant deduplication key must include the tenant as well as the partner and direction. Miss it and tenant B's interchange 000000117 is silently treated as a duplicate of tenant A's — a bug that is invisible in testing and severe in production.

The same applies to the control-number allocator: monotonic per tenant, per partner, per direction, three dimensions in the counter.

A reference architecture

Putting it together, a small but production-ready EDI service is: transport adapters writing raw payloads to an archive and references to a queue; a stateless pipeline consuming that queue, reading partner profiles from configuration, deduplicating on control numbers, and publishing canonical documents; an outbound generator working from the same profiles; and a support screen over the archive. Five components, and every one of them has appeared in this book already.

So what did we actually gain?

A shape to build toward that keeps partner churn away from the core domain and keeps onboarding off the release calendar. Chapter 16 builds the smallest honest version of it.

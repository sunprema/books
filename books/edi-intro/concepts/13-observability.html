Chapter ThirteenObservability and Supportability

The characteristic EDI outage raises no exception, because nothing arrived. If your alerting only watches for errors, it will not fire.

Every EDI support question has the same shape: "what happened to purchase order PO-88213?" Answering it means reconstructing one document's journey across transport, parsing, mapping, a downstream call and an acknowledgment. If those five stages do not share an identifier, the answer takes an afternoon of log archaeology.

Correlate on what both sides know

Use the control numbers. Not a UUID you minted on receipt — the partner has never heard of that. The interchange, group and transaction set numbers are the only identifiers both sides of the relationship share, which means the same key that finds the document in your logs is the key you can quote in an email to their EDI coordinator.

Carry that correlation id through every stage, and attach the business identifier (the PO number) as soon as parsing yields it, so support can search by either.

Logs that do not leak

EDI payloads carry commercially sensitive data — negotiated pricing, volumes, customer addresses — and in healthcare they carry regulated patient data under HIPAA. The convenient debugging habit of logging the whole document copies all of that into your log aggregator, where it is retained, replicated and searchable by more people than should see it.

So: log identifiers and status, not bodies. The raw document is already archived (stage 2) in a store you control, with its own access rules. A log line points at it; it does not reproduce it.

{
  "event": "edi.document.rejected",
  "partner": "acme-retail",
  "transactionSet": "850",
  "interchange": "000000117",
  "purchaseOrder": "PO-88213",
  "findings": [{ "rule": "acme-retail/uom-allowed",
                "segment": "PO1", "position": 12 }],
  "rawRef": "s3://edi-archive/acme-retail/2026/08/15/000000117.edi"
}

The metrics that matter

Count documents by partner and transaction type — both dimensions, always, because an aggregate number hides exactly the failure you care about. The states worth counting are: received, accepted, rejected, retried, delayed and completed.

A dashboard broken down that way answers the operational question at a glance: is this a global problem or one partner's? Is it one transaction type or all of them?

Alert on absence

Here is the heart of the chapter. Exception-based alerting cannot detect the most common EDI production failure, which is that a partner stopped sending. Their scheduler broke, a certificate expired, someone changed an SFTP path. No error is raised on your side because no document arrived to fail.

Three alerts cover it:

  • Acknowledgment timeout — we sent a document and no 997 came back within the agreed window.
  • Volume anomaly — this partner normally sends 40–60 orders on a weekday and today sent none, or sent 4,000.
  • Silence — nothing at all from a partner whose expected cadence has elapsed.
Expected cadence is partner configuration

"Daily by 6am", "hourly during business hours", "weekly on Mondays" — this belongs in the partner profile next to their identifiers and endpoints, captured during onboarding. Without it you cannot distinguish a quiet partner from a broken one, and the silence alert cannot exist.

The support screen

Finally, build the screen. Not a dashboard — a search tool, for the person who has to answer for PO-88213. It needs four things:

  1. Search by control number, PO number, partner, or date range.
  2. The document's status and its full stage-by-stage history.
  3. The raw payload, viewable — because "what did they actually send us" is unanswerable without it, and it is the question that ends most disputes.
  4. A safe replay action, which by chapter 12's design is genuinely safe: it goes through the same deduplication check as any other document.
Build it early

This screen is invariably deferred and invariably the thing that gets built in a panic during the first month of production. It is a day's work against data you are already storing, and it is what allows support to answer partner questions without an engineer.

So what did we actually gain?

A system that can be asked questions. Part IV is done — the pipeline now survives duplicates, replays cleanly, and reports its own silence. Part V takes it to a real trading partner.

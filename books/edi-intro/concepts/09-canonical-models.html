Chapter NineCanonical Data Models and Mapping

Map each partner straight into your ERP and you have signed up for N×M maintenance. One model in the middle turns it into N + M.

Suppose you have eight trading partners and three downstream systems. If each partner's 850 is mapped directly into each system's schema, you own twenty-four maps, and a change to any downstream schema means revisiting eight of them. Nobody plans this; it accretes, one urgent partner at a time.

The nouns worth modelling

EDI keeps re-describing the same handful of things. Model these, and most transaction sets become a rearrangement of them: orders, line items, parties, addresses, products, shipments and invoices.

Here is our 850 as a canonical order:

{
  "documentType": "purchase_order",
  "partner": "acme-retail",
  "purchaseOrderNumber": "PO-88213",
  "orderedAt": "2026-08-15",
  "shipTo": { "idQualifier": "92", "id": "DC07",
               "name": "ACME RETAIL DC 7" },
  "lines": [
    { "lineNumber": 1, "quantity": 24, "unit": "CASE",
      "unitPrice": "18.50", "currency": "USD",
      "productIds": { "buyerPart": "WID-1001",
                      "upc": "000123456789" } }
  ],
  "sourceControl": { "interchange": "000000117",
                     "group": "38",
                     "transactionSet": "0001" }
}

Two design choices in there are worth defending.

First, qualifiers become named fields. The EDI form is "here is an identifier, and here is a code saying what kind"; the canonical form is buyerPart and upc. That is the whole point — downstream code should not be reading qualifier tables. But note that shipTo keeps its idQualifier: where the qualifier is genuinely open-ended, preserving it beats inventing a field per possible value.

Second, sourceControl is retained. The control numbers are our idempotency key (chapter 12) and our correlation id (chapter 13). Dropping them at the mapping boundary is a decision you will regret the first time support asks which interchange an order came from.

Where the real work is

Mapping is rarely hard; normalisation is. Five axes account for most of it:

The five normalisation axes
AxisWhat varies
Unit of measureCA, CS, BX, EA — and whether a "case" is 12 or 24.
Currency & moneyImplied decimals, currency codes, price per unit versus per pack.
Date & timeCCYYMMDD vs YYMMDD, time zones, "requested" vs "must arrive by".
LocationDUNS, buyer-assigned codes, GLNs, store numbers — all in the same field.
Product identityUPC, GTIN, buyer part, vendor part, SKU. Rarely all present.
Money is not a float

X12 frequently uses implied decimals — a total of 44400 meaning 444.00. Parse to a decimal type at the boundary and keep it decimal all the way through. A cent lost to binary floating point in an invoice is a chargeback with your name on it.

Tables, code, or a DSL?

Three approaches, in increasing order of ambition. Mapping tables (data: this qualifier means this field) handle the bulk and are editable by non-developers. Code-based maps handle the conditional cases tables cannot express and are testable with ordinary tools. A declarative mapping DSL is the endgame for a large EDI shop, and premature for anyone else — you will spend more building the DSL than you save on the first ten partners.

Whichever you choose, keep the transformation versioned and testable. A map is business logic; it deserves the same fixtures, review and history as the rest of the codebase.

Guard against drift

The canonical model's failure mode is quiet: it slowly becomes a copy of your largest partner's implementation guide. A useful test — when onboarding a new partner, if the canonical model needs a new field, ask whether the field is genuinely a business concept or just that partner's vocabulary. The latter belongs in their profile, not in the middle.

So what did we actually gain?

A stable centre. Partners change, downstream systems change, and the model between them absorbs both. It also gives chapter 10 something to generate from: outbound documents are built from the canonical model, never from an inbound document.

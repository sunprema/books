Chapter TwelveReliability, Idempotency, and Recovery

Every EDI transport delivers at least once. Exactly-once business effects are something you build, in one specific place, on a key the sender already gave you.

Let's be precise about the guarantee we actually have. Documents get redelivered because of network timeouts, TCP retransmission, upstream retry queues, VAN redelivery, and — most often of all — a human at the partner clicking "resend" because they did not see an acknowledgment. None of these are exotic. All of them are Tuesday.

So the transport gives you at-least-once delivery, and what the business needs is exactly-once effect: one order, one shipment, one invoice. The gap between those two is bridged in exactly one place in your pipeline, and it is worth being deliberate about where.

The key you were already given

You do not need to invent an idempotency key. The sender assigns one, at three levels, and repeats it on a resend: the interchange, group and transaction set control numbers from chapter 3.

Together with the partner and direction, they form a natural primary key:

CREATE TABLE edi_received (
  partner_id      text        NOT NULL,
  direction       text        NOT NULL,
  interchange_ctl text        NOT NULL,
  group_ctl       text        NOT NULL,
  txn_ctl         text        NOT NULL,
  payload_sha256  bytea       NOT NULL,
  received_at     timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  PRIMARY KEY (partner_id, direction,
               interchange_ctl, group_ctl, txn_ctl)
);

Two details in that table do real work.

partner_id and direction are part of the key. Control numbers are only unique within one partner relationship and one direction. Two partners will both send interchange 000000117 eventually, and they are not the same document.

The payload hash is stored alongside. ISA13 is nine digits and partners recycle it — over a long enough relationship the same number comes round again on a genuinely different document. Comparing the hash distinguishes "this is the resend we expected" from "this is a new document that happens to reuse a number", and the two demand opposite responses.

What to do with a duplicate

Not "ignore it". A duplicate usually means the partner never saw your acknowledgment, so the correct response is to re-send the stored acknowledgment and take no further action. That closes the loop that caused the resend in the first place; simply dropping the document silently leaves the partner resending forever.

Don't deduplicate on the filename

Filenames are a transport convention, not part of the document. VANs rename files, partners change their patterns, and the same interchange can arrive twice under two names. The control numbers are inside the document, chosen by the sender, and stable across every transport it might traverse.

Making reprocessing safe

Chapter 7 insisted on archiving raw bytes at stage 2. This is the payoff. If the raw payload survives and the transformation is deterministic — same bytes in, same canonical document out — then reprocessing is a safe, ordinary operation rather than a tense one. You can fix a mapping bug and replay six months of documents through the corrected code.

Determinism is a constraint you have to keep deliberately. A map that reads "today" for a defaulted date, or looks up a mutable product table without recording what it found, is not reproducible — replaying it next year gives different answers. Inject the clock; snapshot or version the lookups that matter.

Dead letters and humans

Some documents cannot be processed no matter how many times you try: an unsupported version, a code the partner has never used before, a ship-to that does not exist in your system. These belong in a dead-letter path with a human resolution workflow — a queue someone actually looks at, with the raw document, the findings, and a button to reprocess after a fix.

The alternative — retrying forever with exponential backoff — is worse than useless: it turns a document that needed five minutes of human attention into a permanent background error rate that everyone learns to ignore.

Out of order, late, missing

Three cousins of the duplicate. An 856 arriving before its 855 is normal and your state machine should tolerate it. A late document may reference an order already closed. A missing one produces no event at all — which is why chapter 13 alerts on absence, not just on errors.

So what did we actually gain?

One deduplication check, correctly placed, on a key the sender already provides — and with it, exactly-once business effects over an at-least-once transport. Plus the ability to replay history through fixed code, which is the closest thing to a superpower an integration team has.

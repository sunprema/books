Chapter TwoThe EDI Ecosystem

Before you can process a document you have to know who sent it, who is relaying it, and whose rules decide whether it is valid. Those are three different parties, and confusing them is expensive.

An EDI relationship has more moving parts than "two systems talking". Let's name them, because the vocabulary shows up in every partner conversation you will ever have.

The actors

Who is who
ActorRole
Trading partnerThe other business. Sends and receives documents; publishes the implementation guide you must conform to.
VANValue-added network. A managed mailbox service that relays documents between partners regardless of each side's protocol.
Service providerA managed EDI vendor that operates translation and connectivity on your behalf.
ClearinghouseThe healthcare equivalent: relays and pre-validates claims between providers and payers.
Business systemYour ERP, OMS or WMS — where the document's meaning finally lands.

The crucial property of a VAN is that it is a post office, not an inspector. You deliver to the VAN and the VAN delivers into each partner's mailbox, handling protocol translation and keeping an audit trail. It does not enforce your partner's business rules. A document can be perfectly delivered and still be rejected on arrival.

Direct or through a network

A direct (point-to-point) connection means you and the partner agree a protocol — AS2, SFTP, FTPS, OFTP — and connect with no intermediary. Every such link is its own small project: certificates to exchange and renew, firewall rules to maintain, connection tests to schedule, and a distinct thing to monitor.

A VAN trades that per-partner work for a fee. Pricing is typically per document or per character, so the economics invert with volume: direct connections favour a few high-volume partners, a VAN favours many low-volume ones. Most organizations of any size end up with both.

Standard, version, transaction set, guide

This is the distinction that most often bites a first implementation, so let's be precise about the four layers.

Standard
The syntax family. ANSI ASC X12 dominates North America; UN/EDIFACT is the international standard; TRADACOMS (UK retail), ODETTE and VDA (European automotive) are regional or industry variants.
Version / release
A dated revision of that standard — 004010, 005010 in X12; D96A, D01B in EDIFACT. Partners on different versions of the same transaction are not interchangeable.
Transaction set
One document type within a version: the 850 purchase order, the 810 invoice.
Implementation guide
The partner's own narrowing of all of the above. It marks optional segments required, fixes code lists, and adds rules the standard never mentions.
Build to the guide, not the standard

The standard tells you what is possible. The implementation guide tells you what is acceptable, and it is the only one of the two that will reject your documents. A validator built solely from the base standard will pass a document that your partner will bounce — usually during certification, and usually a week before go-live.

So what did we actually gain?

A map of where authority sits. Delivery is the VAN's or the protocol's problem. Syntax is the standard's. Acceptability is the partner's, and it is defined in a document you must read and turn into executable rules. We will do exactly that in chapter 14. First, chapter 3 takes a document apart segment by segment.

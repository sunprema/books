Chapter 06

Replication, Durability, and Failure

Durability is not a setting. It is the interaction of three settings, and getting two right while getting the third wrong gives you the confidence of the first two with the safety of the third.

Leaders, followers, and the in-sync set

Each partition has one leader and some followers. All reads and writes for a partition go through its leader; followers fetch from the leader and try to keep up.

A follower that is keeping up is in sync, and the set of such replicas is the ISR (in-sync replicas). A follower that falls too far behind — because its broker is slow, garbage collecting, or partitioned from the network — is removed from the ISR. It can rejoin once it catches up. The ISR is therefore dynamic, and its size is the thing your durability guarantee is actually stated over.

The high water mark is the offset up to which all in-sync replicas have replicated. Consumers can only read up to it. This is what prevents a consumer from reading a record that would vanish if the leader failed right now — you cannot observe data that is not yet replicated.

The three settings that must agree

Durability comes from the interaction of three settings. None is sufficient alone:

  • Replication factor (topic) — how many copies exist at all. This is the ceiling on everything else.
  • min.insync.replicas (topic or broker) — how many replicas must be in sync for a write with acks=all to be accepted. If the ISR drops below this, writes are rejected rather than silently accepted into a fragile state.
  • acks (producer) — whether the producer waits for the ISR at all. A producer with acks=1 ignores min.insync.replicas entirely.

The combination to remember

Replication factor 3, min.insync.replicas 2, acks=all. This is the common production default and the right starting point for anything you care about. With RF 3 and minISR 2, the producer can continue unaffected while a single broker is unavailable, provided another replica remains in sync — you tolerate one failure without losing writes, and you refuse to accept writes that only one replica holds.

The arithmetic is worth stating plainly: you tolerate RF − minISR broker failures while still accepting writes, and you tolerate minISR − 1 failures without losing acknowledged data. RF 3 / minISR 2 gives one of each. That symmetry is why it is the default answer.

Trap

Setting min.insync.replicas equal to the replication factor. It looks maximally safe and is operationally brittle: any single broker going down — including for a routine rolling restart — stops writes to that topic entirely. You have configured an outage into your deployment process.

Unclean leader election

Suppose every in-sync replica for a partition is unavailable, and the only surviving replica is one that had fallen out of the ISR — it is missing the most recent records. You have two choices, and Kafka makes you pick in advance.

With unclean leader election disabled (the safe default), the partition stays offline until an in-sync replica returns. Availability suffers; no acknowledged data is lost.

With it enabled, that out-of-sync replica becomes leader. The partition comes back immediately — and the records it never received are gone, permanently, including records that were acknowledged to producers. Consumers that had read past that point will see offsets go backwards.

This is the clearest availability-versus-durability dial in Kafka. For an OrderPlaced topic the answer is obviously "stay offline." For a clickstream feeding a dashboard it is arguably "come back up." The mistake is having one cluster-wide answer for both.

So what did we actually gain?

A concrete meaning for the phrase "this event is durable." It means: replicated to at least minISR replicas before the producer was told it succeeded, on a topic where an out-of-sync replica will never be promoted. Anything less is a weaker promise, and the consumers of your contract deserve to know which one they have.

Chapter 02

Kafka's Data Model

A topic is an append-only log, not a queue. Almost every design mistake in this book traces back to forgetting that sentence.

A log, not a queue

In a traditional queue, reading is destructive: a consumer takes a message and it is gone. The broker tracks what has been delivered, delivery is the point, and the data's lifetime is "until someone consumed it."

Kafka inverts this. Records are appended to a partition and stay there for as long as the retention policy says, regardless of who has read them. Consumers track their own position — an offset — and reading does not remove anything. Two independent consumer groups read the same records without knowing about each other.

This single difference gives you replay, multiple independent consumers, and the ability to add a consumer years later. It also means the broker will not help you with per-message acknowledgement, priority, or selective redelivery, because those are queue features and this is not a queue.

Key idea

A consumer's offset is independent of whether the data still exists. Retention governs the data; the committed offset only records progress. A consumer can be at offset 400 on a partition whose oldest surviving record is 900 — that consumer has silently lost data and will be reset.

Anatomy of a record

A record has more moving parts than "a message body," and each part is a design decision:

The parts of a Kafka record and what they decide
PartWhat it isWhat it decides
KeyOptional bytesPartition, therefore ordering scope and compaction identity
ValueThe payloadThe contract (Chapters 3, 10–12)
HeadersKey/value metadataTrace context, schema hints, routing — things consumers read without deserializing the value
TimestampCreation or log-append timeRetention, stream-time, windowing
OffsetAssigned by the brokerPosition; monotonic within a partition only

Headers deserve a note. Putting trace context in headers rather than the payload means you can propagate it without a schema change, and consumers that do not care never pay to parse it. Putting business data in headers is the opposite of this — it escapes the contract entirely and no compatibility check will ever look at it. Headers are for plumbing.

Partitions: the decision that keeps costing you

A topic is split into partitions, and this is where the design questions concentrate. Kafka provides ordering guarantees within a partition, not across a topic. A record's key is hashed to select a partition, so the key you choose is the ordering scope you promise.

Three consequences follow immediately, and they are the reason this chapter sits in Part I rather than Part II:

  • Ordering scope is a contract term. If you key by orderId, you have promised consumers that one order's events arrive in order — and promised nothing about two different orders. Write that down; consumers will assume more than you meant.
  • Partition count caps consumer parallelism. A partition is assigned to at most one consumer within a group, so a group can never have more usefully-working members than the topic has partitions. Your throughput ceiling was set when someone typed --partitions 3.
  • Repartitioning breaks the promise. Adding partitions changes the key-to-partition mapping, so a key that used to land in P1 may now land in P4 — while its history sits in P1. Ordering for existing keys is broken at that boundary. There is no clean fix, only planning.

Trap

A null key round-robins across partitions. That is fine for genuinely independent records and catastrophic for anything with a lifecycle — you have opted out of ordering without saying so anywhere.

Consumer groups

A consumer group is Kafka's unit of work distribution. Members of a group divide the partitions between them; each partition goes to exactly one member. Add a member and partitions redistribute; lose a member and its partitions move elsewhere. Different groups are entirely independent — that is what lets a new team start consuming your topic without asking permission or affecting anyone.

Group membership is also where most operational pain lives, which is Chapter 7's subject. For now the contract-level fact is the important one: the group is the unit of "a consumer," and the partition count is the ceiling on its parallelism.

Retention versus compaction

Kafka offers two cleanup policies, and they answer different questions.

Delete (time- or size-based) says: keep records for a while, then drop the oldest. This suits a stream of facts. "We keep 30 days of OrderPlaced" is a retention statement, and it is also a replay-window statement — you can only rebuild a consumer from data that still exists.

Compact says: keep at least the latest value for each key, forever. This turns the topic into a changelog of keyed state rather than a stream of events. A compacted customer-profiles topic lets a brand-new consumer read from the beginning and end up with the current profile of every customer — a bootstrap mechanism, not a history.

Rule of thumb: if replaying the topic from the start should give you everything that happened, use delete retention. If it should give you how things are now, use compaction. If you want both, you want two topics.

Compaction's details are genuinely surprising — it is asynchronous, best-effort, and does not guarantee a single record per key at any given moment. Chapter 9 covers that properly, because several compliance and correctness mistakes depend on believing otherwise.

So what did we actually gain?

A vocabulary where each term is a decision rather than a fact. Key means ordering scope. Partition count means parallelism ceiling and future repartitioning pain. Retention means replay window. Cleanup policy means "history" or "current state."

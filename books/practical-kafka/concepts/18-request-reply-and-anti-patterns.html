Chapter 18

Request/Reply, Work Queues, and Anti-Patterns

Kafka is very good at a specific shape of problem. This chapter is about the shapes it is not good at, and how to recognise one before it becomes your architecture.

Asynchronous commands: when it works

Kafka is a reasonable transport for commands and long-running workflows, despite the contract advice in Chapter 1 being written for facts. It works when:

  • The work is genuinely asynchronous — the caller does not block.
  • Ordering per entity matters, and the natural key gives it to you.
  • Durability matters: the command must survive a consumer restart.
  • The rate is high enough that Kafka's throughput is the point.

A document-processing pipeline, a batch of notifications to render, a workflow moving through stages — these fit. Keep the command topic separate from your event topics, keep its schema owned by the consuming service (it is their interface, unlike a fact), and give it a tighter retention: an unprocessed command from three weeks ago is usually not something you want executed.

Request/reply: mostly a mistake

The pattern: send a request to a topic with a correlation id and a reply-to topic, then block waiting for a response to appear. It can be made to work and it is usually the wrong tool.

The costs are real and mostly non-obvious. The caller now depends on consumer group health for a synchronous operation, so a rebalance (Chapter 7) becomes latency on a user-facing request. Reply topics need partitioning that routes back to the right caller instance, which is awkward when instances scale. Timeouts are your problem entirely: nothing tells you the request failed, only that no reply arrived. And a duplicate request produces a duplicate reply, so both sides need idempotency.

If the caller waits for the answer, use HTTP or gRPC. Kafka's advantages — durability, replay, multiple independent consumers — are all irrelevant to a request that one caller is blocking on and will abandon in two seconds.

Work queues and share groups

The classic work-queue need is competing consumers on one logical stream, scaling past the partition count, with per-message acknowledgement. Chapter 2's model does not offer that: a partition goes to one consumer in a group, so parallelism is capped by partitions.

This is what share groups (KIP-932) address. They add queue-like cooperative consumption over Kafka topics — roughly a durable shared subscription — rather than adding a queue primitive to Kafka. Several consumers in a share group can read from the same partition, with per-record acknowledgement, so consumer count is no longer tied to partition count.

Know the maturity before you plan around it. Share groups shipped as early access in Kafka 4.0 — explicitly not for production clusters — as a preview in 4.1, and reached general availability around the 4.2 timeframe. Check what your cluster actually runs rather than what the blog posts describe.

What share groups change, and what they do not

They remove the partition-count ceiling on consumer parallelism for queue-shaped work. They do not give you global ordering, priority queues, or arbitrary message selection — and using them means giving up the per-partition ordering that consumer groups provide. Pick per workload, not per cluster.

Four anti-patterns

1. The giant shared event bus. One enterprise.events topic that every domain publishes to. It seems like decoupling; it is the opposite. Compatibility policy becomes meaningless (whose schema?), ownership dissolves, ACLs cannot express anything useful, and every consumer filters through ninety percent of records it does not want. Topics are cheap. Use one per event type or per closely-related family, owned by one team.

2. Database-table mirror topics. A topic per table, with no domain meaning, published by CDC because it was easy (Chapter 14). Consumers end up reimplementing your business logic from your schema, and every migration you make is a breaking change for them. If you must mirror tables, treat those topics as internal to the owning team and publish real domain events from them.

3. Kafka as a database. Infinite retention plus "we can always replay" is not a query layer. There is no index, no random access, no query language — reading a specific record means scanning. Build a materialized view (Chapter 15) and query that. Kafka is the log of record; it is not the read model.

4. Kafka as an RPC transport. Covered above, but it is worth naming as its own anti-pattern because it arrives with a plausible justification: "everything goes through Kafka, so we have one integration mechanism." A single mechanism that is wrong for half the cases is not simplicity.

When Kafka is a poor fit, full stop

The "global ordering" row deserves a second look whenever it comes up. Genuine global ordering requirements are rare; usually what is needed is ordering per entity, which Kafka gives you for the price of choosing the right key.

So what did we actually gain?

A clearer boundary. Kafka is excellent at durable, ordered-per-key, replayable, multi-consumer streams of facts. It is mediocre at request/reply, at queues (improving), and at being a database. Knowing that is what keeps an event platform from slowly absorbing every integration problem in the organisation and doing several of them badly.

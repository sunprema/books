Chapter 25

Case Study: Production Incident Walkthrough

A Tuesday afternoon, a schema change, and four hours. Every decision in this chapter is one the book has already argued about in the abstract.

14:02 — the deploy

The orders team ships a change to OrderPlaced. A field that was ["null","string"] becomes a required string: the value is always populated now, so making it required "documents reality better."

The subject runs FORWARD compatibility, set two years ago when this topic's consumers were external and upgraded after the producer. The check asks: can a consumer on the latest registered schema read data written with the new one? A consumer whose schema has the field as nullable can read a value that is always present. The check passes. Registration succeeds, the producer starts, records flow.

Nothing malfunctioned

The registry did exactly what it was configured to do. The configuration was wrong for how this topic is now used — consumers here are internal and are not upgraded first. The policy was never revisited when the consumer population changed (Chapter 13).

14:17 — the first symptom

Fulfillment's consumer lag alert fires. Time-behind is climbing steadily. The on-call engineer looks at the dashboard and finds the combination from Chapter 20:

  • Lag: climbing linearly, all partitions
  • Throughput: zero
  • Error rate: constant, non-zero
  • Rebalance rate: zero
  • Process health: fine. No restarts, no OOM, CPU normal

That is the poison pill signature (Chapter 7). Confirmed in ten seconds by checking the group's committed offset over time: perfectly static. The consumer is polling the same batch, failing, and re-polling it.

14:21 — the actual error

The consumer logs show an Avro resolution error: a field present in the reader's schema, absent from the writer's, with no default.

Which is Chapter 3's rule, in production. Fulfillment's consumer was deployed before the change with the field as required in its own reader schema — and now, reading with resolution against the new writer schema, it hits a field it must supply and cannot.

Two other consumers are unaffected — their reader schemas happen to tolerate it. This is why the alert came from one team and why the first fifteen minutes were spent looking at fulfillment's own code.

14:28 — the decision

Three options on the table.

Options at 14:28
OptionTime to reliefProblem
Roll back the producer~10 minDoes not fix it — the bad records are already in the topic
Skip past the poison records~5 minSilently drops real orders
Fix forward: update the consumer schema~35 minLongest, and it is correct

The first option is the instinctive one and it is a trap worth naming, because it is chosen under pressure surprisingly often. Rolling back the producer stops new unreadable records, but the records written between 14:02 and now are in the log and immutable. The consumer is stuck on those. Rollback alone changes nothing about the outage.

The second restores throughput in minutes by advancing the group past the failing offsets — and discards real customer orders that nothing else will ever process. It converts a visible outage into invisible data loss, which is worse.

They choose to fix forward, and they roll back the producer as well — not to fix the outage, but to stop adding to the affected range while the fix is prepared.

14:55 — recovery

Fulfillment ships a consumer with the field nullable and a default. It restarts, resolution succeeds on the stuck records, and it begins catching up. Lag peaks around 14:58 and drains over the next forty minutes. No records were lost, because nothing was ever skipped — the log kept them safe the entire time, which is the property the whole architecture is built on.

By 15:40 lag is normal. The orders team re-deploys their change, this time as an additive one.

16:10 — reconciliation

Before closing, they verify nothing was lost: count orders created in the window against orders that reached a fulfilled state (Chapter 14's reconciliation job, run on demand). The numbers match. That check is what turns "we think it recovered" into "it recovered," and it takes minutes when the job already exists.

The post-incident controls

Five actions, each pointing at a chapter:

  1. CI compatibility checks on every schema change, with the registry's own explanation printed into the pull request. The change would have been discussed at review rather than discovered at 14:17 (Chapter 13).
  2. Review the compatibility policy per subject. This topic's consumers are internal and are not upgraded first, so FORWARD is wrong for it. Move to FULL_TRANSITIVE — replay must work and the deployment order is not guaranteed (Chapter 11).
  3. Dead-letter routing on every consumer. A record that cannot be deserialized should leave the main path rather than block it. Lag would have stayed flat and this would have been a ticket, not an incident (Chapter 15).
  4. Alert on the signature, not just on lag. "Lag climbing while throughput is zero" is a specific, high-confidence poison-pill alert that fires within a minute (Chapter 20).
  5. Record schema ownership and the consumer inventory, so a producer team can see who consumes their topic before changing it (Chapter 13).

The one-sentence lesson

Every control that would have prevented this is cheap, and every one of them looks like unnecessary process until the afternoon it does not. The compatibility policy in particular is not set-and-forget: it encodes an assumption about deployment order that quietly expires when your consumer population changes.

So what did we actually gain?

A worked example of the book's central claim. The incident was not caused by Kafka, or by Avro, or by a bug in anyone's code. It was caused by a contract decision — a compatibility policy — that was correct when it was made and had not been revisited when the facts changed.

That is what event contracts are: decisions with a long half-life, made by people who will not be in the room when they are tested. Partitions determine ordering scope. Keys determine hot spots. Defaults determine whether a field can ever be added. Compatibility policy determines who can break whom.

None of it is exotic. All of it is permanent. Design accordingly — and see the cheatsheet for the checklists worth keeping close.

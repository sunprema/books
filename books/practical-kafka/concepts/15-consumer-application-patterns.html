Chapter 15

Consumer Application Patterns

A consumer's job is not to process every record. It is to make progress in the presence of records it cannot process — which is a different, harder job.

Classify the failure first

Almost every consumer pathology comes from treating all exceptions the same way. Four categories, four responses:

Failure classification drives the retry decision
ClassExampleWill it ever succeed?Response
TransientDatabase timeout, 503 from a dependencyYes, laterRetry with backoff
PermanentDeserialization failure, unknown schemaNeverDead-letter immediately
MalformedRequired field null, impossible valuesNeverDead-letter immediately
Business ruleOrder references an unknown customerMaybe — ordering or data issueBounded retry, then dead-letter

The expensive mistake is retrying a permanent failure. That is the poison pill from Chapter 7: an uncaught exception means the same batch is re-consumed on the next poll, forever, while lag climbs and the process looks healthy.

The other expensive mistake is dead-lettering a transient failure. A ten-minute database blip should not send ten thousand valid records to a dead-letter topic that someone must now replay by hand.

The default that is usually right

Retry with backoff a bounded number of times; if it still fails, route the record to a dead-letter topic with the failure reason attached, commit the offset, and continue. Progress is preserved, the failure is captured, and no single record can stop the stream.

Where to retry

Retrying in-line on the consumer thread is the obvious approach and it has a hard limit: exceed max.poll.interval.ms and the broker evicts you, triggering a rebalance (Chapter 7). Three retries with exponential backoff can easily blow through a five-minute interval during a dependency outage — and then you have converted a slow dependency into a rebalance storm across the whole group.

So in-line retry is for fast retries only: a handful of attempts over a few seconds. Anything longer needs to leave the poll loop, and the standard mechanism is a retry topic.

Each retry topic has its own consumer that waits before processing. Because that waiting happens on a separate consumer group with its own poll budget, the main stream keeps flowing at full speed. The cost is that retried records are now out of order relative to the main stream — which is fine for independent records and unacceptable for an aggregate's lifecycle. For ordered work, blocking the partition is sometimes the correct behaviour, and you should choose it deliberately.

Dead-letter topics

A dead-letter queue in Kafka is simply another Kafka topic that failed records get routed to. There is no special broker feature — which means everything about its usefulness is your design.

Make it useful by attaching context in headers: the original topic, partition and offset; the exception class and message; the consumer group; a timestamp; and the attempt count. A dead-letter record without that context is an unreadable blob that nobody can act on.

A dead-letter topic nobody monitors is data loss with extra steps

Alert on the record count. Assign an owner. Build the replay tool before you need it — during an incident is the wrong moment to discover that replaying from the DLQ means writing a script.

Idempotent processing

At-least-once is the practical default (Chapter 7), and reliable publication is at-least-once too (Chapter 14). So duplicates are normal traffic, not an exceptional condition, and every consumer with an external side effect needs to be safe against them.

In rough order of preference:

  • Make the write naturally idempotent. An upsert keyed by the business identity, or a state transition that is a no-op when already applied. Nothing to store, nothing to expire, no extra failure mode. This is by far the best answer when the domain allows it.
  • Use a conditional write. "Set status to SHIPPED where status is PACKED" — a duplicate updates zero rows and that is the correct outcome.
  • Keep a processed-event store. Record each processed event id and skip ones you have seen. Store the id in the same transaction as the side effect, or you have reinvented the dual-write problem inside your consumer.
  • Pass an idempotency key to the external system. Payment and messaging providers generally accept one, because this problem is universal.

A processed-event store needs a retention policy of its own, and it must be longer than your maximum replay window — otherwise a replay after the store has expired reprocesses everything. This is the detail that gets missed.

Replay-safe consumers

Sooner or later you will reset a consumer group to an earlier offset — to fix a bug, backfill a new field, or rebuild state. That means reprocessing records the consumer has already handled, at maximum speed, with no rate limiting.

Which raises questions worth answering before the day you need to: Will it re-send ten thousand emails? Will it double every balance? Will it overwhelm a downstream API sized for steady-state traffic?

The design responses are: idempotency (above), a distinction between replayable side effects (database writes) and non-replayable ones (emails, payments), and a way to run the consumer in a mode where non-replayable effects are suppressed. Isolating the two kinds of side effect into different consumers is the cleanest version of this — the projection consumer can be replayed freely, the notification consumer cannot.

Concurrency, backpressure, and partition awareness

A group's parallelism is capped by the partition count (Chapter 2). Adding consumers beyond that does nothing — the extras sit idle.

To go faster within that ceiling you can process records from a partition concurrently, but doing so breaks ordering within the partition, which is the guarantee you chose your key for. The compromise that works: parallelise by key within the partition. Records for different keys can be processed concurrently; records for the same key are serialised. You keep per-aggregate ordering while using more than one thread.

Backpressure comes for free from the poll loop if you respect it: do not read ahead into an unbounded internal queue. Bound the work in flight, and let max.poll.records and your processing rate be the throttle. An internal queue that grows without limit turns a downstream slowdown into an out-of-memory crash, and loses whatever was buffered.

Stateless, stateful, and materialized views

Three shapes, worth naming because they have different operational profiles:

  • Stateless — each record processed independently. Trivially scalable, trivially replayable. Aim for this.
  • Stateful — processing depends on prior records. Now you own state: where it lives, how it is restored after a crash, whether it is consistent with the committed offset. Kafka Streams exists largely to handle this for you (Chapter 16).
  • Materialized view — the consumer's whole purpose is to maintain a queryable projection. The projection is the state, it can always be rebuilt by replaying from the beginning, and that rebuildability is the main operational advantage: a corrupted view is fixed by replaying rather than by repairing.

So what did we actually gain?

A consumer that keeps making progress. Records that cannot be processed leave the main path, transient failures get another chance, duplicates are harmless, and a replay does not cause an incident.

Everything here is hand-built. The next chapter is what happens when you decide not to hand-build it.

Observability and Troubleshooting

Two metrics carry most of the diagnostic weight: consumer lag and under-replicated partitions. Nearly every incident announces itself in one of them first.

Consumer lag

Lag is the difference between the latest offset produced to a partition and the last offset committed by a consumer group. It is the single most load-bearing consumer metric because it answers the question that actually matters: is the consumer keeping up?

Measure it per group and per partition. Aggregate lag hides the case that matters most — one partition badly behind while the rest are fine, which is the signature of a hot key (Chapter 5) or of a single stuck consumer instance.

Alert on time, not on records

"Lag is 40,000 records" means nothing without a rate. Is that four seconds of work or four hours? Time-behind — how old the next unprocessed record is — is what the business cares about and what belongs in an SLO. Alert on trend and on time-behind; use record count for diagnosis.

Note the permissions dependency: lag monitoring requires Describe on both consumer groups and topics (Chapter 19). A monitoring tool reporting zero lag for a group it cannot see is a failure mode worth explicitly testing.

Under-replicated partitions

A URP is a partition where some replicas are not in sync with the leader. It is the primary durability health signal, and a non-zero steady-state value means your replication factor is nominal rather than real — you have fewer live copies than you think.

Brief spikes during a rolling restart are normal. Sustained URPs are not, and they matter more than they look: with RF 3 and minISR 2 (Chapter 6), a partition that has been under-replicated for an hour is one broker failure away from rejecting writes.

The one people forget is rebalance rate. It should be essentially zero in a healthy group. A group rebalancing every few minutes is burning most of its capacity on reassignment, and lag will be climbing for a reason nobody has connected to it yet.

Tracing through Kafka

Distributed tracing does not stop at the broker if you propagate trace context in record headers (Chapter 2). The producer injects the current context; the consumer extracts it and continues the trace. The result is a trace that spans the asynchronous boundary — the request that created the order, the consumer that fulfilled it, the sink that indexed it.

Headers rather than payload, so tracing needs no schema change and consumers that do not care pay nothing.

Six incidents and how to read them

Consumer lag is increasing. First distinguish "slower than production" from "not processing at all." Check throughput: if it is zero while the consumer is alive, suspect a poison pill (Chapter 7). If it is non-zero but insufficient, check whether all partitions are behind (genuine capacity problem — add consumers up to the partition count) or just one (hot key, or one sick instance). Check the rebalance rate before concluding anything.

A partition becomes hot. One partition's lag grows while others are flat. Almost always key skew (Chapter 5): a tenant, a product, a null-ish default value that dominates the distribution. Adding partitions does not help — that key still hashes to one of them. The fixes are a composite key, or special-casing the hot key, and both are contract changes to think through rather than emergency actions.

A producer times out. Look at whether the ISR has shrunk below min.insync.replicas — with acks=all the write is rejected, and the producer surfaces it as a timeout rather than as "your durability requirement cannot currently be met." Also check broker disk and request latency. This is the failure mode most often misread as a network problem.

Consumers repeatedly fail on one record. The poison pill signature: lag climbs, throughput is zero, error rate is constant, nothing crashes. Confirm by comparing the group's committed offset over time — it will be perfectly static. Route the record to a dead-letter topic (Chapter 15); Chapter 25 walks through the whole incident.

Schema registration or deserialization fails. Registration failures mean someone attempted an incompatible change — the system working correctly, and a CI gap (Chapter 13). Deserialization failures on the consumer side mean incompatible data is already in the topic, which is considerably worse: the bytes are written and every consumer will hit them.

Replication is degraded. Sustained URPs. Check which brokers host the lagging replicas — if it is always the same one, suspect that broker's disk or network rather than the cluster. Watch ISR shrink/expand rate: replicas flapping in and out is a different problem from a replica that is simply down, and it is usually resource pressure.

Diagnostic habit worth building: before changing anything, write down which metric you expect to move and by how much. It converts "we restarted it and it got better" into knowledge.

What to actually alert on

Alert fatigue kills observability faster than missing metrics do. A defensible starting set:

  • Page: under-replicated partitions sustained beyond a few minutes; active controller count not equal to 1; a critical consumer group's time-behind exceeding its SLO; producer error rate above zero on a critical topic.
  • Ticket: rebalance rate elevated; dead-letter topic receiving records; registry compatibility failures; disk growth trending toward capacity.
  • Dashboard only: everything else, including absolute lag numbers.

So what did we actually gain?

Two numbers that tell you whether the system is healthy, and a mapping from symptom to cause for the incidents that actually recur. The recurring theme is that Kafka's failures are usually quiet — a stuck consumer looks healthy, a degraded replica set serves traffic normally — so the metrics are not a nicety. They are the only way you find out.

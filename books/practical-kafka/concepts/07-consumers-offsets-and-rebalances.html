Chapter 07

Consumers, Offsets, and Rebalances

Where you commit the offset relative to where you do the work is the entire difference between at-most-once and at-least-once. Everything else is tuning.

The poll loop is a liveness contract

A consumer calls poll() in a loop. Each call fetches records and, invisibly, tells the group coordinator that this consumer is alive and making progress. Those two jobs share one method, and that is the source of most consumer trouble.

If your processing takes longer than max.poll.interval.ms, the broker assumes the consumer has crashed and evicts it from the group, triggering a rebalance. The consumer is not crashed. It is doing exactly what you told it to. But it did not come back to poll() in time, so from the coordinator's point of view it is indistinguishable from a dead process.

The result is a loop that is very hard to diagnose from the outside: process slowly → get evicted → partitions reassigned → new owner starts from the last commit → processes the same records slowly → gets evicted. Lag climbs steadily while every consumer looks busy and healthy.

The number one cause of "random rebalances"

It is almost never a network problem. It is processing time exceeding max.poll.interval.ms. Either reduce max.poll.records so a batch takes less time, move slow work off the poll thread, or raise the interval to match reality — in that order of preference.

Committing offsets

A committed offset says "this group has processed everything before this point on this partition." When a partition moves to another consumer, the new owner starts from that offset. So the commit is a durability decision about work, and its placement decides your delivery semantics.

Auto-commit periodically commits in the background, on a timer, whether or not you finished processing what you polled. It is convenient and it quietly gives you neither semantic reliably — offsets can advance past records that failed. It is fine for a consumer where losing a record is genuinely acceptable, and wrong almost everywhere else.

Manual synchronous commit after processing gives you clean at-least-once: you commit only what you have completed, and you find out if the commit failed. It costs a round trip per commit, so commit per batch rather than per record.

Manual asynchronous commit avoids that round-trip stall at the cost of not knowing immediately whether the commit landed. The standard pattern is async commits during steady state and one synchronous commit on shutdown and on partition revocation.

Design consequence

At-least-once is the practical default, which means duplicate delivery is not an edge case — it is the normal operating mode. Every consumer with an external side effect needs an idempotency key. This is Chapter 15, and it is not optional.

Rebalancing, and how it got better

When membership changes, partitions must be redistributed. Historically this was a stop-the-world event: every consumer revoked everything it held, then everyone rejoined and received a fresh assignment. A group of forty consumers paused entirely because one instance restarted.

Three improvements arrived, and knowing which one you are running matters:

  • Incremental cooperative rebalancing (Kafka 2.4). The CooperativeStickyAssignor splits the rebalance into smaller steps and lets consumers keep processing partitions that are not being reassigned. Most partitions never move at all.
  • Static membership (KIP-345). Giving each consumer a stable group.instance.id lets Kafka preserve its partition assignments across a restart within the session timeout. A rolling deploy stops reshuffling the group entirely — the instance leaves and comes back to the same partitions.
  • The next-generation protocol (KIP-848), generally available in Kafka 4.0. The broker computes assignments instead of delegating to a group leader, and a joining consumer receives partitions incrementally rather than waiting for everyone to revoke and rejoin. It is enabled by default on the broker side, but consumers must opt in with group.protocol=consumer.

If you run Kafka 4.x and have not set group.protocol=consumer, you are still on the old protocol. The broker default does not migrate your clients for you.

Static membership and cooperative rebalancing address different problems and compose well: static membership stops rebalances from happening on planned restarts; cooperative rebalancing makes the ones that do happen cheaper.

Poison messages

A poison pill is a record that will never process successfully — a malformed payload, a schema the consumer cannot resolve, a business invariant that can never be satisfied. If processing throws an uncaught exception on the consumer thread, the consumer re-consumes the same batch on the next poll. Forever.

The symptom is distinctive and worth recognising on sight: lag grows steadily, throughput is zero, error rate is constant, and the consumer never crashes. It is perfectly healthy and perfectly stuck.

The fix is to classify failures rather than retry them uniformly. A transient dependency error deserves a retry; a deserialization failure never will succeed and must be routed out of the main path to a dead-letter topic. Chapter 15 builds this properly, and Chapter 25 walks through it happening in production.

A note on auto.offset.reset

When a group has no committed offset — a brand-new consumer, or one whose offsets aged out — auto.offset.reset decides where it starts: earliest (replay everything retained) or latest (only new records). The default is latest.

This setting is a loaded weapon in both directions. A new consumer that silently starts at latest misses the backlog someone assumed it would process. A consumer that falls behind retention and resets to earliest suddenly reprocesses months of history at full speed, usually into a downstream system that is not expecting it. Set it deliberately per consumer, and alert when a reset actually occurs.

So what did we actually gain?

The knowledge that duplicates are the default, that rebalances are usually self-inflicted through processing time, and that a stuck consumer looks healthy. All three shape how you write consumers in Part IV.

Which raises the obvious question: Kafka advertises exactly-once semantics. What exactly does that buy, and where does it stop? Next chapter — and the answer is narrower than the marketing suggests.

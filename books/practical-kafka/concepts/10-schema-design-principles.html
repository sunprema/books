Practical Kafka Part III · Schemas That Survive Change

Chapter 10

Schema Design Principles

Most unmaintainable event schemas were maintainable on day one. They became unmaintainable because they were modelled on something that was always going to change.

Model business facts, not tables

The single highest-leverage decision in this chapter: an event should describe something that happened in the business, in terms the business would recognise, using identifiers the business owns.

The tempting alternative is to publish your database rows. It is easy — CDC will do it for you (Chapter 14) — and it is a trap, because it welds your event contract to a schema you fully intend to refactor. Split the orders table in two next quarter and you have made a breaking change to an interface that a dozen teams depend on, for reasons that have nothing to do with them.

The same change, under two modelling choices
You doTable-shaped eventFact-shaped event
Rename a columnBreaking changeInvisible
Split a tableBreaking changeInvisible
Add an indexInvisibleInvisible
Change a business ruleSilent semantic driftDeliberate contract change

The last row is the interesting one. Fact-shaped events do not make change free — they make it visible. A change to what "placed" means should be a conversation. A change to which table it lives in should not.

Naming, namespaces, and why it is operational

Avro records carry a name and a namespace which together form the fullname. Under RecordNameStrategy or TopicRecordNameStrategy, that fullname becomes the registry subject (Chapter 4). Naming is therefore not a style question — it determines the scope of your compatibility checks.

A workable convention:

  • Namespace by domain ownership: com.acme.orders, com.acme.payments. The namespace should say who owns the contract, because that is who must approve changes to it.
  • Name the event in the past tense: OrderPlaced, PaymentAuthorized, OrderCancelled.
  • Field names in one consistent case, chosen once for the whole platform. Mixed customer_id and customerId across topics is a permanent tax on every consumer.
  • Do not put the version in the record name unless you have decided that a new version means a genuinely new type (Chapter 12). Otherwise you will have OrderPlacedV2 and schema version 4 simultaneously, and no one will know which is authoritative.

Envelope versus payload

Chapter 1 listed what every event carries: event id, occurred-at, correlation and causation ids, actor, tenant. There are two ways to express that, and both are defensible.

A nested envelope — a shared EventMetadata record referenced by every event schema, with the domain payload as a field. Consistency is enforced by the type system, and changes to the envelope happen in one place. The cost is a schema reference dependency graph and slightly more awkward consumer code.

Flat fields — the metadata fields are simply present in every record. Simpler to read and to consume; consistency depends on review discipline rather than on the schema.

Pick one and apply it everywhere. The genuinely bad outcome is half your topics with an envelope and half without, so no generic consumer, no tracing library, and no audit tool can be written once.

Identifiers

Identifiers are the part of a contract that is hardest to change, because they are what everything else joins on. Three guidelines:

  • Prefer opaque, stable ids — a UUID as a string with the uuid logical type, or your domain's own canonical identifier. Avoid ids that carry meaning (ORD-2026-UK-0001); the meaning changes and the id cannot.
  • Avoid database surrogate keys where you can. A sequence number from your orders table is a promise that you will never migrate that table. If you must publish one, publish it alongside a business identifier rather than instead of it.
  • Model composite keys explicitly as separate fields rather than a concatenated string. Consumers that need only one part should not have to parse your delimiter — and one day a value will contain the delimiter.

Time

Avro's logical types make time precision an explicit contract decision: date, time-millis, time-micros, timestamp-millis, timestamp-micros, and local-timestamp variants.

Choose timestamp-micros by default. Going from millis to micros later is a type change, not an additive one, and type changes are the awkward category (Chapter 11). Microseconds cost you nothing today and buy you the option.

Distinguish the timestamps rather than having one timestamp field whose meaning nobody agrees on:

  • occurredAt — when the fact happened in the business. This is what windowing and event-time processing should use (Chapter 16).
  • recordedAt — when the system observed it. Useful for lag analysis and for detecting late-arriving data.
  • The Kafka record timestamp is a third thing again, set by the producer or by log-append time, and it drives retention. Do not rely on it carrying business meaning.

Money and decimals

Use the decimal logical type, and remember from Chapter 3 that for schema resolution two decimals match only if both precision and scale match. You are fixing your money precision at design time, across the whole life of the topic.

Pick a scale that covers the currencies you might plausibly handle, not just the ones you handle now — scale 2 is wrong the day someone adds a currency with three decimal places, or a unit price that needs four. And always pair an amount with its currency code in the same record. An amount without a currency is not a monetary value; it is a number that will eventually be interpreted wrongly.

Documentation that cannot drift

Avro's doc is part of the schema and travels into the registry with it. Use it for the things a field name cannot say: units, inclusivity, nullability semantics, and what the value means when it is absent. "Amount excluding tax, in the order's currency" belongs in the contract, not in a wiki that will be stale in a year.

PII is a schema-design concern

Once a field carrying personal data is published to a topic, it flows to every consumer and every sink — a warehouse, a search index, a partner integration — and each of those inherits an obligation nobody recorded. Chapter 22 covers the lifecycle consequences; the design-time moves are these:

  • Minimise. Publish the customer id, not the customer's name and address, unless a consumer genuinely needs them. Reference data can be looked up; leaked data cannot be recalled.
  • Classify in the schema. Mark sensitive fields in doc, or with a custom schema property, so tooling can find them. An unmarked PII field is invisible to every downstream control.
  • Isolate by topic. A topic is the unit of authorization (Chapter 19), so sensitive fields on their own topic is the only isolation that is actually enforceable.
  • Consider tokenising at the source, so the event carries a reference rather than the value. Then deleting the token's mapping is a real erasure mechanism — unlike compaction (Chapter 9).

So what did we actually gain?

A schema whose stable parts are the ones the business considers stable. That alignment is what makes the next chapter's evolution rules feel generous rather than restrictive: most of the changes you actually need turn out to be additive, provided you did not model a database.

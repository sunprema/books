Chapter 13

Enterprise Contract Governance

Governance that lives in a document is a suggestion. Governance that lives in CI and in registry permissions is a system. The difference shows up during the incident.

Move the check left

A compatibility violation can be discovered at three moments, and the cost differs by orders of magnitude.

Where you find out
Discovered atBlast radiusCost
Pull request (CI check)One branchMinutes
Deploy (registration fails)One service, failing to startHours, plus a rollback
Runtime (consumer cannot deserialize)Every consumer of the topicAn incident (Chapter 25)

Compatibility can be checked in CI before registration, which turns a runtime failure into a pull-request failure. This is the single highest-value governance control, and it is roughly a day of work: on every change to a schema file, check the proposed schema against the registered versions for its subject under that subject's policy, and fail the build if it is incompatible.

The shape of the CI gate

# for each changed .avsc, ask the registry whether it would be accepted
# under the subject's configured compatibility level — before merging
POST /compatibility/subjects/{subject}/versions/latest
     ?verbose=true
Body: { "schema": "<the proposed schema>" }

# non-compatible => fail the build, print the reason in the PR

Print the registry's own explanation into the pull request. A reviewer who sees "adding a required field is incompatible with BACKWARD" learns the rule; a reviewer who sees "build failed" learns to rerun the build.

Do not rely on clients behaving

CI covers changes that go through CI. It does nothing about a service with a hand-rolled serializer, a console producer used during an incident, or a well-meaning integration written by another department.

Broker-side schema validation closes that gap: brokers can reject records whose schema id is not registered for the topic. This moves the check off the client's honour system entirely — a producer cannot put bytes into a governed topic that registry-aware consumers will be unable to read. Enable it on topics that matter.

Two controls, two threat models

CI checks stop well-intentioned breaking changes. Broker-side validation stops bypasses. You need both, because they fail independently and the second one is what saves you at 2am when someone reaches for a console producer.

Ownership needs permissions behind it

Chapter 1 separated three questions: who may write to the topic, who may evolve the schema, who sets the policy. Registry authorization is what makes the second one real — restricting who may register or evolve a subject is what turns an ownership model from a wiki table into something the system enforces.

A workable arrangement:

  • The owning team may register new versions of its own subjects.
  • Everyone may read every subject. Discoverability is a feature; hiding schemas produces reimplementation, not security.
  • Only the platform team may change a subject's compatibility level, or set it to NONE. Lowering the policy is the dangerous operation, not the schema change itself.
  • Shared referenced types (Chapter 4) are owned centrally and run at a stricter level than leaf schemas, because their blast radius is the dependency graph.

Compatibility by criticality

Because compatibility is configured per subject, you can tier it — and you should, because uniform policy either strangles low-stakes topics or under-protects critical ones.

A three-tier policy that scales
TierLevelReviewExample
Regulated / externalFULL_TRANSITIVEOwner + platform + data governancePayment events, partner feeds
Core domainBACKWARD_TRANSITIVEOwner + one reviewer outside the teamOrder lifecycle, customer state
Internal / analyticsBACKWARDOwnerTelemetry, internal metrics

The transitive levels are the meaningful upgrade for anything you expect to replay from the beginning. Non-transitive checks only against the latest version, and a chain of individually-legal changes can still leave your oldest data unreadable (Chapter 11).

Topic naming and domain ownership

A naming convention's job is to make ownership obvious from the name alone. Something like <domain>.<entity>.<event> — orders.order.placed, payments.payment.authorized.

Two properties matter more than the exact format. First, the domain prefix must map to a team, so an unfamiliar topic can be routed to an owner without asking around. Second, the convention should be enforced at creation — a topic-creation pipeline that rejects non-conforming names, rather than a document nobody reads. Conventions that are not enforced decay within two quarters.

Documentation and discoverability

Consumers you do not know about are the recurring theme of this book. A catalogue reduces the problem: for each topic, its owner, its purpose, its key and ordering scope, its retention and cleanup policy, its classification, its compatibility tier, and its schema with doc strings.

Most of that can be generated. The registry holds the schemas and the docs; the broker holds the topic configuration; ACLs list the principals. A generated catalogue stays true, and a hand-maintained one does not — which is the whole argument for putting semantics in doc (Chapter 10) rather than in a separate document.

Deprecation and consumer impact

To deprecate anything you must know who consumes it. Kafka will tell you: consumer group listings show which groups have committed offsets on a topic. That is not a complete picture — a group that reads without committing, or an occasional replay job, will not appear — but it is the factual starting point, and it beats an email asking who is using something.

A deprecation that works has four parts: an announced date, a named replacement, monitoring showing remaining consumers, and an owner accountable for the number reaching zero. Drop any one and you get "v1 forever" from Chapter 12.

Governance failure mode

Governance that only adds friction — reviews, approvals, checklists — with no corresponding tooling gets routed around. Every control in this chapter is automated: CI checks, broker validation, permissions, generated catalogues. If your governance is mostly meetings, teams will find the path that avoids the meeting, and you will discover the schema change in production.

The registry as an internal platform

The framing worth adopting: your schema registry is an internal developer platform, not a piece of infrastructure. It has users (every producing and consuming team), an API (registration and lookup), a policy surface (compatibility levels and permissions), and an availability obligation (Chapter 4's cold-client problem).

Teams that treat it that way — with an owner, a roadmap, documentation, and an on-call rotation — end up with contracts that hold. Teams that treat it as "the thing the Avro library talks to" end up with forty schema versions and no idea which consumers still work.

So what did we actually gain?

The end of Part III, and the end of the theory. You now have contracts, the mechanics that constrain them, the rules for evolving them, and the controls that keep the rules real.

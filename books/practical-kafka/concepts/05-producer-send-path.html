Chapter 05

What Happens When a Producer Sends a Record

send() returns almost immediately and almost nothing has happened yet. Understanding the gap between "returned" and "durable" is where producer correctness lives.

The path a record takes

Calling send() does not write to a broker. It runs a short client-side pipeline and then hands the record to a background thread. Six stages:

Serialize turns key and value into bytes — for us, the Avro serializer that registers the schema and writes the five-byte prefix from Chapter 4. Partition selects the target: hash of the key if there is one, otherwise a sticky round-robin. Accumulate puts the record into a per-partition batch in the producer's memory buffer. Compress applies to the whole batch, not per record — which is why batching and compression multiply each other.

Then the sender thread issues a produce request, the partition leader appends to its log, and followers replicate. Only at that point is anything durable, and only then does your Future complete.

acks: what you are waiting for

The acks setting decides what counts as "written."

  • acks=0 — fire and forget. The producer does not wait at all. Records are lost silently on any failure. Use for telemetry you would not pay to recover.
  • acks=1 — the leader has written to its log. If the leader fails before followers replicate, the record is gone. This is the setting that produces "we lost a few hundred events during the failover."
  • acks=all — the leader waits for the full set of in-sync replicas to acknowledge the record. This guarantees the record will not be lost as long as at least one in-sync replica remains alive.

Read that last guarantee carefully: it is conditional on the in-sync replica set, which is itself configurable. acks=all on a topic where the ISR has shrunk to one replica guarantees very little. That interaction is Chapter 6, and it is the single most misunderstood thing about Kafka durability.

Idempotence: retries without duplicates

Without idempotence, a retry after a broker failure may write a duplicate — the classic case being an append that succeeded but whose acknowledgement was lost, so the producer retries a record the broker already has.

The idempotent producer solves this by assigning each producer a session id and each record a sequence number per partition. The broker tracks the last sequence it accepted and discards a duplicate. It is enabled by default when no conflicting configuration is set, and it requires a specific combination:

producer.properties — the durability-first profile

# required together for idempotence
acks=all
enable.idempotence=true
max.in.flight.requests.per.connection=5   # must be <= 5
retries=2147483647                          # bounded by the timeout below

# throughput, without giving up the above
batch.size=65536
linger.ms=5
compression.type=lz4

# the real bound on "how long may a send take"
delivery.timeout.ms=120000

Idempotence requires acks=all, retries greater than zero, and max.in.flight.requests.per.connection of five or fewer — and ordering is preserved for any allowable value. That last clause is worth internalising: before idempotence, keeping order under retries meant limiting yourself to one in-flight request, at real throughput cost. With idempotence you keep ordering and pipelining.

Trap

Setting retries very high without thinking about delivery.timeout.ms. Retries are bounded by the delivery timeout, not by the retry count — so the real question is "how long may a record sit in the accumulator before I am told it failed?" Answer that, set the timeout, and let retries be effectively infinite within it.

Batching: linger and batch size

The producer sends a batch when either batch.size bytes have accumulated or linger.ms has elapsed — whichever comes first. Both defaults are tuned for latency; a durability-and-throughput profile typically raises the batch size well above the 16 KB default and sets a small non-zero linger.

The counter-intuitive part: adding a few milliseconds of linger often reduces end-to-end latency under load, because larger batches mean fewer requests, less per-request overhead, and better compression ratios. Latency-versus-throughput is a real trade-off at low volume and frequently a false one at high volume.

Keys, hashing, and hot partitions

The key does two jobs at once, and they can conflict. It defines ordering scope (Chapter 2) and it determines load distribution. A key with good semantics can have terrible distribution.

The canonical failure: keying by tenantId in a system where one tenant is forty percent of your volume. Ordering per tenant is exactly right; the distribution is a permanent hot partition that no amount of extra partitions will relieve, because that tenant's records all hash to one of them. The consumer owning that partition is the bottleneck for the whole topic.

When ordering scope and load distribution disagree, ordering usually wins — you can add capacity, but you cannot retrofit a guarantee. If the hot key is genuinely intolerable, composite keys (tenantId:orderId) narrow the ordering scope deliberately rather than accidentally.

So what did we actually gain?

A precise answer to "is my event safely published?" — which is a contract question, not a tuning question. A producer configured acks=1 is telling consumers, without saying so, that the event stream may quietly lose records during a failover. If your contract says "every accepted order appears exactly once on this topic," the producer configuration is part of that contract.

And since acks=all's guarantee is stated in terms of in-sync replicas, we need to know precisely what that set is and when it shrinks. Next chapter.

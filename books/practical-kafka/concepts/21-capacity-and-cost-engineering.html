Chapter 21

Capacity and Cost Engineering

Partition count is four decisions wearing one number's clothing. Storage is a multiplication most people get wrong by a factor of three.

Partition count is four decisions

Every time someone types --partitions N they are simultaneously deciding:

  1. Throughput — partitions are the unit of write and read parallelism.
  2. Consumer parallelism ceiling — a group can never usefully have more members than partitions (Chapter 2).
  3. Ordering scope — more partitions means the same key set is spread more finely; and repartitioning later breaks existing keys' ordering.
  4. Recovery time and metadata cost — every partition is files on disk, memory on the broker, and work during leader election. Recovery time after a broker failure scales with the partitions it led.

Because these pull in different directions, there is no formula that answers all four. What there is, is an asymmetry worth building a habit around.

The asymmetry that should drive your default

Adding partitions is easy. Removing them is effectively impossible, and adding them changes key routing — so ordering for existing keys breaks at that boundary. Start modest, measure, and grow deliberately. Over-partitioning "to be safe" is the expensive direction of the mistake.

Sizing that works

The approach that survives contact with production:

  1. Start modest — single digits to low double digits for most new topics.
  2. Measure per-partition throughput on your actual cluster, with your record sizes and your consumer's real processing cost. Published benchmark numbers are not your numbers.
  3. Divide your target peak by that measured figure.
  4. Plan for one to two years of peak, using the measured per-partition numbers.
  5. Round up to a number with good divisor properties — 12 rather than 11 — so partitions distribute evenly across a varying number of consumers. Avoid primes.

Step 5 matters more than it sounds. With 12 partitions a group can scale to 2, 3, 4, 6 or 12 members with perfectly even distribution. With 11, every configuration except 1 and 11 is lopsided, and one consumer does twice the work of another.

The consumer side has its own ceiling to check: your partition count must be at least the parallelism your slowest consumer group needs. If processing one record takes 50 ms and you need 2,000 records per second, you need 100 concurrent processors — and therefore at least 100 partitions, unless you parallelise by key within a partition (Chapter 15).

Storage: the multiplication

The formula is simple and routinely misapplied:

A worked example, because the numbers are more persuasive than the formula. 2 KB records, 5,000 per second, 7 days retention, RF 3:

  • Raw per day: 2 KB × 5,000/s × 86,400 s ≈ 864 GB/day
  • Over 7 days: ≈ 6.0 TB
  • × RF 3: ≈ 18.1 TB of actual disk
  • ÷ 3 for lz4 on typical structured data: ≈ 6.0 TB

Then add headroom: you cannot run disks at 100%, retention is enforced lazily by segment (Chapter 9), and you want to survive a broker being down while its partitions' data lives elsewhere. Sizing at 60–70% of capacity is realistic.

Do not forget the topics you did not create: Kafka Streams changelog and repartition topics (Chapter 16), Connect's internal offset topics (Chapter 17), retry and dead-letter topics (Chapter 15). On a mature platform these are routinely a quarter of total storage.

Network: replication and fan-out

Two multipliers on your produce rate, and both are easy to miss in a capacity model:

Replication. Every byte produced is transferred to each follower. RF 3 means roughly 2× the produce rate in inter-broker traffic, on top of the produce traffic itself.

Consumer fan-out. Every consumer group reads every record. Ten groups on a topic means 10× the produce rate in egress. This is the term that surprises people on managed platforms where cross-zone egress is billed — a popular topic can cost more to read than to write, by a wide margin.

A cost the architecture creates

"Anyone can consume any topic" is a good architectural property and a real line item. Before adding the eleventh consumer group to a high-volume topic, check whether it needs the raw stream or whether a filtered or aggregated downstream topic would serve — one consumer of the firehose, ten of the summary.

Retention as a cost decision

Retention is usually chosen once, generously, and never revisited. It is a direct multiplier on storage, so it deserves the same scrutiny as partition count.

Ask what the retention is actually for (Chapter 9): consumer recovery, new-consumer bootstrap, or compliance. Those want very different numbers, and the honest answer is often "7 days for recovery, and the bootstrap case should be served by a compacted topic instead." Long retention adopted for replay convenience also becomes a compliance liability (Chapter 22) — a cost that does not show up on the infrastructure bill.

The compacted-topic risk is the subtler one: it grows with the number of distinct keys that have ever existed, which is unbounded if your key space is unbounded. A compacted topic keyed by session id will grow forever. That is what compact,delete (Chapter 9) is for.

So what did we actually gain?

Numbers you can defend, and an understanding of which ones are reversible. Partition count is nearly permanent, so be conservative. Retention is a dial you can turn, so revisit it. Consumer fan-out is a consequence of your architecture, so make it visible before it appears on an invoice.

Chapter 12

Versioning Strategies

Three tools, in increasing order of cost: evolve the schema, introduce a new event type, or create a new topic. Most pain comes from using the first where the third was needed.

The ladder

Every contract change lands on one of three rungs. Work down the ladder, and stop at the first rung that honestly fits.

Rung 1: when evolution is enough

Evolution is enough when the change is additive under your compatibility policy and — this is the part people skip — the meaning of every existing field is unchanged.

Adding promotionCode to OrderPlaced is a rung-1 change. Existing consumers ignore it, replay works, nobody deploys anything. This should be the overwhelming majority of your changes; if it is not, revisit Chapter 10, because your schemas are probably modelling something unstable.

Rung 2: when you need a new event type

Sometimes the thing you want to publish is not a richer version of the old event — it is a different fact. The signal is that you find yourself adding fields that are only meaningful when other fields have particular values, or making previously-required fields nullable so the same record can serve two purposes.

If OrderPlaced must now cover subscription orders that have no delivery address, no single line total, and a renewal schedule instead — that is SubscriptionStarted, not OrderPlaced with six new nullable fields.

The nullable-field smell

Every field you make nullable to accommodate a new case is a field every consumer must now branch on. Three of those and your event has become a union type wearing a record's clothing, and every consumer contains the same undocumented dispatch logic.

Mechanically, a new event type on the same topic needs a subject naming strategy that permits it — RecordNameStrategy or TopicRecordNameStrategy (Chapter 4), since the default TopicNameStrategy implicitly requires one schema per topic. Keeping it on the same topic preserves ordering with the related events, which is usually why you would want it there.

Rung 3: when you need a new topic

A new topic is the answer when the change is genuinely incompatible and you cannot upgrade all consumers at once. Typically:

  • The partition key must change — meaning ordering scope changes, which no schema mechanism can express.
  • A required field's type or meaning must change.
  • The retention or cleanup policy must change (a stream becoming a changelog).
  • The topic's ownership is moving to another domain.

The migration is then a project with a shape:

  1. Parallel publish. The producer writes both the old and the new topic. Costly in storage, and the only approach that lets consumers move independently.
  2. Consumer migration. Each consumer moves when it is ready. This is the long phase, and it needs a deadline attached at the start rather than negotiated at the end.
  3. Deprecation. Announce, monitor for remaining consumers, then stop publishing the old topic.
  4. Removal. Delete it, once retention has expired and nothing has read it for a full replay window.

Version-in-name versus schema evolution

Should topics be called customer.created.v1? There are two coherent positions, and the incoherent middle is where most platforms end up.

The version-in-name convention makes major versions visible and migrations explicit, at the cost of a namespace full of .v1 topics that will never become .v2. The stable name convention is cleaner and puts pressure on teams to squeeze breaking changes into evolution — which is exactly the pressure that produces six nullable fields.

Either works if you commit. What does not work is orders.placed.v1 that is also on schema version 7 with two semantic redefinitions in its history, because now there are two version axes and neither tells the truth.

The two failure modes

"v1 forever." The new topic exists, a few consumers moved, and the old one is still there three years later because nobody owns the migration. The fix is procedural, not technical: a new version ships with a deprecation date for the old one, agreed before the migration starts, and the date is tracked somewhere a human looks at.

Schema version explosion. Forty registered versions on one subject, each a small additive change, none removable, and nobody can say what the earliest still-readable version is. This is usually a sign that schema changes are too easy — no review, no cost — and Chapter 13 is the answer.

A useful forcing question at design review: "What is the deprecation date for the thing this replaces?" If there is no answer, you are not doing a migration, you are adding a permanent second system.

So what did we actually gain?

Permission to escalate. The most expensive schema decisions come from teams who treat "we need a new topic" as an admission of failure and contort a schema for two years instead. Rung 3 is a normal, healthy outcome — it just needs to be chosen early, with a deadline, rather than arrived at exhausted.

All of this presumes someone is reviewing changes and someone owns each contract. That is not automatic, and it is the last chapter of Part III.

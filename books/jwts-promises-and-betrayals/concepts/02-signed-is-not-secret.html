JJWTs: Promises and Betrayals Contents Cheatsheet
Chapter Two What it does not promise
02

Signed is not secret

A signature is a claim about authorship, not a lock. The payload of every signed JWT you have ever issued is readable by everyone who has ever held it — including the people you were defending against.

One command, no key, no cleverness:

# The middle part of any JWS. No key, no cleverness.
# base64url swaps +/ for -_ and drops the padding, so plain `base64 -d`
# will choke or lie — restore both, then decode.
python3 -c 'import base64,sys
p = sys.argv[1].split(".")[1]
print(base64.urlsafe_b64decode(p + "=" * (-len(p) % 4)).decode())' "$TOKEN"

{"sub":"u_8812","email":"dana@example.com",
 "plan":"trial","internal_risk_score":0.82}

That last field is the kind of thing that ends up in a payload because the backend found it convenient. It is now visible to the user it describes, to every browser extension on their machine, and to anyone they paste the token to while asking for help.

A signed JWT is a postcard with a wax seal. The seal tells you who wrote it and that nobody edited it in transit. It does not stop the postman reading it.

Two different things wearing the same name

"JWT" is a format that can be built two ways, and they provide different guarantees. Nearly everything called a JWT in practice is a JWS — signed, and therefore public. Confidentiality requires a JWE, which is a genuinely different object: RFC 7516's Compact Serialization has five parts, not three.

How I would approach this

The attack — reading the mail

I do not need to forge anything. I collect tokens: from a browser extension, from a shared support ticket, from a screenshot in a bug report, from an access log I can already read. Then I decode the payloads and look for what your engineers put there because it was handy — internal user ids, role names that reveal your permission model, feature flags that name unreleased products, tenant identifiers I can use to guess the shape of your multi-tenancy.

None of that is a vulnerability in your token. It is reconnaissance you handed out yourself, signed, with a guarantee of authenticity.

The defense — payloads are public writing

Apply one rule when adding a claim: would I be comfortable printing this on the user's screen? If not, it does not go in a JWS payload. Put an opaque identifier in the token and keep the sensitive attributes on the server behind it. Reach for JWE only when a claim genuinely must travel through a party that should not read it — and know that you have just taken on key management for encryption as well as signing.

If you do encrypt: order matters

A Nested JWT is a JWT used as the payload of an enclosing JWS or JWE; when you do this, the cty header must be set to "JWT" so the recipient knows to unwrap again. RFC 7519 section 11.2 is explicit about the direction: sign first, then encrypt.

The reason is worth internalizing rather than memorizing. If you encrypt and then sign, the signature sits on the outside, over the ciphertext — so anyone can strip your signature and apply their own, and the signature itself becomes a public marker of who produced the message. Signing first puts the authorship proof inside the envelope, where it is protected by the same encryption as everything else.

The attack — compression as an oracle

RFC 8725 sections 2.4 and 3.6 warn that compressing before encrypting leaks information about the plaintext: compressed length varies with content, so if I can influence part of what gets compressed alongside a secret, the size of the ciphertext tells me whether my guess matched. This is the same family of attack that broke TLS compression. The BCP's guidance is blunt — compression SHOULD NOT be done before encryption.

Trust boundaries

Trusted
That a verified JWS came from the key holder and was not modified.
Never trusted
That anything in a JWS payload is private. Assume every claim is published the moment the token is issued.
Confidential only under
JWE — five parts — with sign-then-encrypt ordering and no pre-encryption compression.
Check
Decode one of your own production tokens right now and read the payload as if you were the user. Then decide whether every field belongs there.

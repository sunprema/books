Requirements
Before Architecture

"Collect telemetry from the fleet and alert on anomalies" is not a requirement. It is a wish with a verb in it. This chapter is the discipline of turning that sentence into numbers you can size against, failure modes you can design for, and non-goals you can defend — before a single box gets drawn.

From prompt to specification

Take the wish apart with four questions, asked in this order. What must the system do? How well, in measurable terms? How does it behave when it cannot? And what is it explicitly not for?

The fourth question is the one teams skip, and it is the one that saves the project. A non-goal is what lets you say no in month nine without relitigating the architecture.

Functional — the connected rail platform

  • Collect telemetry from locomotives, wayside detectors, and terminals.
  • Detect anomalies and raise operator-facing alerts.
  • Track current asset state and position.
  • Store inspection images and video, and make them findable.
  • Produce schedules and maintenance plans.
  • Notify people and downstream systems.

Non-goals

  • Not a signalling or train-control system. It issues no movement authority and enforces no interlocking.
  • Not a system of record for regulatory safety cases.
  • Not a real-time control loop: every decision it emits is advisory and reversible by an operator.

Nonfunctional, stated per class

A single availability number for "the platform" is close to meaningless. State the targets per traffic class, because they differ by orders of magnitude and the differences are what let you shed load intelligently later.

Two of these columns deserve a note. Freshness is a legitimate service level indicator in its own right, measured at the boundary the user actually perceives, alongside success rate, latency and throughput.1 For an operational platform it is frequently the only indicator that matters: a pipeline can be perfectly available and four hours behind, and to a dispatcher those are the same outage.

And "loss tolerance: last-value wins" is a real answer, not a dodge. For position, a dropped sample that is immediately superseded costs nothing. Saying so explicitly is what permits a cheaper delivery path for that class.

Capacity estimation, including the case everyone forgets

Now the arithmetic. Four loads, not one: steady state, peak, reconnect, and disaster recovery. The third is the one that sizes the system and the one that is routinely omitted.

The estimation skeleton
# steady state
ingest_eps   = N_devices × msgs_per_device_per_sec
ingest_bps   = ingest_eps × avg_bytes_per_msg

# peak — shift change, weather event, timetable peak
peak         = steady × peak_factor

# reconnect — the sizing case
burst_eps    = (N_offline × buffered_msgs) / drain_window_sec

# storage
retained     = ingest_bps × replication_factor × retention_sec

Put numbers in it. Say 4,000 locomotives, 40 signals each at 1 Hz, 250 bytes per event after batching and compression.

Look at the third and fourth rows together. The reconnect burst is six times peak, and the retention figure says the storage design — not the ingest design — is where the money goes. Both conclusions are invisible if you only compute the steady state, and both change the architecture. A platform sized for 400k events per second will fall over the first morning a regional outage clears.

The drain window is a design choice

Notice that drain_window_sec is yours to set. Widening it from 10 minutes to an hour cuts the burst by six. That is a decision about how quickly the platform must catch up after an outage, and it belongs in the requirements document — not in a broker configuration file discovered during an incident.

Failure modes as first-class requirements

Write the failure modes into the spec beside the functional requirements, with the same seriousness. Each one gets a trigger, an expected behaviour, and a detection signal. If you cannot name the detection signal, you have not finished designing the mode.

Regional cellular outage
Agents buffer to disk; positions go stale and are labelled stale; no alerts are lost. Detected by fleet last-seen distribution, not by an error rate.
Cloud ingest unavailable
Gateways apply backpressure; agents hold and prioritise by QoS class. Detected by durable-ack latency.
Stream processor lagging
Alerts continue on the hot path; derived state is served with an explicit freshness stamp. Detected by consumer lag and event-time skew.
Optimizer unavailable
Last approved plan remains in force; no replan is issued. Detected by plan age against its validity window.

Read that list again and notice what it is doing: it defines the degraded product. Most teams design the healthy product and let the degraded one emerge by accident, which is how you end up with a dispatch screen showing hour-old positions with no indication that they are old.

RPO, RTO, and the discipline they belong to

Recovery point objective is the maximum tolerated data loss; recovery time objective is the recovery-time target. Both are disaster-recovery targets and are a different discipline from a daily availability SLO — they answer "what happens after the bad day", not "how often is the bad day".1 Set them per data class, exactly as you did freshness, and expect the answers to diverge sharply: an operational alert may have an RPO of zero while diagnostics can lose an hour without anyone caring.

One requirement follows from the regulated setting and is easy to miss: recovery procedures must be designed and tested, not merely implied by replicated infrastructure, and fault-injection experiment reports are used as documented evidence of that capability.2 That makes "DR is exercised quarterly, with a report" a requirement line item, not an operational nicety.

A requirement you cannot measure is a preference. A failure mode you have not written down is an outage you have not designed.

What the specification contains

  1. Functional requirements, and the non-goals that bound them.
  2. Per-class targets: freshness, loss tolerance, retention, RPO, RTO.
  3. The four load cases, with the arithmetic shown.
  4. Failure modes, each with trigger, degraded behaviour, detection signal.
  5. Ordering and consistency requirements, per event family.
  6. Regulatory, privacy, and audit constraints.
  7. Cost ceilings, expressed per unit — per asset per month is the form that survives fleet growth.

Exercise

  1. Compute the reconnect burst for your own system's worst credible outage. Compare it to your provisioned capacity. Most readers find a factor they did not expect.
  2. Write three non-goals for a platform you currently work on. If any of them would be controversial, you have found the conversation that is overdue.
  3. Pick one failure mode above and name the metric that would page someone. If the metric is "error rate", try again — the interesting modes here are silent.

Multi-AZ, Multi-Region,
and Disaster Recovery

Multi-AZ is close to table stakes and mostly a configuration decision. Multi-region is a different animal: it is expensive, it complicates every stateful component, and it is the right answer only when you can say what it buys. This chapter is about making that case honestly — and about the fact that untested recovery is not recovery.

Zones first

Availability zones are separate failure domains close enough for synchronous replication. That combination is unusually good value: you survive a datacentre-level failure with no data loss and no application changes, which is not true of anything at the region level.

Get the basics right before considering regions. Every stateful service replicated across at least three zones; quorums that tolerate losing one; Kafka's replicas spread across zones by rack awareness; and — easily forgotten — capacity headroom to run at full load with a zone gone. A three-zone deployment sized so that losing one zone saturates the remaining two has bought nothing.

The multi-region decision

Multi-region architecture is the recommended posture for workloads whose consequences of unavailability are severe enough to justify its cost and complexity.1 That framing puts the burden where it belongs: state the consequence, then decide.

Mix them deliberately. A defensible arrangement for this platform: ingest active-active (a device must always find somewhere to deliver), alerting active-active, operational services warm standby, optimization pilot light (planning can pause), and the lake backed up with a long restore window. Uniform postures are how multi-region budgets get spent on the wrong things.

Stateless is easy; state is the whole problem

Stateless services fail over by moving traffic. Everything difficult is about state, and the choice of replication mode is a direct trade of latency for data loss.

Synchronous
No loss; every write pays the cross-region round trip. Viable across zones, rarely across regions.
Asynchronous
Fast writes, a real replication lag, and therefore a real RPO. Measure the lag and quote it as your RPO — do not quote zero.
Event-log replication
Mirror the durable log; consumers in each region rebuild state. Fits this architecture naturally.
Database replication
Native, well understood, and usually async across regions with the same caveat.
Asynchronous replication means non-zero RPO

This is the most common false claim in DR documents. If replication is asynchronous, there is a window of writes that exist in one region only, and a regional failure loses them. Measure the p99 replication lag, write it in the DR plan as the RPO, and design the reconciliation for what falls in that window.

The edge buffer softens this in a way most systems do not enjoy. Because agents hold events until a durable ack, a region loss that discards recent writes can often be repaired by asking devices to resend from a known sequence number. That is a genuine architectural advantage of store-and-forward — the field is a replica of last resort. Design the resend path deliberately rather than discovering it.

Cross-region streaming

Stretching a single Kafka cluster across regions is usually a mistake: the replication protocol is latency-sensitive and inter-region latency turns every produce into a slow one. The standard alternative is a cluster per region with asynchronous mirroring between them.

Mirroring brings two constraints worth stating plainly. Offsets do not correspond between clusters, so a consumer failing over cannot simply resume at its old offset — it needs offset translation or an application-level checkpoint keyed on event identity. And mirroring is asynchronous, so the mirror lag is part of your RPO. Both are manageable; neither is automatic.

Split-brain and fencing

The failure that turns an outage into a data-integrity incident: region B is promoted while region A is merely unreachable, not dead. Now two primaries accept writes and diverge, and the merge afterwards is manual, slow, and lossy.

Prevent it structurally rather than procedurally. Failover decisions come from an external arbiter with its own quorum, never from the region trying to promote itself. The old primary is fenced — its credentials or storage leases revoked — before the new one is promoted. Writers carry a monotonically increasing epoch and storage rejects a stale one. And promotion is deliberately not automatic for the components where two writers would be unrecoverable.

Tested failover, or it does not count

This is the chapter's real thesis. Disaster recovery requires recovery procedures that have been designed and tested, not merely replicated infrastructure.2 Fault-injection experiments are used to test real disruptions and demand spikes, and their reports serve as documented evidence of recovery capability2 — which in a regulated domain is an artifact someone will ask you for.

Managed recovery controllers exist to shift traffic across availability zones and orchestrate cross-region failover without bespoke manual scripting.2 Prefer them: the failover script that only one engineer understands is itself a single point of failure, and it is the one that will be executed under the most pressure.

Build the exercise ladder in this order, and note that the last rung is the one most programmes never reach:

  1. Kill a single instance. Weekly, automated.
  2. Remove a zone. Monthly.
  3. Fail over one stateful service, and measure actual RPO against the claimed one.
  4. Region-level game day, quarterly, with the real on-call.
  5. Fail back. Failing over is half the procedure; a programme that never rehearses failback leaves the platform stranded in its DR posture after every real event.
A DR plan that has never been executed is a hypothesis. The quarterly game day is what turns it into a capability.

RPO and RTO by data class

One platform-wide RPO is as unhelpful as one platform-wide availability number. Set them per class, and let the differences drive the spend.

Row three is the one to notice, because it is free money. Derived state does not need replicating if it can be recomputed from a replicated raw log within its RTO. Every piece of state you can move into that row is state you do not have to replicate, and the replay discipline from Chapter 8 is what makes it possible.

Exercise

  1. Measure your actual replication lag at p99 for a week. Compare it to the RPO in your DR document. Reconcile the difference.
  2. Write the failback procedure for your most critical stateful service. Time it in a rehearsal.
  3. Which of your replicated state could be recomputed instead? Cost the replication you would stop paying for.

03

Core Concepts
and Tradeoffs

This is the vocabulary chapter, and it earns its place by being precise where the industry is usually loose. Three ideas here — the path split, event time, and what "exactly once" actually buys you — account for most of the architectural rework we have watched teams do on operational platforms.

Control plane and data plane

The data plane moves telemetry: high volume, simple per-message work, and it must keep running when everything else is broken. The control plane manages the fleet: enrollment, configuration, firmware, certificates — low volume, complex logic, and it may be down for an hour without a train noticing.

The rule that follows is short and load-bearing: the data plane must never take a synchronous dependency on the control plane. An agent that has to look up its configuration before it can buffer an event has just made a cloud service a prerequisite for offline operation.

Hot, warm, and cold

The mistake is not having three paths — it is building one and hoping it satisfies all three budgets. It cannot: the hot path must emit before late data has arrived, and the cold path must wait for it. Accepting that a fast answer and a complete answer are different answers is what makes the design tractable. What operations then needs from you is that each answer says how old it is.

Event time versus processing time

Event time is when the thing happened in the field. Processing time is when your system found out. In a web system the gap is milliseconds; here it is routinely hours, because a locomotive spent the morning in a valley. Watermarks exist precisely to decouple the two — they track the progress of event time through a stream so a processor can decide a window is complete despite out-of-order arrival.1

Three timestamps, not one

Every event in this book carries event_time (stamped by the device, with a quality flag), ingest_time (stamped by the cloud, trustworthy), and an implicit processing time per stage. Windowing on the wrong one is the single most common correctness bug in telemetry pipelines — and it is invisible until an asset reconnects with six hours of backlog and rewrites yesterday's averages.

Delivery semantics, stated honestly

At-most-once drops on failure. At-least-once retries and therefore duplicates. "Exactly once" is the one that needs unpacking, because the guarantee people imagine is not the guarantee that is on offer.

Kafka's idempotent producer assigns each producer a producer ID and tracks a per-partition sequence number, which lets the broker discard a retried duplicate. What it does not do by itself is deduplicate across producer restarts, or make writes to several partitions atomic.2 Transactions add that atomic multi-partition write, and enabling transactions implies idempotence.3

So the broker gives you an exactly-once segment. It does not give you exactly-once effects. If your consumer writes a work order to a database and the process dies before committing its offset, the work order is written twice regardless of what the producer guaranteed.

Effectively-once is an end-to-end business property established by idempotent effects and deduplication keys. The broker's guarantee is one segment of it.

The practical consequence is a design rule we will apply for the rest of the book: give every event a stable identity and make every effect idempotent under that identity. Then at-least-once delivery is sufficient, and you have bought yourself the freedom to replay.

Identity and idempotent effect
-- the event's identity travels with it, end to end
INSERT INTO maintenance_alert (alert_key, asset_id, kind, raised_at)
VALUES (:event_id, :asset, :kind, :event_time)
ON CONFLICT (alert_key) DO NOTHING;

That one ON CONFLICT is worth more than a transactional producer configuration, because it holds no matter how the duplicate arrived — broker retry, consumer restart, or an operator replaying a day of history to fix something else.

Consistency, and CAP without the slogan

CAP is a statement about behaviour during a partition. It is not a menu you pick from at design time and it is not a property of your database's marketing page. Outside a partition, you can have consistency and availability both; the theorem only forces a choice while the network is split.

In an operational platform partitions are not hypothetical — they are Tuesday. So the useful question is per-service and concrete: when this service cannot reach its peers, does it refuse to answer, or answer with what it has? Both are defensible. Answering with stale data without saying so is not.

Objectives, budgets, and what they authorise

An error budget is the permitted unreliability implied by an SLO: a 99.9% target leaves 0.1%, and that budget is consumed by deployments, maintenance, and outages alike.4 Its value is that it converts an argument about caution into an arithmetic question — is there budget left? — which is a much better argument to have at 3 a.m.

Keep these four separate in your head, because they answer different questions and teams routinely quote one when asked for another:

SLO
How good is good enough, on an ordinary day.
Error budget
What that target authorises you to spend.
RPO / RTO
What happens after the bad day — loss, and recovery time.
MTTR
How fast you actually recover, observed rather than promised.

Design review questions

  1. Name one place where your data plane calls the control plane synchronously. What happens to the field when that call times out?
  2. Which of your windows are computed on ingest time because it was easier? What does a six-hour reconnect do to them?
  3. Pick a consumer that writes to a database. Is its effect idempotent under the event's identity, or under a database-generated one? Only the first survives a replay.

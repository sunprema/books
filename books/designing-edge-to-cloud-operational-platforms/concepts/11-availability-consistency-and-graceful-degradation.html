Availability and
Graceful Degradation

Every system degrades. The only question is whether it degrades in a way you designed or a way you discovered. This chapter is about designing the degraded product deliberately — deciding, per service, what it does when it cannot do its job, and making sure the operator can tell.

Classify by consequence, not by importance

"Importance" is a conversation nobody wins; every team believes their service is critical. Consequence is a question with an answer: what happens in the world if this is wrong or absent for an hour?

Four consequence classes
ClassConsequence of failureDesign posture
InformationalSomeone is mildly less informedShed freely under load
Operationally importantWork is slower or manualDegrade to cached/stale, labelled
Safety-adjacentA safety process loses an inputFail loudly; never fail silently
Safety-criticalDirect safety consequenceNot in this platform

The distinction between the last two rows is the one to hold onto. A defect-detection alert is safety-adjacent: it informs a process that has its own independent checks. It is not the check. That is what lets us build it on ordinary cloud infrastructure with ordinary availability targets — provided it fails loudly, so nobody mistakes silence for "no defects".

The safety boundary

Safety-critical functions are not made safe by high availability. They are made safe by dedicated systems designed, certified, and operated under a regime this platform does not participate in. Our obligation at the boundary is narrower and absolutely firm: never present degraded output as healthy, and never let our silence be interpretable as an all-clear.

The four degraded modes

Between "working" and "down" there are four useful states. Naming them makes them designable, and makes them testable.

Read-only
Serve queries, refuse writes. Right when write correctness needs coordination you have lost — the device registry, plan approval.
Cached
Serve the last known value, labelled with its age. Right for asset position and health, where stale beats absent.
Delayed
Accept work, queue it, apply later. Right for anything whose effect is not time-critical — work-order creation, image analysis.
Offline
The field operates on local rules alone. The mode every design in this book must remain correct in.

Pick one per service, write it down, and then — this is the part that gets skipped — exercise it on a schedule. A fallback path that has never run is not a fallback; it is untested code that will execute for the first time during the incident it was meant to survive.

The dependency hierarchy

Draw your services as a dependency graph and mark each edge with what happens when it breaks. Two rules govern the drawing, and violations of either are architectural bugs rather than operational ones:

  1. A more critical service must never synchronously depend on a less critical one. Alert raising must not call the reporting service.
  2. The data plane must never synchronously depend on the control plane — the rule from Chapter 3, which is really a dependency-hierarchy rule wearing a different hat.

Where an inversion is genuinely unavoidable, convert it from synchronous to asynchronous: publish an event instead of making a call. The dependency remains, but it no longer transmits failure upward in real time.

This is exactly why the resilience literature emphasises that loosely coupled dependencies — queues, streams, workflows, load balancers — give you more flexibility to isolate failures.1 Every durable log in this architecture is a place where failure stops propagating.

The four containment patterns

A note on each, because they are routinely confused. Bulkheads give separate resource pools so one slow dependency cannot consume every thread. Timeouts must be shorter than the caller's timeout, or you queue work nobody is waiting for any more. Circuit breakers stop asking a service that is clearly down, which is as much about letting it recover as protecting you. And load shedding drops the cheapest work first — which requires knowing which work is cheap, which is why traffic classes were worth defining.

Degradation, made concrete

Abstractions are easy to agree with. Here is what these choices actually look like on the rail platform.

Three degradations, and what the operator sees
FailureBehaviourOperator sees
Position pipeline laggingServe last known position"Positions as of 09:14 — 6 min old"
Optimizer unavailableKeep last approved plan; local rules stand"Plan from 06:00; replan unavailable"
Image analysis downQueue images; gates keep operating"1,204 images awaiting analysis"

Every row's third column is the deliverable. A dispatcher acting on an unlabelled stale position is worse off than one who knows the data is six minutes old — the second can compensate, the first cannot. This is the single highest-leverage habit in the chapter: degradation must be visible at the point of use, not on a status page in another tab.

Stale data with a timestamp is a tool. Stale data without one is a trap.

Escalation and runbooks

Automation should hand off to a human when it reaches the edge of what it can decide, and the handoff needs to carry context rather than an error code: what degraded, what the fallback is doing, what is accumulating, and what happens if nobody acts within the hour.

The runbook then belongs to a named owner and is exercised, not filed. A useful test: can someone on their first on-call shift follow it at 3 a.m. without asking who wrote it? If not, it is documentation rather than a runbook.

Design review questions

  1. List your services in consequence classes. If more than a quarter are safety-adjacent, the classification has not done its work.
  2. Find one place where a critical service synchronously calls a less critical one. What is the async version?
  3. Pick a screen your operators use. Does it show the age of what it is showing? If not, that is this week's change.

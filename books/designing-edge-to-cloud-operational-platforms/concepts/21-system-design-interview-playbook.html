System Design
Interview Playbook

The same material, compressed into 45 minutes. This chapter is useful for interviews, and it is more useful for the design reviews that resemble them — because the discipline of justifying a choice out loud, under time pressure, is the same discipline either way.

The commonest structural mistake is spending twenty-five minutes on the drawing and reaching failure handling with three minutes left. Failure handling is the part that distinguishes an operational design from a diagram, and a strong candidate gets there early — often by raising a degradation concern while still drawing.

Clarifying: the five questions

Ask these regardless of the prompt. Each one changes the architecture, and asking them signals that you know it.

  1. Who consumes this, and how fast do they need it? Establishes latency class per consumer.
  2. What happens if it is late, wrong, or missing? Establishes consequence class — and whether safety is in scope.
  3. How many producers, at what rate, and can they disconnect? Establishes scale and whether buffering is mandatory.
  4. What ordering do consumers actually require? Establishes the partition key before you draw the log.
  5. How long must it be retained, and who asks for it later? Establishes storage cost and audit requirements.

Estimating out loud

Round aggressively, state assumptions, and show the arithmetic. The interviewer is checking that you can reason to an order of magnitude, not that you can multiply.

Worked, in about ninety seconds
4,000 vehicles × 40 signals × 1 Hz     = 160k events/s
160k × 250 B                          = 40 MB/s
40 MB/s × 86,400                       ≈ 3.5 TB/day raw
× 3 replicas × 400 days               ≈ 4 PB retained

# and the one that sizes the system:
4,000 offline 1 h, drained in 10 min   ≈ 960k events/s

Say the last line out loud with emphasis. Volunteering the reconnect burst — the load nobody asked about — is the single highest-signal move available in an IoT-shaped interview, because it demonstrates you have thought about the system's actual worst day.

Weak answers, and their strong versions

What to say instead
WeakStrong
"Use Kafka""Kafka, keyed by asset id for per-asset ordering; N partitions sized from the drain requirement"
"Multi-region for HA""Ingest active-active; async replication elsewhere, so RPO equals replication lag — measured, not zero"
"Exactly-once processing""At-least-once delivery with idempotent effects keyed on event id, and a bounded dedup window"
"Add AI for defects""Edge inference; detections are findings for human review, with drift monitoring and per-device rollback"
"Cloud handles it""The edge buffers and evaluates local rules, so the field stays useful through an outage"
"We'll scale horizontally""Consumer parallelism is capped by partition count, so that is the number to size"

The pattern across the right-hand column: each answer names the mechanism and the constraint it operates under. "Use Kafka" is a technology choice; "keyed by asset id, because per-asset ordering is what the anomaly detector needs" is a design decision.

Name the mechanism and the constraint. A technology without a constraint is a preference; with one, it is a decision.

Discussing bottlenecks honestly

When asked what breaks first, do not deflect. Naming a real limit and its mitigation reads as senior; claiming the design has no limits reads as inexperienced.

  • Partition count caps consumer parallelism, so it caps recovery speed after a burst.
  • Stateful processor recovery grows with state size; an unbounded keyed state eventually exceeds your RTO.
  • Cross-region replication lag is your real RPO, and it degrades exactly when the network is stressed.
  • Human review capacity bounds the vision pipeline — a detector is only as useful as the queue it feeds.
  • Storage cost, not throughput, is what usually forces an architectural change first.

Mock prompts

Five prompts, each with the trap it is built around. Work them under a timer; the constraint is the point.

Design locomotive telemetry ingestion
Trap: designing for steady state. The reconnect burst is the sizing case, and buffering is mandatory rather than an optimisation.
Design a wayside defect-detection platform
Trap: treating detections as truth. They are probabilistic findings needing review, calibration, and negative sampling.
Design real-time asset tracking
Trap: lat/long as the answer. Operations needs track and milepost, and the map-match carries uncertainty.
Design a terminal scheduling engine
Trap: solving for optimality. Plans decay, need validation before publication, and must be explainable and reversible.
Design an offline-capable dispatch application
Trap: caching as an afterthought. Staleness must be visible, and conflict resolution on reconnect is the hard part.

Two habits worth keeping

First, state the safety boundary unprompted when the domain has one. Saying "this platform is advisory; movement authority stays with the signalling system, which is independent of it" takes eight seconds and demonstrates the judgement the whole book has been arguing for. Claiming a safety property for a platform whose boundary was never stated is the one answer that should sink a design.

Second, say what you would measure. Not "it'll be fine" but "I'd track end-to-end freshness p99 per asset class, consumer lag trend, and buffer depth across the fleet, and I'd expect buffer depth to warn me before the other two move." Naming the instrumentation is how you show a design is operable rather than merely drawable — which is, ultimately, the difference this book has been about.

Practice

  1. Take mock prompt one and run it in 45 minutes against a timer. Note exactly when you reached failure handling.
  2. Write your reconnect-burst estimate as a reusable four-line calculation you can produce from memory.
  3. For a system you know well, write the six-row weak/strong table. Which row is your team's habitual weak answer?

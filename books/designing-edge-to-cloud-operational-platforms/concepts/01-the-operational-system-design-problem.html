The Operational
System Design Problem

Most of what you know about building distributed systems still applies here. What changes is the ground it stands on. In a web system the network is a detail that occasionally fails; in an operational system the network is a moving, weather-dependent, partly-absent thing, and the assets you are trying to observe are diesel-electric locomotives that will be in service longer than your company will exist. This chapter is about what that difference actually costs you at design time.

What actually differs

Start with the honest version of the comparison. A typical web backend has clients that reconnect within seconds, a datacentre network with known characteristics, and a fleet you can redeploy in an afternoon. An operational platform for a rail network has none of those. Its "clients" are on vehicles moving through terrain, its links are cellular and satellite with per-byte costs, and a firmware change may take a maintenance cycle to reach the whole fleet.

Where the assumptions break
AssumptionWeb systemOperational platform
ConnectivityPresent; loss is an errorIntermittent; loss is a mode
Client lifetimeMonthsDecades
Client upgradePush, minutesStaged, weeks, sometimes physical
BandwidthEffectively freeMetered, sometimes per-megabyte
ClockNTP-synchronisedDrifts; GPS lost in tunnels
ProtocolsOne or two you choseMany you inherited
Consequence of failureA retryA truck roll, or a stopped train

The last row is the one that reorganises everything above it. When the cost of being wrong is a physical intervention, you stop optimising for average-case throughput and start optimising for bounded behaviour under partial failure. That is a different design target, and it is the target this book aims at.

Four kinds of traffic, four sets of rules

The single most useful early move is refusing to call everything "data". An operational platform carries at least four categories, and they have genuinely different requirements. Conflating them is how a platform ends up applying safety-grade latency budgets to diagnostic counters, or — much worse — the reverse.

Telemetry is high-volume, one-directional, and tolerant of delay as long as it is not tolerant of loss. Commands are rare, must be authenticated per-message, and need an acknowledgement path that distinguishes "received" from "carried out". Decisions — a schedule, a maintenance priority, a routing plan — are proposals about the future, and are almost always advisory. And safety-critical control is the category that does not belong in this platform at all.

The safety boundary

This book teaches high availability and graceful degradation, which are architectural properties you can design and measure. Operational safety in a rail-like domain is a different discipline: it is bounded explicitly, owned by dedicated safety systems and procedures, and subject to certification regimes that a general cloud platform does not participate in. Every chapter states where that line falls rather than blurring it. If a design in this book appears to be making a safety decision, the design is wrong.

The running example

We will build against one domain for the whole book: a connected rail network. It is a good teacher because it contains every awkward case at once, and a fair one because none of it depends on a particular railroad or cloud vendor.

  • Locomotives — mobile, high-value, hundreds of sensors each, connectivity that comes and goes with terrain.
  • Wayside detectors — fixed, sparse, bursty: silent for hours, then a train passes and they emit a dense burst.
  • Terminals and gates — fixed, well-connected, and the place where automated decisions meet physical throughput.
  • Inspection vehicles and drones — media producers, gigabytes per run, uploaded opportunistically at a depot.
  • Dispatch, maintenance, planning — the humans and services that consume all of it and act on it.

Note how badly these five want different things. The wayside detector's burst and the locomotive's steady trickle cannot share a capacity plan. The drone's gigabytes and the maintenance alert's few hundred bytes cannot share a delivery priority. A platform that treats them uniformly will be sized for the wrong thing and will drop the wrong messages first.

The cloud can optimise operations. The field must remain useful when the cloud, the network, or any individual service is unavailable.

Design goals, in the order they bite

We will keep returning to seven goals. They are listed here in roughly the order they cause architectural pain, which is not the order they usually appear on a slide.

Safety boundaries
Stated first because it constrains everything else: what may this platform decide, and what must it never decide?
Availability and graceful degradation
Not "does it stay up" but "what does it do while it is partly down, and does the operator know?"
Latency
Per traffic class, end to end — sensor to operator's screen, not broker to consumer.
Correctness
Ordering, duplication, and reconciliation. Usually the deepest rework if you get it wrong.
Auditability
Can you reconstruct what the system knew and when? Regulated domains make this non-optional.
Cost
Bandwidth, retention, and replication dominate; compute rarely does.
Evolvability
Assets outlive architectures. Whatever you build will be maintained by someone who has never met you.

Notice that resilience for critical systems is consistently framed in the industry literature in terms of redundancy, failure isolation, automated recovery, and tested failover, rather than any assumption that outages will not occur; geographic distribution exists specifically to survive localised failure, and the dominant lever on availability is how quickly failed capacity is replaced.1 That framing is worth internalising now, because it is the opposite of the instinct to make individual components more reliable.

The idea to carry forward

Loosely coupled dependencies — queues, streams, workflows, load balancers — are what give you room to isolate a failure.1 Every time this book chooses a durable log over a synchronous call, that is the reason.

So what did we actually gain?

A vocabulary and a boundary. We can now say "this is warm-path telemetry with a five-minute freshness target and a retention class of 90 days" instead of "we need to collect sensor data", and we can say what the platform is not responsible for. The next chapter turns that vocabulary into numbers, because a requirement you cannot measure is a preference.

Design review questions

  1. For each of the five asset types, which traffic class dominates its volume, and which dominates its urgency? They are rarely the same.
  2. Name one decision your platform makes today that is closer to the safety boundary than you would defend in a review.
  3. If the cloud were unreachable for six hours, list what the field can still do. If the list is empty, the architecture has a premise problem.

Observability and
Operational Forensics

Standard observability assumes the thing you are watching is trying to talk to you. Half of this system is not: it is in a valley, buffering. The central adaptation is to stop measuring availability and start measuring freshness, then to keep enough evidence to reconstruct what the system knew and when.

The three pillars, and where they run out

Metrics, logs, and traces are the standard toolkit, and the four golden signals — latency, traffic, errors, saturation — are the standard starting set for a service.1 All of it applies to the cloud half of this platform and none of it is sufficient for the field half.

Where each pillar stops
PillarLimit here
MetricsDevices cannot report while offline — exactly when you need to know
LogsEdge logs are bandwidth-expensive and arrive hours late
TracesSpan the cloud well; a store-and-forward hop is not a span

The gap they share is the same one: absence of data is itself the signal, and none of the three pillars is built to alert on silence. That is why the fleet needs its own instrumentation model.

Freshness is the indicator that matters

Freshness is a legitimate service level indicator alongside success rate, latency, and throughput, measured at the boundary the user actually perceives.2 For an operational platform it is frequently the only one worth paging on.

The reason is simple. A pipeline can be 100% available, throwing zero errors, and four hours behind. Every dashboard is green, and the dispatcher is looking at where the trains were at breakfast. Availability monitoring cannot see this failure at all; freshness monitoring sees nothing else.

Available and four hours behind is an outage. If your monitoring says otherwise, your monitoring is measuring the wrong thing.

Measure it end to end, as the difference between now and the event time of the most recent event the consumer has processed — and report it as a distribution across the fleet, not a mean. The mean is dominated by the healthy majority; the tail is where the story is.

Fleet freshness, as a distribution
freshness(asset) = now() - max(event_time of processed events for asset)

-- report percentiles, and a count beyond a hard threshold
p50   12s        -- healthy
p95   48s
p99   6m10s
>30m  37 assets  -- the number an operator actually needs

The fleet metrics worth having

These are the edge-specific signals. Each one detects a failure the golden signals cannot see.

Last-seen distribution
Per asset. The primary fleet-health signal; a shifting tail is a regional problem before anyone reports one.
Sequence gaps
Missing seq ranges. Unambiguous evidence of loss, as opposed to silence.
Buffer depth
How much each agent is holding. Predicts the reconnect burst before it arrives.
Upload lag
Event time to durable ack, measured at the device.
Clock skew and clock_src mix
A fleet drifting to unsynced corrupts event-time processing quietly.
Eviction counts
Events dropped by retention class. Non-zero means you are losing data by policy — which is fine, but must be visible.
Consumer lag
Per group and partition, alerted on trend rather than level.

Correlation across four boundaries

Distributed tracing with propagated correlation identifiers is the practice that makes a request legible across service boundaries.1 Here the id has to survive four hops of very different character: device, gateway, stream, and service.

The gateway is where it usually breaks, and it is exactly where an edge-to-cloud investigation needs it to survive. Generate the id at the device, carry it in the envelope rather than in a transport header (headers do not survive a store-and-forward hop), and propagate it into every derived event so an alert can be traced back to the readings that produced it.

Alert on symptoms

Alert on what the user experiences, not on the cause you happen to have a metric for. Causes are many and change with every deployment; symptoms are few and stable.

Cause-shaped versus symptom-shaped alerts
Instead ofAlert on
Broker CPU > 80%End-to-end latency p99 above the class target
Consumer restartedFreshness beyond target for N assets
Disk 90% fullProjected time-to-full under 6 hours
Devices disconnectedLast-seen tail growing faster than baseline

Note the third row: the useful alert is a projection, not a threshold. "90% full" is either an emergency or irrelevant depending on the fill rate, and the alert that tells you which is the one worth waking up for.

Then attach an error budget to each SLO, which converts an argument about caution into arithmetic: is there budget left?2 That is a much better conversation to have during a change freeze.

Forensics: reconstructing what was known

In a regulated domain, "what did the system know, and when did it know it?" is a question you will be asked formally. Answering it requires more than logs — it requires an immutable event record and the ability to replay it.

That is why telemetry.raw is never edited and why every derived event carries its inputs' identities. Three artifacts make the reconstruction possible:

  1. The immutable raw record, with both event time and ingest time — so you can distinguish when it happened from when you could have known.
  2. Decision provenance — every alert and plan records the input snapshot, the rule or model version, and the code version that produced it.
  3. Replay determinism — reprocessing the same raw events with the same versions yields the same outputs. Test this periodically; it decays silently.

With those, an incident timeline is generated rather than reconstructed from memory, and the distinction between "the system was wrong" and "the system had not been told yet" — which matters enormously in a review — is answerable from evidence.

Ingest time is an audit field

The gap between event_time and ingest_time is the honest answer to "could you have acted sooner?". Keeping both is what lets you say "the event occurred at 09:14 and reached us at 11:40, because the asset was out of coverage" — a defensible statement rather than an apology.

Design review questions

  1. What is your fleet's freshness p99 right now? If you cannot answer in one query, that is the first thing to build.
  2. Take a recent alert and trace it back to the raw readings. How many boundaries did the correlation id survive?
  3. Replay yesterday's raw telemetry. Do you get yesterday's alerts, exactly? Any difference is an undeclared dependency on wall-clock time or mutable reference data.

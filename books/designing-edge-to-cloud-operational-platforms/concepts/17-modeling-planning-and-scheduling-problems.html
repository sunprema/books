Modeling Planning and
Scheduling Problems

Everything so far has been a transactional system: an event arrives, something happens, it is correct or it is not. Optimization is a different animal. There is no single right answer, the answer degrades as its inputs age, and the output is a proposal that a human may overrule. Getting the modelling right matters more than the solver.

How optimization differs

Two very different kinds of system
TransactionalOptimization
Correct answerExactly oneA quality spectrum
RuntimeMilliseconds, boundedMinutes to hours, open-ended
InputThe current requestA snapshot of the whole world
FailureError to the callerNo plan, or a worse plan
Value decaysNoYes — continuously

The last row drives the architecture. A plan computed against a 30-minute-old snapshot may be worse than a cruder plan computed against current data, and there is a crossover point. Finding it is an engineering question, not a solver question — and it is the question that decides how you decompose the problem.

A schedule is a plan, not truth

This is the framing to hold. The optimizer's output is a proposal about the future, which is why it is published as a versioned plan rather than written as authoritative state. Reality diverges from it within minutes, and the platform's job is to notice the divergence, not to pretend it away.

Concretely, that means a plan is published as an immutable versioned event (schedule.plan.updated.v1) with an explicit validity window, a provenance record, and an approval status. Consumers reference a specific plan version, so "which plan were we operating under at 14:20?" is always answerable. A plan that is silently overwritten in a table cannot answer that.

Modelling the domain

Model the network as a graph, but be careful about what the nodes are. The naive choice — stations as nodes, track as edges — cannot express the constraint that actually binds: two trains cannot occupy the same block at the same time.

Use track blocks as the resource, and model the problem as allocating block-time intervals to movements. Now capacity conflicts are interval overlaps, which is a representation a solver can reason about directly.

The core entities
Asset      : locomotive, car, inspection vehicle — availability, location, capability
Crew       : hours-of-service limits, qualifications, home terminal
Block      : a track segment that holds one movement at a time
Terminal   : finite tracks, finite processing rate
Movement   : an asset traversing an ordered block sequence in a time window
Plan       : a set of movements + block-time allocations, versioned

Hard and soft constraints

Separating these correctly is the highest-leverage modelling decision in the chapter. A hard constraint must hold or the plan is invalid; a soft constraint is a preference with a penalty.

Hard
Block exclusivity. Crew hours-of-service limits. Terminal track capacity. Asset physical capability. Maintenance windows already committed.
Soft
Preferred departure times. Crew shift preferences. Fuel-efficient speed profiles. Balanced terminal utilisation. Minimising plan churn against the last published plan.
Encoding a preference as a hard constraint

The most common modelling failure, and the most frustrating: it turns a merely expensive schedule into an infeasible one, and the solver then returns nothing at all. Nothing is far worse than a plan with a penalty on it. When in doubt, make it soft with a large penalty — you get an answer, and the penalty tells you how badly it hurt.

Do design for infeasibility anyway, because it will happen. When a solver cannot satisfy the hard constraints, it should return the minimal conflicting set — "these three commitments cannot all hold" — rather than a bare failure. That is an actionable message for a planner; a timeout is not.

Objectives are plural

Operations is trading on-time performance against energy, utilisation, crew constraints, and delay simultaneously. A single scalar objective hides that trade rather than resolving it.

Two workable approaches. Weighted sum is simple and tunable, but the weights are a policy decision that belongs to operations rather than to engineering — make them configuration, version them, and record which weights produced a given plan. Lexicographic ordering optimises in priority tiers: satisfy safety-adjacent commitments first, then on-time performance, then cost. It is easier to explain to a planner, which matters more than it sounds.

Whichever you choose, report the objective breakdown with the plan. "On-time 94%, energy index 1.08, crew overtime 3.2 h" lets a human judge the trade. A single score of 8,412 does not.

A plan nobody can explain is a plan nobody will follow. The breakdown is part of the output, not a debugging aid.

Decomposition

A network-wide, day-long schedule is too large to solve monolithically at a useful cadence. Decompose it — and the decomposition dimension is itself a design choice with consequences.

Decomposition dimensions
ByNatural boundaryCoupling left over
GeographyCorridor, divisionHandoffs at boundaries
Time horizonNext 2 h vs next 24 hState at the seam
TerminalOne yard's internal planArrival/departure commitments
ScenarioIndependent what-ifsNone — embarrassingly parallel

Geographic decomposition is usually first, because corridors are genuinely loosely coupled — but the handoffs are where plans go wrong. Model the boundary explicitly as a commitment: corridor A promises to deliver a movement to the boundary within a window, and corridor B plans against that window rather than against A's internal detail. Then a change inside A that still meets its commitment does not force B to replan.

Uncertainty and stale inputs

Every input is a measurement with an age and an error bar. Chapter 13's freshness discipline pays off here: an optimizer should know how old its snapshot is, and behave differently when it is stale.

  • Buffer proportionally to input age — a plan built on 20-minute-old positions needs more slack than one on current data.
  • Prefer robust to optimal — a plan that is good across likely perturbations beats one that is optimal for a single forecast and brittle around it.
  • Refuse past a staleness threshold. Better to keep the previous plan than to publish one built on data too old to trust; say so, loudly, rather than degrading silently.
The safety boundary

Nothing here grants movement authority. The optimizer proposes a schedule; the systems and procedures responsible for safe separation of movements remain wholly independent of it and are authoritative. A plan that appears to conflict with them is simply a plan that will not be executed — and the platform must be designed so that this outcome is normal and visible rather than exceptional.

Design review questions

  1. List your hard constraints. For each, ask what happens if it is violated in the real world. If the answer is "it costs money", it is soft.
  2. How old is your optimizer's input snapshot at the moment the plan is published? Is the plan still better than the one it replaces?
  3. Can a planner explain a plan to a dispatcher without opening the solver? If not, the objective breakdown is missing.

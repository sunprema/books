Kafka and the
Streaming Backbone

Kafka is the durable buffer that isolates a fleet of producers from consumers that are slower, restarting, or temporarily broken. Most of what makes it work well for telemetry comes down to two decisions — the partition key and the partition count — and both are much harder to change later than to get right now.

The mental model, briefly

A topic is split into partitions; each partition is an append-only, ordered log replicated across brokers; consumers track their position with offsets. Ordering is guaranteed within a partition and nowhere else, which is the single fact from which most design consequences follow.

Within a consumer group each partition is assigned to exactly one consumer at a time, and a group coordinator manages assignments as membership or topic metadata changes.1 Two things follow immediately: your parallelism is capped by the partition count, and consumers beyond that count sit idle.1

Choosing the partition key

The common mistake is choosing a key for even distribution. That is the second consideration. The key is an ordering decision first. Whatever shares a key shares an order; everything else is concurrent.

Candidate keys and what each one buys
KeyOrdering guaranteeRisk
asset_idPer locomotive — usually what you wantA busy asset makes a warm partition
consist_idPer train, across its vehiclesChanges as consists are re-formed
regionPer geographySevere skew; regions differ hugely
sensor_idPer signalLoses cross-sensor ordering on one asset
none (round-robin)NoneMaximum parallelism, no ordering at all

For telemetry, asset_id is nearly always right: an anomaly detector reasoning about one locomotive needs that locomotive's events in order, and does not care how they interleave with another's. The skew it produces is mild, because assets are broadly similar in output. Region keys, by contrast, produce genuinely painful skew — one corridor may carry ten times another's traffic.

Partition count, and why it is nearly permanent

Here is the trap. Raising the partition count later changes the key-to-partition mapping, so events for one asset that used to land on partition 3 start landing on partition 11 — while older events sit unread on partition 3. Per-key ordering breaks across the change, silently, for exactly as long as the backlog lasts.

So provision for the fleet you expect, not the fleet you have. Partitions are cheap; a broken ordering guarantee is not.

Sizing sketch
target_throughput / per_partition_throughput  # consumer-side, measured
  × headroom_for_growth                    # 2–3× a 3-year fleet plan
  × headroom_for_recovery                  # drain the reconnect burst
  = partition_count

The recovery term is the one Chapter 2 warned about: you must be able to drain a burst several times steady-state, and consumer parallelism is capped by partition count. Sizing only for steady state guarantees a slow recovery on exactly the morning you need a fast one.

Durability settings that matter

Since Kafka 3.0 the producer defaults to the stronger delivery guarantees — acks=all with enable.idempotence=true.2 That is the right default for telemetry, and it is worth knowing it is a default rather than something you must remember to turn on. acks=all requires acknowledgement from the in-sync replica set, and works together with the broker-side min.insync.replicas to determine durability.3

Producer and topic settings for a telemetry workload
# producer — durability first
enable.idempotence=true          # default since 3.0
acks=all
compression.type=zstd            # the link is metered; so is retention
linger.ms=25                     # batching pays for itself at fleet scale
max.in.flight.requests.per.connection=5

# topic / broker
replication.factor=3
min.insync.replicas=2             # tolerate one replica loss, still durable

The pairing of replication.factor=3 with min.insync.replicas=2 is the standard compromise: you survive losing one replica and keep accepting writes, but you refuse writes rather than accept them with only a single copy. Setting min.insync.replicas=1 converts a durability guarantee into a hope.

Verify defaults against your release

Kafka's defaults have moved over time and continue to. Treat the values above as a starting point to confirm against the version you actually run, and pin them explicitly in configuration so an upgrade cannot change your durability posture without a review.

Retention, compaction, and replay

Two retention models, used for different purposes:

Time/size retention
For event streams. telemetry.raw keeps enough history to re-derive downstream state — typically days to weeks, with the lake holding the long tail.
Log compaction
For state topics. asset.position keyed by asset retains the latest value per key indefinitely, so a new consumer can rebuild current state by reading from the beginning.

Replay is the capability all of this exists to protect. When normalisation has a bug, you fix the code and reprocess telemetry.raw from an offset. That works only if every downstream effect is idempotent under event_id — the rule from Chapter 3, now cashing in.

Partition count caps your parallelism, and your parallelism caps how fast you recover. Size for the recovery, not the average day.

What to actually monitor

Broker health dashboards are reassuring and mostly beside the point. The metrics that tell you whether operations can see the field are these:

The backbone's real signals
MetricWhy it mattersAlert on
Consumer lagHow far behind the truth you areTrend, per group and partition
End-to-end latencySensor to consumer — the real SLIp99 against the class target
Under-replicated partitionsDurability is degraded nowAny, sustained
ISR shrink rateBroker or network trouble brewingRate of change
Producer error rateGateways failing to publishAny sustained non-zero
Partition skewA hot key throttles a whole groupMax/median ratio

Lag deserves a nuance: alert on its trend, not its absolute value. Lag spikes every time a fleet reconnects, and that is the system working. Lag that is not shrinking is the system failing.

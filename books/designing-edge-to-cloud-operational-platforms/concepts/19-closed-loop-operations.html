Closed-Loop
Operations

Sensing, streaming, deciding, acting — each piece is built. Connecting them into a loop is the last step, and it introduces a class of problem none of the pieces had on their own: the system now reacts to its own outputs. Control theory calls this feedback. Operations calls it oscillation, and it is the failure mode to design against.

The loop

The operational digital twin

The twin is the platform's live model of the network: where every asset is, what state it is in, what is planned, and how far reality has diverged. It is the join of everything built so far — and it must be honest about its own quality.

Concretely: every element of the twin carries the event time it was derived from and a confidence. An asset whose last position is 40 minutes old is shown as 40 minutes old, everywhere, including to the optimizer that is about to plan around it. The twin is not a picture of reality; it is a picture of what we know about reality, and the distinction has to be visible in the data model rather than in documentation.

The device shadow from Chapter 6 is the twin's other half: it maintains desired and reported state, which is the mechanism by which a decision becomes an instruction a disconnected asset will eventually receive.1 Reported state flows up into the twin; desired state flows down as action.

Plan versus actual

Divergence is measured continuously, per movement and in aggregate. The metrics that matter operationally: schedule adherence per movement, projected knock-on delay, resource conflicts implied by current positions, and how many commitments in the plan are now infeasible.

That last one is the trigger candidate, because it is the one that means the plan has stopped being executable rather than merely imperfect.

Triggering a replan without oscillating

The naive rule — replan whenever divergence exceeds a threshold — oscillates. The new plan changes what "divergence" means; reality diverges from the new plan; another replan fires; and the dispatcher watches the schedule churn every four minutes while nothing improves.

Four dampers, used together:

Hysteresis
Trigger at divergence > X, but do not re-arm until it falls below Y < X. Prevents chatter around a single threshold.
Cooldown
A minimum interval between replans, so a burst of events produces one replan rather than ten.
Churn penalty
Chapter 17's soft constraint: penalise difference from the current plan, so the optimizer only moves what is worth moving.
Improvement gate
Publish only if the new plan is better by a margin that exceeds the cost of the disruption of changing. Marginal improvements are not improvements once you count the humans re-reading the schedule.

The improvement gate is the one teams most often omit, and it is the most effective. It reframes the question from "is there a better plan?" to "is the better plan worth the switch?", which is the question operations was actually asking.

A loop that replans every four minutes has not automated planning. It has automated indecision.

Decision provenance

Every decision the loop emits records what produced it: the input snapshot, the model and constraint versions, the solver version, and any operator overrides in force. Chapter 18 built this into the job descriptor; here it becomes the audit trail that makes the loop reviewable.

The test to hold it to: six months later, can you answer "why did the system recommend this?" without guessing? If the answer requires reconstructing what the world looked like, provenance is incomplete.

Explainability and human factors

A dispatcher who does not understand a recommendation will either ignore it or follow it without judgement, and both are bad. Explanations do not require an interpretable solver — they require reporting the reasoning you already have.

  • What changed against the previous plan, as a short diff.
  • Why — which constraint or event drove it ("block conflict at MP 186 following the 09:40 detector alarm").
  • What it costs — the objective breakdown, in operational units rather than solver units.
  • What was rejected — the runner-up option and why it lost, which is often the most trust-building item.

Watch the automation-trust failure modes in both directions. Too little trust and operators override everything, and the loop degrades to an expensive dashboard. Too much and they stop applying judgement, which is worse, because the loop's whole design assumes a human retains it.

Bounded automation

Bounded automation means the loop's authority is explicitly limited, and the limits are enforced structurally rather than by convention.

  1. Scope — which decisions may auto-apply at all.
  2. Magnitude — above a change threshold, a human approves.
  3. Rate — at most N automatic changes per hour, per division.
  4. Reversibility — never automate an action whose reversal is manual. That is the definition of unbounded automation in an operational setting.
  5. Escalation — on repeated failure to converge, the loop stops and asks, rather than trying harder.
The safety boundary

The loop closes on operational efficiency — schedules, maintenance priorities, resource allocation. It does not close on safety. Safety functions have their own independent sensing, logic, and authority, and this loop's failure — including its complete unavailability — must never remove a safety protection. Design the loop so that switching it off entirely is always a safe action.

Measuring outcomes without eroding margins

The loop will be judged on business outcomes: on-time performance, asset utilisation, energy, maintenance cost. Measure those — and measure the margins alongside them, because an optimizer's easiest source of improvement is the slack that existed for a reason.

So track buffer time consumed, inspection intervals, maintenance deferrals, and how often plans are executed at the edge of their constraints. A change that improved throughput 4% by consuming 30% of the schedule's resilience is not an improvement; it is a loan. Reporting both columns is what keeps that visible before it is repaid with interest.

Design review questions

  1. Replay a disrupted day through your trigger logic. How many replans fire? If it is more than a handful, tune the dampers before shipping.
  2. Take a recommendation your system made last week. Write the four-part explanation. Which part could you not produce?
  3. Name one automated action whose reversal is manual. That is your highest-priority fix in this chapter.

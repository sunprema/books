Connectivity, Sync,
and Backpressure

Connection loss is not an error condition here — it is an operating mode with a duty cycle. This chapter is about the three problems that follow: how a device decides when to retry, how an offline log reconciles with the cloud, and how the cloud tells ten thousand vehicles to slow down without falling over in the process.

Link modes and what each one permits

Before retry logic, classify the link. A vehicle moves between these states many times a day, and the right behaviour differs in each.

Retry, and the storm it can cause

Exponential backoff alone is not enough, and the reason is worth being precise about. If a regional outage takes 10,000 vehicles offline at once and they all back off on the same schedule, they will also all return on the same schedule. Backoff without jitter converts an outage recovery into a synchronised stampede against a cold cache.

Backoff with full jitter and a retry budget
fn next_delay(attempt: u32, rng: &mut impl Rng) -> Duration {
    let cap  = Duration::from_secs(300);
    let base = Duration::from_millis(500);
    let exp  = base * 2u32.saturating_pow(attempt.min(10));
    // full jitter: uniform over [0, exp], not exp ± noise
    rng.gen_range(Duration::ZERO..=exp.min(cap))
}

Full jitter — a uniform draw over the whole interval, not a small perturbation of it — is what actually spreads the fleet. Pair it with a retry budget: a cap on the fraction of requests that may be retries, so a struggling backend is not handed more load than the original traffic. And a circuit breaker per endpoint, so a device stops hammering a service that is clearly down and falls back to buffering.

Design the recovery, not just the outage

Chapter 2 computed a reconnect burst six times peak. Jitter, a widened drain window, and per-class draining are the three levers that make that number survivable. Decide them before the outage: during it, you will not be able to change device behaviour.

QoS classes and what they actually mean

MQTT defines three delivery qualities: QoS 0 at most once with no delivery guarantee, QoS 1 at least once which can produce duplicates, and QoS 2 exactly once at additional protocol overhead.1 The interesting fact is what industrial deployments actually choose.

They commonly pick QoS 1 combined with a paged ring buffer and per-packet confirmation tracking — accepting duplicates and deduplicating downstream rather than paying QoS 2's four-step handshake.1 That is the same conclusion Chapter 3 reached from the other direction: if every effect is idempotent under the event's identity, at least once is sufficient and much cheaper.

Separately from the protocol's QoS, your platform needs its own application priority classes, because the protocol has no idea which of your messages matters:

Class 0 — operational alerts
Sent immediately, never batched, never evicted, own connection budget.
Class 1 — high-value telemetry
Position and health. Batched briefly, delivered in order per asset.
Class 2 — routine diagnostics
Batched heavily, downsampled under pressure.
Class 3 — bulk media
Depot links only by default. Resumable, and interruptible without loss.

The rule that makes classes worth having: a lower class must never be able to starve a higher one. If media uploads share a connection pool with alerts, a video backlog will delay the signal that mattered, and it will do so precisely during the incident that produced both.

Three acknowledgements, not one

This is the section to read twice. A device that receives a single undifferentiated "ack" cannot know when it is safe to free buffer space, and will either delete data too early or hold it forever.

A useful operational signal falls out of this: acknowledgement latency is a connection-health indicator. When confirmations stop arriving although the TCP connection still appears active, gateways force a reconnect1 — a half-open connection looks perfectly healthy to the socket layer and is one of the classic silent-loss modes.

Synchronising an offline log

When a vehicle reconnects it has a log the cloud has never seen, possibly overlapping with data the cloud already has. Reconciliation rests on the two fields we put in the envelope for exactly this moment.

  1. The device reports its buffered range: seq low and high watermarks, per class.
  2. The cloud replies with the highest contiguous seq it has durably stored for that device.
  3. The device sends from there, highest class first.
  4. The cloud deduplicates on event_id and detects gaps from seq — a missing range is loss, not silence.

Note that out-of-order delivery is expected here and is fine. Because the events carry event time and a sequence number, ordering is reconstructible after the fact; insisting on ordered transmission would only make the drain slower and more fragile.

Backpressure that stops at the gateway is not backpressure. It has to reach the device, or the device will keep producing into a wall.

Backpressure that reaches the field

Backpressure has to propagate the whole way: consumer lag raises a signal, the ingest tier degrades gracefully, and the gateway tells devices to slow down — per class, so that alerts keep flowing while diagnostics throttle. The signal is usually a small directive returned on the acknowledgement path: a suggested batch interval, a class ceiling, and a validity period.

Give it a validity period so a device that loses contact reverts to its default rather than staying throttled forever. This is the same lease-shaped thinking that keeps a device shadow honest, and it is the difference between backpressure and an accidental permanent downgrade.

Dead letters and quarantine

Some messages cannot be processed: an unknown schema version, a sensor id not in the registry, a payload that fails validation. Retrying them forever blocks everything behind them. Route them to a quarantine topic with the original bytes, the failure reason, and the correlation id.

Then treat the quarantine as a product surface, not a graveyard: it needs an owner, an alert on rate-of-arrival, and a replay path once the cause is fixed. A dead-letter queue nobody reads is just a slower way to drop data.

Design review questions

  1. Simulate 10,000 vehicles reconnecting after a one-hour regional outage. What is your p99 drain time, and which class finishes last?
  2. Can your device tell a transport ack from a durable one? If not, when does it free buffer space — and is that safe?
  3. What is in your quarantine topic right now? If you do not know, that is the answer.

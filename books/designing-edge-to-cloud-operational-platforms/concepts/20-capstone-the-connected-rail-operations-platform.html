Capstone: The Connected
Rail Operations Platform

Everything assembled, in one place. This chapter is the reference architecture plane by plane, then — the part that actually matters — eight failure walkthroughs and a cost model. If you read one chapter twice, make it the failure section: the architecture is unremarkable, and the degradation behaviour is the design.

The planes

Nothing here should surprise you now. The field plane buffers and evaluates local rules; ingest is active-active and does nothing but authenticate and append; Kafka is the seam that decouples everything; hot and warm paths split by latency budget; the lakehouse holds the durable truth; twin, optimizer and vision workflow make decisions; applications consume with freshness attached.

Read the third column down the page. In every row the operator is told what is true and what is stale, and in no row does the platform present degraded output as healthy. That column, not the diagram above, is what the architecture is for.

Two rows deserve a second look. The Kafka broker loss shows nothing to the operator — correctly, because the design absorbed it, and paging a human for an absorbed failure trains them to ignore pages. The optimizer row shows the opposite: the platform continues on the last approved plan and says so, because silently operating on a stale plan is the failure that would actually hurt.

The architecture diagram is the easy half. The degradation table is the design.

The cost model

Costs for this shape of platform are dominated by storage and egress, not compute — which surprises teams who sized their budget from the ingest rate. For the Chapter 2 fleet, the rough proportions:

Lakehouse + object storage
~45%
Streaming (brokers, retention, replication)
~20%
Cellular / satellite data
~15%
Stream + batch compute
~10%
Optimization compute
~5%
Everything else
~5%

The three levers that actually move the number, in order of effectiveness: retention policy for unflagged media (the single biggest line, and a pure policy decision); edge inference and adaptive upload, which cuts the link bill and the storage bill together; and tiering, moving cold data off hot storage on a schedule rather than when someone notices.

Express the budget as cost per asset per month. That form survives fleet growth, makes the marginal cost of the next thousand vehicles obvious, and turns a finance conversation into an engineering one.

A scaling roadmap

Build in this order. Each stage is independently useful, which is what keeps the programme funded.

  1. Telemetry spine — agent, ingest, Kafka, raw lake. Value: you can see the fleet.
  2. Operational state — normalisation, position, health, the twin with freshness. Value: dispatch works.
  3. Alerting — hot path, suppression, escalation. Value: problems find you.
  4. Fleet management — enrollment, config, OTA, certificates. Value: you can change the fleet safely.
  5. Vision — media pipeline, edge inference, review queue, findings. Value: inspection scales.
  6. Optimization — snapshots, solver, validation, versioned plans. Value: planning improves.
  7. Closed loop — triggers, damping, bounded automation. Value: it sustains itself.

Resist reordering. Optimization before reliable operational state produces confident plans built on data nobody trusts, and it burns the credibility the programme needs for stages five through seven.

The safety boundary, restated

Across all seven planes: this platform observes, informs, and proposes. Safety-critical control lives in dedicated, independently certified systems that do not depend on it. The strongest statement we can make about the architecture above is that switching it off entirely is a safe action — degraded operations, but safe. Any design that cannot make that statement has crossed the line.

Capstone exercise

  1. Add a ninth row to the failure table for a failure this book did not cover, and write all three columns.
  2. Recompute the cost proportions for a fleet ten times larger. Which line grows superlinearly, and why?
  3. Pick the stage of the roadmap your organisation is actually at. What is the smallest next thing that would be independently useful?

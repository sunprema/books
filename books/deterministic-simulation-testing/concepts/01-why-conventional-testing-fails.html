Chapter 01 · Part I — The Problem & The Idea

Why Conventional Testing Fails

Here is a question worth sitting with: your unit tests are green, your integration suite is green, QA signed off, and the thing still falls over in production at 3 a.m., in a way nobody can reproduce. Why? Not "why did it break" — systems break, that's fine — but why didn't any of the testing we did tell us it could?

The honest answer is a little uncomfortable: most of what we call "testing" was invented for a world where a program is one thread doing one thing at a time, and where running it twice does the same thing twice. A distributed system violates both of those assumptions constantly, on purpose — that's what makes it distributed. So before we build a simulator to fix this, let's actually feel out why the old tools can't.

Heisenbugs: the bug that vanishes when you look at it

Physicists have a name for a measurement that disturbs the very thing it's measuring — the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. Programmers stole the joke: a Heisenbug is a concurrency bug that changes behavior when you try to observe it. Attach a debugger, add a log line, even just slow the program down a little to watch it — and the timing shifts just enough that the race no longer lines up. The bug isn't gone. Your flashlight moved it.

This isn't folklore. Researchers built a tool called CHESS specifically because Heisenbugs were eating engineering time at Microsoft: instead of hoping a bug's timing recurs, CHESS took direct control of the thread scheduler and drove the program through interleavings on purpose. It found bugs in code that had already been tested extensively by conventional means — and, crucially, once found this way, each bug reproduced on demand, because the scheduler was no longer left to chance.1

Why does ordinary testing miss what a controlled scheduler catches so easily? Count it out. Two threads, each taking just two steps, already interleave four different ways. Run a normal test and you get one of those orderings — whichever one your OS scheduler, your CPU, and the phase of the moon happened to produce that millisecond. Real systems aren't two threads with two steps; they're dozens of threads, processes, and network messages, each with many steps, and the count of possible orderings grows combinatorially — on the order of nk for n threads taking k steps each.1 Ordinary testing doesn't explore that space. It samples one path from it, blindly, and hopes the bug happens to live there.

Flaky tests: the smoke alarm you keep unplugging

A flaky test is one that passes and fails on the same code, with no changes in between. The usual suspects are exactly the ingredients of a distributed system: race conditions between concurrent operations, timing assumptions like a hard-coded sleep that's "usually" long enough, unseeded randomness, and dependence on a live network or external service whose latency simply isn't the same twice.2

Here's the trap: a flaky test isn't noise to be filtered out. It's a real bug, caught in the act, that you're choosing not to look at. "Retry until green," quarantining the test, deleting it — every one of those makes the CI dashboard calmer and tells you strictly less about your system than you knew yesterday.

Watch out A test that fails 1 run in 400 isn't "mostly reliable." It's a bug with a 1-in-400 reproduction rate — which is a much harder bug than one that fails every time, not a more trustworthy test.

Partial failure and the gray-failure blind spot

Conventional monitoring and most integration tests are built around a binary picture of health: a node is up, or it's down. Reality is messier. A gray failure is a partial failure with differential observability — the monitoring system sees the node as healthy while the application actually using it experiences it as failed or badly degraded.3 The health check pings fine. The disk still answers reads. And requests time out anyway, because the one queue that matters is silently stuck.

By construction, a test — or a monitor — built around the failure modes someone thought to check for will not see the failure modes nobody thought to check for. And as systems grow more components and more cross-component interactions, gray failures don't get rarer; the research is blunt about this — they get more common, because there are more rare-event interactions to compound.3

Key idea Partial failure isn't an edge case you can special-case away. It's the normal condition of a system large enough to have parts that fail independently of each other.

Chaos, fuzzing, integration tests, model checking — kin, not substitutes

None of this is a knock on the tools distributed-systems engineers already reach for. Jepsen-style fault injection against real database clusters is how the field learned, empirically, that widely used systems violated their own advertised guarantees — one well-known run found a Redis configuration losing 56% of acknowledged writes under a particular partition, and some MongoDB configurations treating network errors as successful acknowledgments.4 That's real, valuable evidence. But it's evidence of a different shape than what deterministic simulation gives you — and it's worth being precise about how each technique earns its keep, because DST doesn't replace any of them.

The last column is the whole story. Everything above deterministic simulation in this table trades away either "runs your real code" or "reproducible on demand." DST is the row that keeps both — which is precisely what the rest of this book is about building.

But why does "more testing" not just fix this?

Because more testing, done the conventional way, means more samples from a combinatorial space so large that you'll never sample a meaningful fraction of it — and every sample you do take, you throw away the moment the test finishes. You didn't map the space. You didn't even keep the one point you happened to visit. The fix isn't "test harder." It's: stop leaving the ordering, the faults, and the timing to chance — put a single number, a seed, in charge of all of them, so that whichever hostile timeline you stumble into, you can walk straight back to it.

Try this
  1. A test flakes once in 400 CI runs, and a teammate proposes a "retry until green" step. In one sentence, why does that make the underlying bug harder to find, not easier?
  2. A monitoring dashboard shows every node green during an outage a customer just reported. Name the failure mode this is, and explain why a synthetic health check missed it.
  3. You're handed a Jepsen report showing a database lost acknowledged writes under a network partition. What can you deterministically reproduce from that report, and what can't you — and why does that gap matter?

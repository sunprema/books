Messaging Systems

Chapter 20 ended on a question every transport in this book has had to answer sooner or later: what happens to a message once you've handed it off? A file sits on disk until something moves it. An HTTP call gets an answer or it doesn't, right now, on the calling thread. A database row is exactly as durable as the transaction that wrote it. A message broker answers differently again — it takes the message off your hands entirely, holds onto it, and delivers it on its own schedule to whoever is listening. That's the whole point of messaging middleware, and it's also why jms and kafka are two of the most important components in Camel's catalog. This chapter covers both: JMS's queues and topics, request-reply over a broker, Kafka's partitioned log and consumer groups, and how each shapes the way you write an event-driven route.

JMS: queues and topics, over any compliant broker

Camel's core jms component speaks the Java Message Service API — JMS 1.1 or 2.0, through Spring's JMS support underneath — to whatever broker is on the other end: ActiveMQ Classic, ActiveMQ Artemis, IBM MQ, and others all qualify. camel-activemq is not a separate protocol; it's a thin extension of camel-jms pre-configured for ActiveMQ 5.x, and its own documentation just points back at the JMS component for the real reference. (ActiveMQ 6.x uses camel-activemq6 instead, and Artemis is usually driven through the generic jms component rather than a dedicated one.) Learn jms once and you've learned how Camel talks to most of the broker world.

A jms endpoint URI names a destination and, optionally, its kind: jms:FOO.BAR and jms:queue:FOO.BAR are the same thing — queue is the default when no prefix is given — while a topic needs the prefix spelled out: jms:topic:Stocks.Prices. There's no silent default toward pub-sub; you always opt into a topic explicitly.

A queue load-balances; a topic fans out
from("jms:queue:orders.new?connectionFactory=#jmsCF&concurrentConsumers=5")
    .to("direct:processOrder");

from("direct:publishOrderEvent")
    .to("jms:topic:order.events?connectionFactory=#jmsCF");

That difference in prefix is the difference in delivery model. A queue is point-to-point: each message goes to exactly one consumer, so five concurrentConsumers threads (or five instances of the same route) compete for messages and share the load — the queue is doing exactly what Chapter 17's SEDA queue did in-process, now durably, out of process. A topic is publish-subscribe: every active subscriber gets its own copy of every message. A subscriber that isn't connected when a message is published simply misses it, unless it holds a durable subscription — durableSubscriptionName alone under JMS 2.0, or that plus a clientId that must be unique per connection network-wide under JMS 1.1, which is exactly the kind of coordination problem ActiveMQ's Virtual Topics exist to sidestep.

Configuring the connection: caching, acknowledgement, and delivery

connectionFactory is required — there's no default, because there's no default broker. concurrentConsumers sets how many consumer threads a jms consumer runs (default 1), and maxConcurrentConsumers lets that pool grow under load. cacheLevelName decides what Spring's listener container keeps warm between deliveries — a connection, a session, or a consumer — and it auto-selects based on transacted: CACHE_CONSUMER when transacted=false, but CACHE_NONE when transacted=true, because XA transactions need a fresh session and consumer every time. If a route uses a plain local (non-XA) transaction, setting cacheLevelName=CACHE_CONSUMER by hand gets that caching back.

acknowledgementModeName and transacted aren't meant to be combined

acknowledgementModeName (AUTO_ACKNOWLEDGE, CLIENT_ACKNOWLEDGE, or DUPS_OK_ACKNOWLEDGE; default AUTO_ACKNOWLEDGE) governs plain JMS session acknowledgement. Setting transacted=true switches the whole session into a JMS-transacted session that commits or rolls back as one unit at the end of route processing — the same commit-or-roll-back-together idea Chapter 20's transacted() gave to SQL, just now scoped to a JMS session instead of a PlatformTransactionManager. Once transacted=true is set, the acknowledgement mode is no longer what decides delivery outcome.

On the producer side, deliveryPersistent (default true) sets the JMS PERSISTENT delivery mode, so the broker writes the message to disk before acknowledging it — the JMS equivalent of Chapter 20's database commit. priority (0–9, default 4) and timeToLive (unset by default, which usually means "no expiry" on the broker) are ordinary JMS message properties exposed as URI options. On the consumer side, selector takes a JMS selector — SQL-92-like syntax over headers and properties — so a route only ever receives the messages matching a filter the broker evaluates before delivery, not something Camel filters after the fact.

Why my.custom-header still works over JMS

The JMS spec requires header names to be valid Java identifiers — no dots, no hyphens. Camel rewrites what it sends: a dot becomes _DOT_, a hyphen becomes _HYPHEN_, and both are reversed on the way back in. A header named my.custom-header round-trips through the wire looking nothing like itself, and you never have to think about it — unless you're debugging with a raw JMS browser tool and wondering where _DOT_ came from.

Request-reply over JMS: temporary queues and JMSCorrelationID

Chapter 17 named the mechanism without opening it up: jms defaults its base exchange pattern to InOnly, but auto-promotes an individual message to InOut whenever a JMSReplyTo header is present on it. Send an InOut exchange to a jms endpoint and, with no extra configuration, Camel creates a temporary reply queue, sets it as the outgoing message's JMSReplyTo, and correlates the eventual reply using JMSCorrelationID.

A temporary reply queue, created automatically
from("direct:priceCheck")
    .inOut("jms:queue:pricing.requests?connectionFactory=#jmsCF&requestTimeout=5000")
    .log("price=${body}");

Temporary queues are fast and need nothing extra configured, which is why they're the default. A route can instead set a fixed replyTo destination that every reply lands on; Camel then filters that shared queue with a JMS selector keyed on JMSCorrelationID, which works fine in a cluster but costs more than a temporary queue because of the selector evaluation on every message. replyToType=Exclusive skips the selector entirely for a fixed reply queue — at the cost of that queue name needing to be genuinely unique to the one endpoint using it.

requestTimeout (default 20000ms) bounds how long the producer waits, overridable per message with the CamelJmsRequestTimeout header. A timeout raises an ExchangeTimedOutException on the original exchange — structurally the same failure Chapter 17 described for a SEDA producer giving up on a slow consumer, just crossing a broker instead of an in-VM queue.

Kafka: a partitioned log, not a queue or a topic

A kafka: endpoint URI names one topic — or a comma-separated list, or a regex when topicIsPattern=true: kafka:orders.created. But "topic" means something different here than it did in the JMS section. Every Kafka topic is a partitioned, retained log; there is no queue-versus-topic choice to make. What decides whether messages are shared out across competing workers or fanned out to every independent listener isn't the topic — it's the consumer group.

brokers names the bootstrap servers; groupId identifies the group a consumer joins. Kafka assigns each partition of a topic to exactly one consumer instance within a group at a time — a rebalance reassigns partitions as instances join or leave — and two different groups reading the same topic each see every message independently, the Kafka equivalent of two topic subscribers in JMS. autoOffsetReset (earliest, latest, or none) decides where a brand-new group starts reading, since unlike a JMS queue there's no concept of "the messages waiting for you" — there's only a position in the log.

Ordering is per-partition, not per-topic

A single JMS queue naturally preserves the order messages were enqueued in. Kafka only guarantees order within one partition — two messages on different partitions of the same topic can be delivered out of relative order, because different partitions are consumed in parallel with no coordination between them. A route that needs strict ordering for one logical entity (every event for one order, say) has to key its producer calls so all of that entity's events land on the same partition — ordering is something you design for, not something the topic hands you by default.

Keying by orderId keeps one order's events in order
from("direct:publishOrderEvent")
    .setHeader(KafkaConstants.KEY, simple("${body.orderId}"))
    .to("kafka:order.events?brokers=localhost:9092");

Committing offsets: automatic, or on your own terms

autoCommitEnable defaults to true, committing offsets on Kafka's own schedule — simplest, but if the offset auto-commits before a route finishes handling that record and processing then fails, the broker already considers the record consumed. It will not be redelivered; that failure is now the route's problem alone, with no help from Kafka's own redelivery, because from Kafka's point of view there was nothing left to redeliver.

Setting autoCommitEnable=false and allowManualCommit=true exposes a KafkaManualCommit object on the CamelKafkaManualCommit header, which a processor calls once it has genuinely finished with a record — commit only after success, not before.

Manual commit, after processing actually succeeds
from("kafka:orders.created?brokers=localhost:9092&groupId=order-fulfillment"
    + "&autoOffsetReset=earliest&autoCommitEnable=false&allowManualCommit=true")
    .process(exchange -> {
        // ... handle the record ...
        KafkaManualCommit commit = exchange.getIn()
            .getHeader(KafkaConstants.MANUAL_COMMIT, KafkaManualCommit.class);
        commit.commit();
    });
Manual commit has to happen on the consumer's own thread

Kafka's client-side contract requires records from a partition to be committed by the same thread that polled them. Route a manual-commit exchange through an async EIP, or hand it to a different thread before calling commit(), and the risk isn't a clean error — it's a ConcurrentModificationException from the underlying Kafka client. Keep the whole poll-process-commit cycle on the consumer's own thread, the same discipline Chapter 20's transacted() demanded of a database transaction bound to one thread.

Two more knobs shape what happens when things go wrong. pollOnError (default ERROR_HANDLER) governs a failure in polling itself: DISCARD drops the batch, RECONNECT tears the consumer down and recreates it, RETRY polls again, STOP halts the route, and ERROR_HANDLER routes the failure into Camel's own error handling. breakOnFirstError (default false) governs a failure in route processing of one record: enabled, the consumer commits the current offset and reconnects so that failing record is retried on the next poll rather than the batch marching past it — though with auto-commit already in effect for everything earlier in the batch, this can't retroactively undo records that auto-committed before the failure happened.

Transactions and idempotency, Kafka-style

Setting transacted=true with a transactionalId turns on Kafka's own producer transactions, committing or aborting a batch of produced records atomically together (driven by the exchange's success, failure, or an explicit RollbackOnly). It's tempting to read that as "the Kafka version of Chapter 20's transacted()," but the guarantee is narrower: Kafka transactions are not an XA/JTA two-phase-commit participant. A route that needs a database row and a Kafka event to succeed or fail together as one atomic unit cannot get that from Kafka transactions alone — that's a distributed-transaction problem Kafka was never designed to solve on its own.

Chapter 16 listed KafkaIdempotentRepository in a comparison table alongside JdbcMessageIdRepository; here's what it actually does. Instead of a database table, it publishes each processed key as an event to a dedicated Kafka topic, and every instance rebuilds its in-memory cache of "already seen" keys from that topic on startup — finishing after either 30 seconds or an empty poll, whichever comes first. No database in the picture, but the same underlying idea as Chapter 16's idempotent consumer: check a key against a shared repository before letting a message through twice.

Not every header value survives onto Kafka

Kafka message headers only serialize a narrow set of types: String, Integer, Long, Double, Boolean, and byte[]. A header holding anything else is silently skipped, not rejected with an error — a header that worked fine in-memory or over JMS can quietly go missing the moment the same route is pointed at kafka: instead, with nothing in the logs to explain why.

JMS or Kafka: two answers to "what happens after I hand this off?"

Neither is a strict upgrade of the other — they encode different answers to the same question. A JMS queue's message is gone once a consumer takes it, which is exactly what you want for "process this order once." A Kafka topic's message stays on the log after every current consumer group has read it, which is exactly what you want when a new service six months from now needs to replay everything that ever happened to build its own view of history. In event-driven route design, that distinction usually settles the choice before any performance number does: is this a work item to be claimed and consumed, or an event other things might want to read again later?

Every transport so far in Part V has answered the same question — what happens to a message once you hand it off — a little differently. A file sits until something moves it. An HTTP call answers on the spot or doesn't. A database row is as durable as its transaction. A broker takes the message off your hands and promises to deliver it on its own terms: once, to one worker, off a queue — or forever, to anyone who asks, off a log. Event-driven route design is mostly the discipline of picking the promise your route actually needs, instead of defaulting to whichever component was already running.

So what did we actually gain?

Two messaging models, both reachable from the same from(...) / to(...) vocabulary this book has used since Chapter 6. JMS gives queues (point-to-point, competing consumers, native request-reply through temporary queues and JMSCorrelationID) and topics (publish-subscribe, durable subscriptions for offline listeners). Kafka gives one model — a partitioned, retained log — and lets the consumer group decide whether that log is shared out or fanned out, at the cost of ordering only ever being guaranteed within a partition and offset management being something a route has to reason about explicitly rather than get for free. Both reuse ideas this book already built: JMS's transacted session echoes Chapter 20's transacted(); Kafka's manual-commit thread discipline echoes that same chapter's warning about transactions bound to one thread; and KafkaIdempotentRepository turns out to be Chapter 16's idempotent consumer pattern with a Kafka topic standing in for a database table. Chapter 22 turns from these two specific components to the catalog they came from — how to read a component's options, and how to judge whether a connector belongs in a route at all.

Check yourself

  1. A route consumes from jms:topic:order.events without setting a durable subscription, then the consuming service goes offline for an hour during a deploy. What happens to the events published during that hour, and what single option would change that?
  2. A Kafka consumer route has autoCommitEnable left at its default and fails partway through handling a record after the offset has already auto-committed. Will that record be redelivered? Why or why not, and what would you change to get a stronger guarantee?
  3. Two events for the same order are published to a Kafka topic without setting a key. Why might they be delivered out of order relative to each other, and what change to the producer route would fix it?

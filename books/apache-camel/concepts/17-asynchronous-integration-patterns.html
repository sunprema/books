Asynchronous Integration Patterns

Chapter 6 made a promise it didn't keep yet: direct: endpoints call a route the way a method call invokes a function — same thread, no queue, no boundary — and when you actually want to hand a message off and move on without waiting, Part IV's seda: component and the Wire Tap pattern are built for that. Chapter 16 ended on the same open question from a different angle: three EIPs for surviving a retried, reordered, or bursty delivery, and then the admission that none of them touch the more basic question of whether your route should be blocking on the downstream call at all. This chapter is where both threads close. We'll put direct: and seda: side by side to see exactly what "asynchronous" buys you and what it costs, revisit Wire Tap with the machinery this time instead of just the idea, and turn Chapter 8's InOnly/InOut vocabulary from a fact about the Exchange into a design decision you make on purpose.

direct: and seda: are the same interface, opposite threading models

Both direct: and seda: connect two routes inside one CamelContext — no network, no serialization, both routes live in the same JVM. That's where the resemblance ends. A direct: producer calls straight into the consumer's processor chain, in the same method call, on the same thread; there's no queue anywhere in the path, which is exactly why a slow direct: consumer route stalls whatever called it, as Chapter 6's callout warned. A seda: producer does something structurally different: it builds a copy of the Exchange, places that copy on an in-memory BlockingQueue, and returns — a separate consumer thread pulls the copy off the queue and processes it independently. The producer's thread and the consumer's thread are no longer the same thread, which is the entire trick.

Same shape, opposite threading — direct: blocks, seda: hands off
// direct: — synchronous, one thread does the whole thing
from("direct:orderReceived")
    .to("direct:validateOrder");   // caller waits here

// seda: — producer hands off, a separate thread consumes
from("direct:orderReceived")
    .to("seda:notifyWarehouse?concurrentConsumers=5");   // returns immediately

from("seda:notifyWarehouse")
    .log("Notifying warehouse for order ${header.OrderId}")
    .to("http:warehouse.internal/notify");
Decoupled, not durable

A seda: queue lives entirely in one JVM's heap. It isn't shared across other instances of the same route, and it isn't persisted. If the process crashes or the route stops with messages still queued, those messages are simply gone — seda: doesn't give you at-least-once delivery the way a broker would; it only gives you a thread hand-off inside the process that's currently running. If you need the queue itself to survive a restart or to be visible from a second instance, that's what Chapter 21's JMS and Kafka components are for.

Tuning the queue: concurrentConsumers, size, and what happens when it's full

Three URI options decide how a seda: queue actually behaves under load, and the defaults are worth knowing precisely, because they're easy to assume wrong. concurrentConsumers defaults to 1 — a fresh seda: endpoint is single-threaded on the consuming side until you say otherwise, capped at 500 by limitConcurrentConsumers (default true) so a typo doesn't spin up an unbounded thread pool. The queue's capacity, size, defaults to 1000 — not unbounded, a common assumption that doesn't hold. And what happens once that queue is full is controlled by two mutually exclusive flags: blockWhenFull and discardWhenFull, both false by default.

What a seda: producer does when the queue is full, by option
ConfigurationBehavior on a full queue
blockWhenFull=false, discardWhenFull=false (default)Throws IllegalStateException immediately
blockWhenFull=trueProducer thread blocks until space frees up, or until offerTimeout elapses
discardWhenFull=trueMessage is silently dropped — no exception, no trace

Enabling both blockWhenFull and discardWhenFull at once isn't a subtle configuration — Camel throws IllegalArgumentException at startup rather than let you define a queue that can't decide what it wants to do. Pick one on purpose: block if a producer that briefly pausing is an acceptable cost for never losing a message; discard if losing an occasional message under a genuine spike is fine and you'd rather keep the producer moving.

A wider, blocking queue for a bursty upstream
from("direct:incomingEvents")
    .to("seda:eventLog?size=5000&blockWhenFull=true&offerTimeout=2000");

Two more options round out the picture. multipleConsumers (default false) flips seda: from point-to-point — one message, one consumer — into publish-subscribe: every consumer registered on that endpoint gets its own copy of each message, the in-VM equivalent of a JMS topic. And purgeWhenStopping (default false) discards whatever's still queued when the route stops, letting shutdown finish faster at the cost of dropping that backlog rather than draining it — consistent with the "decoupled, not durable" warning above.

waitForTaskToComplete: choosing whether the producer waits at all

Enqueueing is only half the story. The other half is what the producer's thread does next, and that's governed by waitForTaskToComplete, which takes three values. Never returns to the caller the instant the message is on the queue, full stop. Always makes the producer wait for the consumer to finish, regardless of exchange pattern. IfReplyExpected — the default — splits the difference along exactly the line Chapter 8 drew: it waits only if the exchange is request-reply (InOut), and returns immediately for a one-way (InOnly) exchange. A default seda:to() call on the ordinary InOnly exchanges most routes carry behaves like Never without anyone having to say so.

When you do wait — Always, or IfReplyExpected on an InOut exchange — timeout (default 30000ms) bounds how long. Let it elapse and Camel sets an ExchangeTimedOutException on the exchange the producer is holding and removes the still-queued, unconsumed copy from the queue outright — the consumer never gets a chance to process a message its own producer has already given up waiting for.

Forcing a wait, with a real timeout instead of the indefinite default
from("direct:priceQuote")
    .to("seda:pricingEngine?waitForTaskToComplete=Always&timeout=5000");
The producer and consumer never share one mutable Exchange

Whichever waitForTaskToComplete mode is in play, the producer never hands the consumer thread its own live Exchange object. It builds a correlated copy — a separate instance, with its own unit of work — and queues that. If the producer is waiting, Camel copies the results back onto the original exchange once the consumer finishes, rather than the two threads ever touching one shared, mutable Exchange at the same time. That's what makes seda: safe to use with concurrent consumers in the first place — there's nothing to race on.

Wire Tap, properly this time: always a copy, always InOnly, always another thread

Chapter 3 introduced Wire Tap as "copy a message to a secondary destination without disturbing the primary flow." Now that seda:'s machinery is on the table, here's precisely how that promise is kept. wireTap() always copies the Exchange and forces the copy's pattern to InOnly — fire-and-forget — no matter what pattern the main route is running, then dispatches that copy on a separate thread so it runs alongside the primary route instead of in front of it.

Tapping a validated order for an audit trail, without slowing the primary route
from("jms:queue:orders.validated")
    .wireTap("direct:auditLog")
        .executorService(auditThreadPool)
        .onPrepare(new DeepCloneOrder())
    .to("direct:processOrder");

Two details in that snippet aren't decoration. wireTap()'s copy is shallow by default — it copies the Exchange, not a deep clone of the message body and headers, so the tap and the original can end up sharing the same underlying mutable objects. onPrepare() is the escape hatch: hand it a Processor and it runs against the copy before dispatch, which is where a genuine deep clone belongs if the primary route or the tapped route might mutate shared state. executorService() is the other detail: without one, Wire Tap falls back to Camel's shared default async pool (10 core threads, 20 max, a queue of 1000, CallerRuns as the rejection policy).

CallerRuns means "async" can quietly become "synchronous" under load

Once the default pool and its queue are both saturated, Java's CallerRunsPolicy does exactly what its name says: it runs the rejected task on the calling thread instead of throwing it away. A wire tap you configured expecting it to never touch the primary route's latency can, under sustained load, start running synchronously on that very thread — a source of unexplained latency spikes that looks nothing like an obvious failure. A dedicated executorService with its own sizing is the fix, not a symptom to chase after the fact.

The same fire-and-forget design has a second, quieter cost: because the primary route's callback fires before the tapped copy finishes routing, an exception thrown inside the tapped route is recorded on the tap's own exchange, never the original. The primary route has no idea the tap failed unless the tapped route is given its own error handling — its own onException or dead-letter destination.

InOnly and InOut, as a decision instead of a fact

Chapter 8 introduced ExchangePattern as a fact riding along on every Exchange: InOnly for one-way, fire-and-forget delivery, InOut for request-reply, where the caller waits and a response comes back. What's worth adding here is where that fact comes from by default, because it's what makes seda:'s waitForTaskToComplete=IfReplyExpected do the right thing without any extra configuration on most routes: SEDA, JMS, and File all default to InOnly. JMS is the one exception worth knowing in detail — it auto-detects per message, based on whether the incoming message carries a JMSReplyTo header. If it does, the JMS consumer treats that exchange as InOut and the producer side honors it by creating a temporary reply queue, setting JMSReplyTo and a JMSCorrelationID on the outbound request, and matching the eventual reply back to it by that correlation ID.

Overriding the pattern is a route-side decision, and it only works in one direction from the URI: exchangePattern=InOnly or InOut as a URI parameter is only honored on a consumer endpoint, inside a from(...). Trying the same parameter on a to(...) producer URI to control whether that one step waits doesn't do what it looks like — for that, reach for .setExchangePattern(ExchangePattern.InOnly|InOut) in the DSL, which takes effect from that point in the route onward, or the two-argument form Chapter 8 already showed for a single step.

Forcing InOut on one step, the form Chapter 8 introduced
from("jms:queue:orders.raw")
    .to(ExchangePattern.InOut, "bean:pricingService?method=quote")
    .log("Quoted price: ${body}");

Request-reply versus event-driven: the design question underneath all of it

Every option in this chapter — direct: versus seda:, InOnly versus InOut, whether a Wire Tap can afford to lose its own errors — is really one design question wearing different clothes: does the producer need to know the outcome before it can move on? Hohpe and Woolf named the two answers Request-Reply and Event-Driven Consumer, and Camel's two exchange patterns are a direct implementation of that same choice, not a coincidence of naming.

Neither shape is the "correct" default — each is the right tool for a different question. Reach for request-reply (InOut, a synchronous to(), or seda: with waitForTaskToComplete=Always) when the route's own next step genuinely can't proceed without the answer: a price quote it has to embed in the response, a validation result that decides which branch runs next. Reach for event-driven (InOnly, plain seda:, Wire Tap) when the producer's job is done the moment the message is handed off: an audit log entry, a notification, a downstream system that will get to the message on its own schedule. The cost of the second choice isn't hypothetical — it's exactly the "decoupled, not durable" and "CallerRuns" callouts above. Event-driven integration buys you a caller that doesn't wait; it doesn't buy you a caller that automatically finds out when something downstream went wrong.

Async isn't a performance switch you flip — it's a promise you stop making. The moment a route stops waiting for an answer, it also stops knowing one, and every option in this chapter is really about deciding, on purpose, which of those two things you'd rather have.

So what did we actually gain?

A vocabulary for exactly one question: should this step wait? direct: always does, because there's no queue to hand off to. seda: lets you choose, per endpoint and per exchange, through waitForTaskToComplete and the pattern on the exchange itself. Wire Tap answers the question by never waiting, on purpose, in exchange for never directly knowing if the tap succeeded. None of this makes a route more reliable on its own — that was Chapter 16's job, and it still applies equally to an asynchronous hand-off, which can drop a queued message on a crash just as easily as a synchronous one drops a connection. What changes here is only who's waiting, and for how long. Chapter 18 turns from patterns for moving a message inside your own JVM to the real external systems those messages actually come from and go to — starting with files, SFTP, and object storage.

Check yourself

  1. A route sends to seda:notify?waitForTaskToComplete=Always&timeout=2000, but the exchange it sends is InOnly. Does the producer wait? For how long, and what happens to the exchange if the consumer hasn't finished by the time the timeout elapses?
  2. A seda: endpoint is configured with the defaults for blockWhenFull and discardWhenFull. A burst of traffic fills its queue past size. What does the next producer that tries to send to it observe, and how would that change if you set discardWhenFull=true instead?
  3. A wireTap() call has no executorService configured and the tapped route throws an exception under heavy load. Name two separate ways this can go wrong for the primary route that a reader who only knows "Wire Tap runs async" wouldn't expect.

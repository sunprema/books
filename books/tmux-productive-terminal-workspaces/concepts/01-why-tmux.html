Before we split a single pane

Why tmux?

Let's start with the question you're actually asking, which isn't “how do I split a terminal window” — your terminal app can already do that. It's “why do people who live in a terminal all day bother with this extra layer at all?” The answer isn't about panes. It's about what happens to your work the moment the window goes away.

The problem: your terminal remembers nothing

Here's a scenario you've probably lived through. You SSH into a server, kick off a long build, and step away for coffee. Your laptop's Wi‑Fi naps for eight seconds while you're not looking. The SSH connection drops. The build is still running on the server — it doesn't know or care about your laptop — but the terminal window that was showing it to you now just says Connection closed, and there is no way back into that conversation. You SSH in again and start over.

Or you never leave your own machine at all: you're running tests in one tab of your terminal app, a log tail in another, a REPL in a third, and then the terminal app itself crashes, or you close it by reflex, or your editor's integrated terminal panel restarts because the editor updated itself. Every one of those tabs dies at the same instant, because every one of those tabs was never really independent — it was on loan from a process that just disappeared.

The common thread: an ordinary terminal window (or an IDE's built-in terminal panel) is the only thing keeping your shell alive. Close it, lose it, or get disconnected from it, and everything running inside it goes down too. That's not a bug anyone can fix from your end — it's just how those tools are built.

Key idea

tmux exists to break that dependency. It puts your shells on a background process that keeps running whether or not anything is looking at it, and lets you attach and detach a view onto that process at will. The window is no longer where your work lives — it's just where you're currently looking.

The idea: separate the session from the client

tmux stands for terminal multiplexer, and like most good systems tools, the name undersells it. The part that actually matters is the architecture: tmux runs as a small background server process, and every terminal window you open onto it is just a client — a thin, disposable view. Your shells, your running programs, your pane layout: all of that belongs to a session, which lives on the server, not on the client. We'll get precise about sessions, windows, and panes in the next chapter; for now, the piece worth sitting with is just this split between the disposable part and the durable part.

That's also why detaching feels different from every other way a terminal session can end. Nothing is torn down. The client simply stops looking, and the server keeps the session exactly as it was — same panes, same scrollback, same programs mid-run — until a client, any client, asks to look again.

A little history

tmux was written by Nicholas Marriott and first released in November 2007 under the permissive, BSD-style ISC license, explicitly as a cleaner-codebase alternative to GNU Screen — which had pioneered the same detach-and-reattach idea years earlier but, in Marriott's view, had grown unwieldy to extend. tmux was imported into the OpenBSD base system in 2009, and its client-server split and native, layout-preserving pane splits are the two design choices that most set it apart from Screen to this day.

How this compares to what you're already using

It's worth being concrete about who owns your shell in each environment you might be coming from, because “owns” is exactly the question that decides whether your work survives the unexpected.

Environment What owns your shell Survives closing the window/app Survives an SSH drop
OS terminal tabs (Terminal.app, GNOME Terminal, Windows Terminal) the terminal app's own process No No
IDE's built-in terminal panel the editor/IDE process No No
GNU Screen a background server process Yes — detach/reattach Yes
tmux a background server process, separate from any client Yes — detach/reattach Yes
iTerm2 with tmux integration (tmux -CC) the tmux server — iTerm2 just renders it as native tabs Yes — the session lives in tmux Yes

That last row is worth calling out if you already love your terminal emulator's look and feel: you don't have to choose between tmux's persistence and a native window manager. iTerm2's tmux integration mode attaches to a real tmux session but displays its windows and panes as ordinary iTerm2 tabs and split panes — the session is still owned and kept alive by the tmux server underneath. GNU Screen earns real credit here too: it invented detach-and-reattach, and if you've been happy with it, most of this book's ideas carry over directly. What tmux adds is the session/window/pane hierarchy and native split panes from day one, which is why it's what we're building on.

Not just an SSH trick

It's tempting to file tmux under “things you need for remote servers,” but the server-owned model protects local work just as well. A terminal app that crashes, a force-quit by reflex, an OS update that restarts your login session — none of those are SSH drops, and all of them are survived the same way: the session was never depending on that particular client in the first place.

Hands-on: prove it to yourself

Three commands are enough to see the whole idea in action — we'll go much deeper on sessions in the next chapter, but there's no substitute for watching this happen once with your own hands.

# start a new, named session
tmux new -s work

# inside it: detach without killing anything
# press the prefix (Ctrl-b), release, then press d
Ctrl-b d

# later — from the same terminal, a different one, or after
# reconnecting over ssh — reattach to exactly where you left off
tmux attach -t work

Naming the session (-s work) instead of leaving it anonymous is a small habit worth starting immediately: tmux attach -t work is something you can type from memory, weeks later, without first running tmux ls to remember what you called it.

A realistic workflow

Picture an ordinary Tuesday. You SSH into a build box, start a named session, and kick off a test watcher that reruns your suite on every save. You detach and go to a meeting. The watcher keeps running — it has no idea you left. Your laptop sleeps overnight. The next morning you SSH back in and run tmux attach -t work, and the same session is sitting there: the test watcher's output is exactly where you'd expect, including every run it did while you were away. You didn't lose your place, and you didn't lose the history of what happened while you weren't watching. Later chapters build directly on this — long-running work and remote development each get a full chapter of their own — but the win in both cases is the same one you just saw here.

Try it

Start a session with something visibly alive running in it, then kill the window around it and prove the session doesn't notice:

  1. tmux new -s trial
  2. Inside it, run while sleep 2; do date; done and watch it print a fresh timestamp every couple of seconds.
  3. Detach with the prefix, then d (default prefix is Ctrl-b).
  4. Fully quit your terminal application — not just the tab, the whole app.
  5. Reopen the terminal app and run tmux attach -t trial.

The timestamps should still be ticking, with no gap longer than your own typing speed. That gap is the entire pitch of this chapter, made visible.

Common mistake

Detaching is not the same as exiting. Typing exit or pressing Ctrl-d in a pane closes that shell for real — and if it's the last pane in the session, the session ends with it. That's the opposite of what you want when stepping away. The habit to build is: leaving on purpose is prefix, then d. Nothing else reliably leaves the work running behind you.

So what did we actually gain? Not panes, not a prettier terminal — a session that outlives any single window looking at it. Everything from here on, the splits, the copy mode, the scripting, is built on top of that one guarantee.

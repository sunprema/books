Part I — Understand the Running Database

What Happens When You Ask and Then Save

Real name: Oracle Architecture — Follow a Query and a Commit

A shop's order app says “save this order.” A moment later the screen says “done.” What did the machine do in that moment, and what did it still not do?

The problem

Our made-up shop takes orders all day. One night the power goes out in the middle of a busy minute. When the machine comes back, a man asks you: “Did we lose any orders the app called done?” If you cannot say how saving works, you can only guess. This book will not ask you to guess.

Most of the trouble is that people use two words as if they were one. Let us pull them apart first.

Two things with one name

Picture a library. The books on the shelves are the part that stays when the lights go off. The workers and the desks are the part that is awake and busy only while the library is open.

  • The shelves are the files on disk. Oracle calls this the “database”: the data files, the small file that lists where everything is (the “control file”), and the files that keep a diary of every change (the “online redo logs”).
  • The workers and desks are memory plus a small crowd of helper programs. Oracle calls this the “instance.”

The workers open the shelves, and they can close them again. One set of shelves can be run by one crew at a time (in the bigger setups of Chapter 14, several crews share the shelves). When someone says “the database is down,” ask: are the shelves damaged, or is the crew just not here? The fix is very different.

Who is in the room

When the app connects, Oracle gives it a “session.” In the plain setup, one worker program is set aside for that session only: a “server process” (the “dedicated server” way; there is also a shared way, which Chapter 5 covers). That worker is the only one that touches your work. It has a small desk of its own, called the “PGA,” for sorting and holding your private things. Nobody else sees it.

The big shared table in the middle of the room is the “SGA.” Everyone uses it. Three parts matter now:

PartPlain pictureWhy you care
“Buffer cache”Copies of shelf pages, kept on the tableReading from the table is far faster than going to the shelf
“Shared pool” (with the “library cache”)A binder of questions already worked out, with the plan for eachA repeated question skips the hard thinking
“Redo log buffer”A notepad where every change is written firstThis notepad is what saves you when the lights go out

Helper programs run in the background all the time. The ones to know now: DBWn (“database writer”) copies changed pages from the table back to the shelf; LGWR (“log writer”) copies the notepad into the diary file; CKPT (“checkpoint”) keeps a mark of how far the shelves are caught up; SMON (“system monitor”) tidies up after a crash; PMON (“process monitor”) cleans up after sessions that died.

Asking a question

Your app sends a question in words. The worker does three steps: “parse,” “execute,” and, for a question that returns rows, “fetch.”

  1. Parse. Check the words make sense and that you may see what you asked for. Then look in the binder. If this exact question is already there, use that plan: an easy “soft parse.” If not, work out a new plan from scratch: a costly “hard parse.”
  2. Execute. Follow the plan. The worker needs pages: it looks on the table first; if a page is not there, it fetches it from the shelf and puts it on the table.
  3. Fetch. Hand the rows back, a batch at a time.
Why the shop's app should reuse questions

If the app builds a new sentence for every order number (“find order 1001,” “find order 1002”), every one looks new and gets a hard parse. If it asks one sentence with a blank to fill in (a “bind variable”), the plan is found in the binder. This is the first thing to check when a busy app wastes its time thinking.

Saving an order, step by step

Now the part you came for. The app changes a row (say, order 1001 goes from “new” to “paid”) and then says commit. Follow it:

  1. The worker brings the page holding that row onto the table, if it is not already there.
  2. Before changing it, it writes down how to undo the change. That note (“undo”) is also kept on pages on the table. This is what lets other people still see the old value, and lets you take it back. Chapter 2 is all about that.
  3. It changes the page on the table only. That page now differs from its twin on the shelf. Oracle calls it “dirty.”
  4. It writes a short note about what it did on the notepad (the redo buffer). The note covers both the change and the undo note.
  5. Commit. The worker asks LGWR to copy the notepad into the diary file on disk and waits until the disk says “got it.” Only then does the app hear “done.” Time spent waiting here shows up as the wait called “log file sync.”
  6. Much later, when it suits it, DBWn copies the dirty page to the shelf. Nobody is waiting for this.
A commit saves the diary, not the page.

That seems backwards until you see why. A diary is written in one line, front to back, so the disk does it fast. Pages are spread all over the shelves, and writing them one at a time while the app waits would be slow. So Oracle makes the quick write the one you wait for, and puts off the slow one. The price: for a while, the shelf is behind the truth, and the diary is the only proof.

One rule keeps this honest: the diary note for a change must reach the disk before the changed page does. (LGWR also writes on its own: about every three seconds, and when the notepad is a third full; and when many sessions commit together, one write can cover them all.)

The lights go out

Back to our night. At the moment of the power cut, some paid orders were on the notepad only, some were in the diary file, and some of the changed pages had reached the shelf. What now?

As the crew wakes up, it reads the diary from the last mark CKPT left. A “checkpoint” is that mark: “every change before here is already on the shelf.” Everything after the mark is replayed onto the pages: this is the “roll forward.” Now the shelf matches the moment of the crash, including changes that were never committed. Then the crew uses the undo notes to take back every change whose commit never made it to the diary: the “roll back.” Oracle calls the whole thing “instance recovery.” You do not run it; it runs itself when the instance opens.

The answer to the man's question

Any order whose “done” reached the app had its diary note on disk first, so it is back. An order that did not get “done” may or may not be back; the app must check, not assume.

Why do the checkpoint marks matter to you? The longer since the last mark, the more diary there is to replay, and the longer the crew takes to open. And the diary files are used round and round: a diary file cannot be written over until the pages it describes have reached the shelf. If they are small and busy, Oracle stalls to catch up. Lots of people find this part maddening, and that is fair: it is a balance, not a setting you can leave alone.

How the crew opens the shelves

Startup goes in three steps, and the middle one is a good place to stand when something is wrong. Nomount: the crew and the table exist; no shelf is touched. Mount: the crew reads the control file, so it now knows where every file should be. Open: it opens the data and diary files, and runs instance recovery if one is needed. A damaged file is found at the third step; a missing control file at the second.

Lab: follow one order through

Use a lab copy of 19c, never the shop's real one. We make a small table, change a row, and watch our own numbers move. You need one session, and the right to read the “V$” views.

Step 1: Who is running, and what do they hold?

SELECT instance_name, status, database_status FROM v$instance;
SELECT name, log_mode, open_mode FROM v$database;
SELECT group#, sequence#, status FROM v$log;
SELECT member FROM v$logfile;

The first line is the crew; the second, the shelves. The last two show the diary: which file group is being written now (“CURRENT”) and where its copies live.

Step 2: Take a reading, change a row, take another.

CREATE TABLE orders_lab (id NUMBER, status VARCHAR2(10));
INSERT INTO orders_lab VALUES (1001, 'new');
COMMIT;

-- baseline for this session
SELECT n.name, s.value FROM v$mystat s JOIN v$statname n USING (statistic#)
WHERE n.name IN ('redo size','user commits','session logical reads');

UPDATE orders_lab SET status = 'paid' WHERE id = 1001;
-- run the V$MYSTAT query again: redo size has grown, user commits has not
COMMIT;
-- run it a third time: user commits is one higher

Between the update and the commit, “redo size” already grows (the notepad is filling) but “user commits” has not moved. After the commit it does. That is steps 3 and 4 of the story, counted.

Step 3: Watch the diary move on.

SELECT group#, sequence#, status FROM v$log;
ALTER SYSTEM SWITCH LOGFILE;      -- lab only
SELECT group#, sequence#, status FROM v$log;

The CURRENT group changes and the old one becomes “ACTIVE” (its pages are not all on the shelf yet) and then, after a checkpoint, “INACTIVE.” Run ALTER SYSTEM CHECKPOINT; and look once more to see it happen.

Step 4: Write your trace. Fill this in for order 1001, with the name of the place each thing happened: which memory part held the changed page, which held the note, which helper wrote the diary, which file received it, and which helper will write the page to the shelf.

A misleading sign

Back at the shop, on a busy Friday the app slows down. Someone says “the disks that hold the data are slow, buy faster ones.” You look at what the sessions are waiting on, and the top wait is “log file sync.” Now you know what that is: every commit is waiting for the diary write. The pages are not the problem. Two real causes: the diary files sit on a slow or busy disk, or the app commits after every single row where one commit per order would do. Faster data disks would have changed nothing.

Safe fix: first measure how long the diary writes take and how many commits per second the app makes. If the app commits too often, change the app. If the disk is slow, move the diary files to a faster place, with the copies kept on separate disks. Check: the same wait should drop, and the order rate should hold. Undo: moving diary files is done by adding new ones first and dropping the old ones after, so you can back out any time before that. Chapters 4 and 6 show how to do that safely.

Checklist before you touch a production copy

  • Say which you are looking at: the instance, the files, or both.
  • Know whether the shop's diary files are mirrored on separate disks, and that no group is at the point of waiting on a checkpoint.
  • Do not force a checkpoint or a log switch on a busy machine to “be safe.” Try it in the lab.
  • When a commit feels slow, look at the diary write first; when a question feels slow, look at the plan and the table first.

When you know that a save means “the diary is on disk,” you can tell the man exactly which orders are safe, and why.

Part II — Build and Administer the Environment

Finding the Door When Calls Do Not Get Through

Real name: Connectivity and Oracle Net

The order shop’s house is open. You can walk in from inside and everything works. But the shop’s app, which lives on another computer, says “cannot connect.” Where did the call get lost? This chapter gives you a way to find out, one step at a time, instead of guessing.

The problem

Think of a big building with one front desk. A visitor says, “I am here for the orders office.” The desk person looks at a list, finds that office, and sends the visitor up. Many things can go wrong. The visitor may go to the wrong building. The desk may be closed. The list may not have the office on it. The office may be locked. The guard at the gate may turn the visitor away before the desk is even reached.

An app that cannot connect gives you one sad message, and the message sounds the same for all of these. Lots of people find this maddening, and that is fair. The fix is to learn the order in which a call travels, and to test each stop in turn.

The path a call takes

The program that answers at the front desk is the “listener.” It waits at one door number on the computer (the “port”; the usual one is 1521). It does not hold your data. It only knows which offices exist and sends each visitor to the right one. The offices are called “services.” A visitor asks for a service by name, and the full set of directions (where, which door number, which service) is the “connect descriptor.”

Read the figure left to right. 12154 means the app could not turn the name into directions. 12170 means the call went out and nothing came back in time, often because a gate dropped it without a word. 12541 means the call reached the computer but no desk answered at that door number. 12514 means a desk answered but its list does not have your office. 01017 means you got all the way to the house, and the house did not like your name or secret word.

Office names, not house numbers

In old days a visitor named the house itself, with an “SID” (the short name of one running house). Today, ask for a service by name, with SERVICE_NAME. A service is a name for a kind of work. One house can have many, and a home inside a house (Chapter 3) gets its own, named after the home. A visitor who asks for a house name can never reach a home inside it. Ask for the service.

How does the desk learn what offices exist? The house tells it. A background helper in the house, the “LREG” helper, walks to the desk and writes the service names on its list. You do not need to type them into the desk’s own file. This works by itself when the desk sits at the usual door number. If your desk sits somewhere else, tell the house where with the LOCAL_LISTENER setting. If the house just started, or the desk just restarted, the list may be empty for a short while. You can make the house write it again right now.

SHOW PARAMETER local_listener
ALTER SYSTEM REGISTER;
SELECT name, network_name FROM v$services;

Line 1: where does this house try to sign in? Line 2: write the list again, now. Line 3: which office names does this house offer? On the desk’s side, the same question is lsnrctl services. If an office name is in line 3 but not on the desk’s list, the sign-in is what is broken.

How the app finds its directions

The app has to turn a short name into directions. There are three common ways. The shortest is to write the directions in the name itself, called “easy connect”: dbhost:1521/orders_svc. The second is a text file on the app’s computer, tnsnames.ora, that maps a short name to the full directions. The third is a shared directory that many apps read. Which one the app tries first is set in sqlnet.ora.

ORDERS =
  (DESCRIPTION=
    (ADDRESS=(PROTOCOL=TCP)(HOST=dbhost)(PORT=1521))
    (CONNECT_DATA=(SERVICE_NAME=orders_svc)))

Walk through: the short name is ORDERS; it means “go to computer dbhost, door 1521, and ask for the service orders_svc.” A trap: the app may read a different tnsnames.ora than the one you edited. If the shell word TNS_ADMIN is set, that folder wins over the one inside the Oracle home. When the name works on your laptop but not on the app computer, compare the files on that computer.

One helper each, or helpers shared?

Once the visitor is in, the house gives them a helper. By default, each visitor gets a helper of their own, the “dedicated server.” The other way is the “shared server”: visitors talk to a few “dispatchers,” who pass the work to a small pool of shared helpers. Shared helpers save room when thousands sit idle, but a long job can hold a helper while others wait, and some admin jobs need their own helper. Most shops use dedicated, and add (SERVER=DEDICATED) to a descriptor when they must be sure. See which one a visitor got in the SERVER column of v$session.

Who may come in, and can others listen?

After the desk sends the visitor in, the house checks the name and secret word. That check can fail on its own (01017), or because the account is locked or its secret word ran out. This is a different problem from a lost call, and the error number says so.

By default, a call on the road can be heard by someone on the same road. Two ways to fix that. The first is built into Oracle: in sqlnet.ora, you set SQLNET.ENCRYPTION_SERVER and SQLNET.ENCRYPTION_CLIENT to one of REJECTED, ACCEPTED, REQUESTED or REQUIRED. Two sides that both say “REJECTED” or one that says “REQUIRED” against one that says “REJECTED” will not agree, and the call fails. The second way is “TLS” (the same lock that web pages use): a second door on the desk, for the TCPS kind of call, plus a locked box of keys (a “wallet”) on both sides. In either case, test with the lab’s own pair of computers before you change a real one, and keep the old door open until the new one is proven.

Many apps, many calls

Apps keep a “connection pool”: a bunch of calls left open so each new job need not dial again. From the house’s side, each of those is a real visitor with a real helper, holding memory even when idle. Ten app computers each keeping 200 open is 2,000 visitors, and the house has a limit (the PROCESSES setting); past it, new visitors get ORA-00020. The other trouble is a storm: an app restarts and every pool dials at once, and the desk and the house spend their time signing people in. Add up the biggest pool sizes of all apps before you size the house, and look at who is connected with a count by machine and program.

SELECT machine, program, service_name, server, COUNT(*) n
  FROM v$session WHERE type = 'USER'
 GROUP BY machine, program, service_name, server
 ORDER BY n DESC;

This lists who is inside, grouped by the computer they came from, the program, the office they asked for, and the helper kind. A strange machine name, or a number far above the pool size you were told, is the first clue.

A way to test, in order

1House computersqlplus / as sysdba: is the house open?The house is the trouble, not the road.

One warning: tnsping only knocks on the desk. It says nothing about whether the office exists or whether your secret word works. And step 1 uses a private back door that skips the desk entirely, so “it works on the house computer” tells you the house is open and nothing more.

Lab: works here, fails there

You need the lab house from Chapter 4 with a home called orders, and a second computer (or container) to play the app. Use Oracle 19c.

Step 1: Show it works inside. On the house computer run sqlplus / as sysdba, then ALTER SESSION SET CONTAINER=orders;. Run lsnrctl status and lsnrctl services; note the door number and the office names.

Step 2: Make the app side fail. On the app computer try sqlplus app_user@//dbhost:1521/orders_svc. It will fail in whichever way the lab was set. Write the exact ORA- number.

Step 3: Read the number. Use the figure. 12541 or a long wait: go to step 4 of the table. 12514: the desk’s list lacks that name; run lsnrctl services and compare it to the name you typed. 12154: look at the name file. 01017: you are past the road.

Step 4: Test the road. From the app computer run nc -zv dbhost 1521. A refusal at once means no desk at that door. A long wait that ends in a timeout means something on the road is dropping the call, usually a firewall. On the house computer, open the door in the computer’s own firewall (for example firewall-cmd --add-port=1521/tcp) and, if a gate in between is yours, ask its owner for the same.

Step 5: Fix and test the whole path. Run the sign-in again from the app computer, then SELECT sys_context('USERENV','SERVICE_NAME'), sys_context('USERENV','CON_NAME') FROM dual; to prove you landed in the right office and the right home. Finally, on the house computer, run the session count query above and find your own visit.

A misleading sign

The app says “ORA-12514: listener does not currently know of service requested.” The DBA runs tnsping, sees “OK,” and says the road is fine so the app must be wrong. But 12514 means the road is fine: the desk answered. What is wrong is the list. Maybe the app asks for ORDERS but the home’s office is called orders with a different domain suffix, or the home was closed, or the house was restarted a minute ago and has not yet written its list.

Evidence: lsnrctl services (is the name there, and is it READY or BLOCKED?) and v$services. Safe fix: open the home if it is closed, run ALTER SYSTEM REGISTER, or correct the name in the app; do not restart the desk, which cuts nobody off but makes the list empty for a moment. Check: the full sign-in from the app computer. Undo: put the old name file back; the desk has no state to roll back.

The opposite trap: someone asked for the SID of a home. It looked like it worked in an old test against a house with no homes. Now the house has homes, and only services reach them.

Checklist for production

  • Apps ask for a SERVICE_NAME, never a SID; each home has its own service for its own app.
  • One written list of desks, door numbers and service names, kept with the build notes.
  • LOCAL_LISTENER is set on purpose, and lsnrctl services matches v$services.
  • Name files come from one source; TNS_ADMIN is known on every app computer.
  • You know which server kind (dedicated or shared) each app uses.
  • Sum of pool sizes of all apps is below PROCESSES, with room for admins.
  • Encryption settings on both sides are written down and tested together before change.
  • The six-step test is in the runbook, with the ORA- number table beside it.

When you know the six stops a call makes, a lost call stops being a mystery and becomes a short list of questions you can answer in turn.

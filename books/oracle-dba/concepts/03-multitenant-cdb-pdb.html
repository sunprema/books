Part I — Understand the Running Database

One Big House, Many Small Homes

Real name: Multitenant Architecture — CDBs and PDBs

The shop now has three order books: one for real orders, one for the testers, one for the new web team. You type a command. Which book did it change? This chapter gives you one question to ask before every command: where am I standing?

The problem

Say the shop wants three order books. Old way: build three whole houses, each with its own pipes, wires and caretakers. That costs a lot, and most of the time the caretakers sit and do nothing.

The newer way is one house with many homes inside. All homes share the pipes, the wires and the caretakers. Each home has its own doors, its own rooms, and its own people. The whole house is called a “CDB” (a container database). Each home is called a “PDB” (a pluggable database). In version 19c you may still meet a house with just one home and no walls, the old way, but that way is on its way out: from version 21c on, every new database is a house with homes.

Here is the trouble this brings. A command can now mean “fix the whole house” or “fix one home.” Mix them up and you turn off the water for everyone, or you change the test home when you meant the real one.

The special rooms

  • The house office is the “root” (named CDB$ROOT). It keeps what the whole house shares and the list of all homes. Your house-wide work happens here. Never put shop data in it.
  • The model home is the “seed” (PDB$SEED). It is locked, read only. When you want a new, empty home, Oracle makes a copy of it.
  • The homes are the PDBs. Each has its own rooms (tables and files), its own people, and its own settings where the house allows.
  • Special homes for shared parts are “application containers.” Several app homes can share one set of parts kept in an app office. We name them here and stop; they are a topic for another day.

What is shared? The memory, the helper processes, the logs where every change is written (the “diary” from Chapter 1), and the control file. What is not shared? Each home has its own data files and its own list of its own people and things. In 19c each home can also have its own old-page drawer from Chapter 2 (“local undo”); check that yours does, because later jobs depend on it.

Where am I standing?

Every session stands in exactly one place: the office or one home. Ask it, always, before you do anything that changes something.

SHOW con_name
SELECT con_id, name, open_mode FROM v$pdbs ORDER BY con_id;

The first line prints the name of the place you stand in. The second lists the homes and whether each is open for reading and writing, open for reading only, or shut (“MOUNTED”).

You can walk from the office into a home without logging in again, if you have the right:

ALTER SESSION SET CONTAINER = orderstest;
SELECT sys_context('USERENV','CON_NAME') FROM dual;

First line: step into the home called orderstest. Second line: prove it. From now on every command lands there.

Also look at who you are. A command reads like a house-wide order in the office and like a one-home order in a home. The same ALTER SYSTEM line may be allowed in both, but it does not mean the same thing. Some settings (memory size, for example) belong to the whole house and can only be changed in the office. A few can be set per home; the list of those is in v$system_parameter, in the column ispdb_modifiable. When a setting can go either way, you say which one: CONTAINER=CURRENT or CONTAINER=ALL.

Two kinds of people

A house-wide person is a “common user.” They are made in the office and they exist in every home. Their names must start with C##. A home person is a “local user.” They are made inside one home and exist only there. The shop clerk who writes orders should be a home person, in the real-orders home, and nowhere else.

-- in the office (CDB$ROOT)
CREATE USER c##ops IDENTIFIED BY "<password>" CONTAINER=ALL;
GRANT CREATE SESSION TO c##ops CONTAINER=ALL;
-- in one home
CREATE USER clerk IDENTIFIED BY "<password>";

The same split holds for rights. A right given inside one home works only there. A right given from the office with CONTAINER=ALL works in every home, including the ones you build tomorrow. That is strong, so give it to few people. Chapter 7 goes deeper.

Which door do you knock on?

Each home gets a door name, called a “service,” which has the same name as the home. The listener (the helper at the front gate; Chapter 5) knows these names. An app should name the home’s door in its connect text, never the house’s old “SID” name. If you knock on the office door by mistake, you stand in the office.

sqlplus clerk@//dbhost:1521/orderstest
-- check the doors the house offers
SELECT name, con_id FROM v$services ORDER BY con_id;

The first line walks in through the orderstest door. The second, run in the office, lists every door and which home it belongs to. You can add more doors to one home (for example, one for reports and one for the app), so you can tell their traffic apart later.

Making, copying, closing and moving homes

Build a new empty home from the model home. You must name a first home person (the “admin user”) and say where the files go.

CREATE PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev
  ADMIN USER pdbadmin IDENTIFIED BY "<password>"
  FILE_NAME_CONVERT = ('/pdbseed/', '/webdev/');
ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev OPEN;
ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev SAVE STATE;

Line by line: build the home; tell it to turn the lights on; ask Oracle to remember that, so the home opens by itself after the house restarts. Skip the last line and, after a restart, the home waits shut (“MOUNTED”) and the app says the door is gone. The paths here are only examples; if your house names files for you, you can leave out the path line.

Copy a home with one line. This is how testers get a copy of real orders.

CREATE PLUGGABLE DATABASE orderstest FROM orders;
ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE orderstest OPEN;

If the house keeps a full diary of changes (“archive log mode”) and each home has its own old-page drawer, the real home can stay open while it is copied (a “hot clone”). If not, Oracle asks you to put the real home in read-only first, and the clerks cannot write while you wait. Two warnings. A copy of real orders holds real names and real addresses, so hide or change those before the testers see it. And if the house has a standby twin (Chapter 14), a plain copy may not reach the twin; plan that first.

Close a home before you do work on its files: ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE x CLOSE IMMEDIATE. The other homes keep running. That is one of the big gains.

Move a home to another house. You “unplug” it, which writes a small list of what the home holds, then drop it from this house but keep its files. In the other house you “plug” it in, using that list.

-- old house
ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev CLOSE IMMEDIATE;
ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev UNPLUG INTO '/stage/webdev.xml';
DROP PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev KEEP DATAFILES;
-- new house: first ask if it will fit
SELECT dbms_pdb.check_plug_compatibility('/stage/webdev.xml') FROM dual;
CREATE PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev USING '/stage/webdev.xml' NOCOPY;

The check asks “will this home fit in that house?” Differences in version or settings show up, if you plug in anyway, in the list pdb_plug_in_violations. NOCOPY says the files stay where they are; use COPY if you want a second set.

Which jobs belong to the house, and which to the home?

House: start and stop everything, memory, the shared diary and control file, patching the program files. Home: its people, its tables and files, its open/close, its copies, and getting back one home to an earlier time without touching its neighbours.

Seeing the whole house

Most views that start with DBA_ show only the home you stand in. The same views starting with CDB_, when asked from the office, show every home, with a con_id column that says which one. Most V$ views also carry con_id. This is how you count space, find sessions and list people for the whole house in one query.

SELECT p.name, ROUND(SUM(d.bytes)/1024/1024/1024,1) gb
  FROM cdb_data_files d JOIN v$containers p ON p.con_id = d.con_id
 GROUP BY p.name;

This adds up the size of each home’s files. Run it in a home and you see only that home.

For sharing the house fairly, the house has a rule-maker for who gets how much work time (“Resource Manager”; Chapter 12), and each home can be given a cap on how much space it may fill. Without these, one loud home can slow the others. Backups can be taken for the whole house or for one home alone (Chapter 8).

Lab: build a home, copy it, check where you stand

Use a lab 19c house with a home called orders already inside (if you have one called something else, use that name). You need a house-wide account such as SYS. Run steps 1–2 in the office.

Step 1: Look around. Run the two lines from “Where am I standing?”. Write down the homes and their open modes. Also check SELECT log_mode FROM v$database; and SELECT property_value FROM database_properties WHERE property_name='LOCAL_UNDO_ENABLED';. These two answers decide whether a hot copy is allowed.

Step 2: Build an empty home and copy a home. Make webdev from the model home, open it, save its state. Then copy: CREATE PLUGGABLE DATABASE orderstest FROM orders; and open it. If Oracle refuses with a message about read-only or archive mode, that is the rule from the last section showing itself; read the message, then decide whether to put orders in read-only for a short time.

Step 3: Add a door and test it. Inside orderstest, add a second door name, then see both doors in the office.

ALTER SESSION SET CONTAINER = orderstest;
EXEC dbms_service.create_service('orderstest_rpt', 'orderstest_rpt');
EXEC dbms_service.start_service('orderstest_rpt');
ALTER SESSION SET CONTAINER = CDB$ROOT;
SELECT name, con_id FROM v$services WHERE con_id > 2;

The first three lines go into the home and make and start a new door. The last two come back to the office and list the doors. From another machine, connect by //dbhost:1521/orderstest_rpt and run SHOW CON_NAME. A door made this way is not remembered through a restart by itself; in a real house you would tie it to the home with the tools your site uses (Chapter 5 touches this).

Step 4: Prove the walls. In orderstest, create a local user tester with CREATE SESSION. Try to log in to orders as tester: it fails, because that person does not exist there. Then, in the office, look at everyone: SELECT con_id, username FROM cdb_users WHERE username='TESTER'; shows one row, for one home only. Last, create c##ops with CONTAINER=ALL and run the same query for it: it shows up in every home.

Step 5: Prove the scope of a change. Run SHOW CON_NAME in the office and in orderstest; read it before each of the next two lines. Inside orderstest run ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE CLOSE IMMEDIATE; check that orders is still open. Reopen it. Cleanup: close and drop what you made with DROP PLUGGABLE DATABASE x INCLUDING DATAFILES, once you are certain of the name.

A misleading sign

After a Sunday restart of the house the web app says “listener does not know the service.” The team says the gate (listener) is broken. But the gate answers fine for the real-orders home. What differs? The new webdev home. Look at the evidence first: in the office, SELECT name, open_mode FROM v$pdbs; shows webdev as MOUNTED. A home that is shut does not offer its door. Nobody had saved its open state, so the restart left it shut.

Safe fix: open it (ALTER PLUGGABLE DATABASE webdev OPEN), then save the state. Check: restart the house in the lab and see the home come up open by itself, and see the door in v$services. Undo: closing the home puts you back where you were; no data was changed.

A second sign that looks scary: a fourth home is made on an Enterprise Edition house and nothing stops you. Oracle lets a house hold three homes of your own without the extra “Multitenant” license; more than that needs the paid option, and the software does not count for you. In 12c and 18c, the free limit was one. Standard Edition has its own limits; read the licensing manual for your version rather than trust a blog.

Checklist for production

  • Run SHOW CON_NAME before any command that changes something. Put it in your prompt if your tools allow.
  • Keep shop data out of the office. Keep house-wide people few, with C## names, and give house-wide rights rarely.
  • Connect apps by the home’s door name, never by the SID or the office door.
  • After you open a home, save its state; then restart the lab house and check.
  • Before a copy: check the diary mode and local undo, hide private data, and think about the standby twin.
  • Count your homes against your license, on purpose, every time a new one is made.
  • For a move, run the fit check and read the violations list before you open the plugged home.

When you always know whether you are standing in the whole house or in one home, you can share a lot of cheap pipes without sharing a mistake.

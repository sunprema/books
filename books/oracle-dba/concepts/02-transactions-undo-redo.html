Part I — Understand the Running Database

How Many People Read and Write at Once

Real name: Transactions, Undo, Redo, and Concurrency

At noon, the shop's clerk is changing an order while the boss reads a report that counts all orders. Does the boss wait? Does the report show half a change? Oracle's answer: no, and no. This chapter shows how.

The problem

Picture one big paper ledger on a table. If the boss reads it while the clerk crosses out a number and writes a new one, the boss may see a mess. You could make the boss wait until the clerk is done. That is safe, but on a busy day everyone stands in line.

Oracle does something smarter, and it uses the same two tools you met in Chapter 1, plus a third one. Let us name them with a picture: a diary (the “redo”, which says how to do a change again), an eraser-and-old-page drawer (the “undo”, which says how to put a change back), and a clock that only goes up.

The clock that only goes up

Oracle keeps one counter for the whole database. Each time someone saves a change for good, the counter goes up by at least one. This counter is called the “SCN” (the system change number). Think of it as the number on a ticket machine at a store: every saved change gets a later ticket than the one before.

Every page of the ledger remembers the ticket number of its last change. Every question you ask also gets a ticket number when it starts. That is how Oracle can tell “this page changed after you began asking.”

SELECT current_scn FROM v$database;

This line asks the clock for its number right now. Run it twice with a commit in between and the number is higher.

Diary versus drawer

Both are written whenever a row changes, but they are for opposite jobs.

Here is the part people miss: the drawer itself is also written into the diary. So after a crash, Oracle replays the diary forward (the drawer comes back too), and then uses the drawer to take back every change that was never saved for good. You saw this in Chapter 1 as the two halves of crash repair.

Readers and writers do not wait for each other

This is called “read consistency.” When you ask a question, Oracle notes your ticket number. For each page it reads, if the page is newer than your ticket, Oracle makes a copy and uses the drawer to put it back to how it looked at your ticket. You see one clean picture of the ledger from one moment, even if it changes while you read. Nobody is stopped.

By default that moment is when each question began. That is the normal way, called “statement-level” consistency. If you need all the questions in one piece of work to see the same moment, you can ask for that: SET TRANSACTION READ ONLY, or the stricter “serializable” way, which can refuse a change with error 08177 if the ledger moved under you.

What this means for the shop

A long report does not slow down the clerks, and the clerks do not slow down the report. The price is paid in the drawer: the longer the report, the longer the old pages must be kept.

When two people want the same row

Readers never wait. Two writers on the same row must. When a clerk changes a row, that row gets a lock, like a “being written” sign on one line of the ledger. The sign stays until the clerk saves (“commit”) or takes it all back (“rollback”). A second clerk who wants that same line must wait. Other lines are free, and Oracle does not turn many line signs into one big sign on the whole page or table.

Lots of people find this part maddening, and that is fair: the wait has no end of its own. If the first clerk walks away for lunch with the change unsaved, the second waits all lunch. The database is doing exactly what it should; the fault is the open piece of work.

Two clerks, each holding what the other wants

Clerk A holds line 1 and wants line 2. Clerk B holds line 2 and wants line 1. Both wait forever. This is a “deadlock.” Oracle spots it in a few seconds, takes back the last statement of one clerk (not the whole piece of work) and sends that clerk error 00060. It also writes a trace file and a note in the alert log. Your app has to be ready for that error.

The fix lives in the app, not the database: touch rows in the same order every time, keep each piece of work short, and give foreign-key columns an index so one clerk's change does not lock more than it should.

The drawer is not endless

The drawer is a fixed amount of space, the “undo tablespace.” Its pages come in three kinds:

  • Active: belongs to work not yet saved. It can never be thrown away, because a rollback needs it.
  • Unexpired: from saved work, but younger than the “undo retention” target (a number of seconds you set). Oracle tries to keep it, but may reuse it if it must.
  • Expired: saved and old enough. First to be reused.

Now see the trap. The boss starts a report that runs for two hours. The clerks save thousands of changes in that time, and the drawer fills with new things. If the pages the boss needs have been reused, the boss gets error 01555, “snapshot too old.” The report fails, late, after hours of work. Your retention number is a hope, not a promise, unless you set the space to “retention guarantee”. That keeps the old pages but may stop clerks from saving when the drawer is full (error 30036). You are choosing who gets hurt.

A common self-made 01555

An app reads rows with a cursor and saves after every row it changes in that same table. Each save lets Oracle reuse the pages the cursor is about to need. Save once at the end of a piece of work, not inside the loop that is reading.

Lab: make a wait, find the waiter, watch the drawer

Use a lab 19c. You need two sessions (two windows), called A and B, and rights to read the “V$” views from a third window, C, or from B.

Step 1: Set the table (session A).

CREATE TABLE orders_lab (id NUMBER PRIMARY KEY, status VARCHAR2(10));
INSERT INTO orders_lab VALUES (1001, 'new');
INSERT INTO orders_lab VALUES (1002, 'new');
COMMIT;

Step 2: Hold a line, and try to take it. In A, change 1001 and do not save. In B, try the same row: it hangs. Also in B, in another window, read the row: it does not hang, and shows ‘new’.

-- session A
UPDATE orders_lab SET status = 'paid' WHERE id = 1001;

-- session B (hangs)
UPDATE orders_lab SET status = 'shipped' WHERE id = 1001;

A change to row 1002 from B would go through at once. That shows the sign is on one line only.

Step 3: Find who is waiting and who is holding (session C).

SELECT sid, serial#, username, blocking_session, final_blocking_session,
       event, seconds_in_wait, sql_id
  FROM v$session
 WHERE blocking_session IS NOT NULL;

The waiting session shows the wait “enq: TX - row lock contention” and, in blocking_session, the id of A. If many wait in a chain, final_blocking_session points at the head of the line. Then look at what the holder is: its sql_id may be empty because it is idle, doing nothing but holding the sign. Look at its status, last_call_et and prev_sql_id before you decide anything.

Step 4: Free the line the safe way. First ask the owner of A to save or roll back. Only if that is not possible, and you are sure, end the session. Ending it takes back all its unsaved work, and that can take as long as the work did.

ALTER SYSTEM KILL SESSION 'sid,serial#';   -- values from step 3; lab only until you are sure

Roll back in A instead, and watch B's update go through.

Step 5: Make a deadlock. In A update 1001; in B update 1002; then in A update 1002 (waits); then in B update 1001. After a few seconds one session gets error 00060. Which one was it, and was its earlier change kept? (It was: only the last statement was taken back.)

Step 6: Look at the drawer.

SHOW PARAMETER undo
SELECT tablespace_name, retention FROM dba_tablespaces WHERE contents = 'UNDO';

SELECT status, ROUND(SUM(bytes)/1024/1024) mb
  FROM dba_undo_extents GROUP BY status;

SELECT begin_time, undoblks, txncount, maxquerylen,
       ssolderrcnt, nospaceerrcnt, tuned_undoretention
  FROM v$undostat ORDER BY begin_time DESC FETCH FIRST 12 ROWS ONLY;

The first two lines show your retention target and whether it is guaranteed. The third shows how much of the drawer is active, unexpired and expired. The last shows one row per ten minutes: how much drawer was used, the longest question in seconds (maxquerylen), and how many times a question failed for old pages (ssolderrcnt) or a write failed for space (nospaceerrcnt).

Last, as a small proof of read consistency: in B, run SELECT status FROM orders_lab WHERE id = 1001 while A still holds an unsaved change. You get ‘new’, built from the drawer. Then ask for the past: SELECT * FROM orders_lab AS OF TIMESTAMP SYSTIMESTAMP - INTERVAL '5' MINUTE. It uses the very same drawer, so it reaches back only as far as the drawer remembers.

A misleading sign

At month end the shop's night report starts failing with 01555 about every other night, and the team says: “the report has a bug.” The report is the same as last month. What changed? A new job now loads orders all night and saves in small bites. Look at the evidence before you touch anything: the alert log lists each failure with the question's id and how long it ran; v$undostat shows maxquerylen climbing past tuned_undoretention on those nights, and ssolderrcnt above zero. The report was fine; the drawer got too small for its new neighbour.

Safe fix, in order of kindness: make the report faster or split it, so it is shorter than the drawer's memory; then make the drawer bigger and set the retention above the longest question, with some room to spare; use the guarantee only if losing a report is worse than blocking the loader. Check: ssolderrcnt stays at zero through a month-end night and nospaceerrcnt stays at zero too. Undo: the retention number can be put back with one command, and space added to the undo area can be left as is.

Checklist for production

  • Keep pieces of work short, and always end them with a save or a rollback; an idle open piece of work holds signs and drawer pages.
  • Do not save inside a loop that is reading the same table.
  • Have the app catch error 00060 and try again; keep a standard row order.
  • Before killing a session, learn what it holds and how much it must take back.
  • Know your longest question (maxquerylen) and make the retention bigger than it. Check it again after any new job.
  • Know whether the undo space has the guarantee, and who gets hurt if it fills.

When you know that readers use old pages from the drawer and writers use signs on lines, you can say who waits, who fails, and why.

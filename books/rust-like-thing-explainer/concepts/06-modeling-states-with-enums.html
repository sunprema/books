A Box That Can Only Hold One Kind of Thing at a Time

Rust calls this: enums — and two of them, Option and Result, you'll use every single day

So far, every box you've met holds one kind of thing, always: a number, a word, a whole other box. This chapter is about boxes that can honestly be more than one shape — but only ever one shape at a time, chosen from a short, known list. Rust has a name for that kind of box, and it turns out to be one of the most useful ideas in the whole language.

Here's the problem

Picture the door of a small shop. The owner wants the door to say one thing: open, closed, or "back at a certain time." So she starts pinning up signs. A green "OPEN" sign. A red "CLOSED" sign. A little paper clock that says "BACK AT 3:15." Nothing stops her from thumbtacking all three to the door at once. Now a customer walks up and has no idea what's actually true. Is the shop open? Closed? Back soon? The door is trying to say three things, and none of them agree.

Code has this exact problem. Say you track a shop with three separate little boxes: is_open, is_closed, and back_at (which might hold a time, or might hold nothing). Nothing in the code stops is_open and is_closed from both being true at once, or from back_at holding a time while is_open also says true. Every one of those loose little boxes is honest on its own — and together, they can still tell a lie.

The fix: one frame, one sign at a time

Rust's answer is to give the door exactly one frame, built to hold exactly one sign. You write down, ahead of time, the whole short list of signs that are allowed to go in that frame — and the frame will never, ever hold more than one at once. Rust calls this kind of box a "enum," short for "enumeration," which just means "a list of the things this is allowed to be."

rust — a door that can only show one sign at a time

enum ShopSign {
    Open,
    Closed,
    BackAt(u8),   // carries a number — minutes from now
}

let door = ShopSign::BackAt(15);

Look closely at that middle line. BackAt isn't just a name — it's a sign with a little clock built right into it, holding a number. You couldn't do that with a plain list of names. This is the part that makes an enum more than just a fancy label: each sign on the list can carry its own extra information, right along with it, and different signs can carry completely different kinds of information, or none at all.

And door is never secretly two signs at once. At any given moment it is exactly one of Open, Closed, or BackAt(some number) — never all three, never a mix. The frame simply has no room for a second sign. Rust calls each entry on the list — Open, Closed, BackAt — a "variant." The enum names the frame; the variants are the signs that are allowed to sit inside it.

A frame that's allowed to be empty: Option

Now picture the shop's tip jar by the register. Most of the time there's a coin in it. Sometimes — first thing in the morning — there isn't. You need a box that can honestly say "here's a coin" or "there is nothing here," and never quietly pretend an empty jar has a coin in it. Rust has exactly that box, already built, and it turns out to be just another enum:

rust — the actual definition, straight from Rust's own toolbox

enum Option<T> {
    None,
    Some(T),
}

That's it. No magic, no special "null" hiding anywhere — just an enum with two signs: None ("empty, for real") and Some ("here's a coin, and here it is"), able to hold any kind of coin you like. This box is called "Option," and it's everywhere in Rust — anytime a value might honestly be missing, its box is an Option.

rust — a tip jar that might really be empty

let tip_jar: Option<u32> = Some(25);   // a 25-cent coin, right there
let before_open: Option<u32> = None;   // truly nothing — not zero, not a guess

Here's the small, important thing: Option<u32> and a plain u32 are two different kinds of box, on purpose. You cannot reach into an Option and use the coin inside as if it were an ordinary number, without first checking whether there's really a coin in there at all. Rust's compiler — the strict friend from earlier chapters — insists on that check, every time, before your program is even allowed to run. Plenty of other languages let you reach for a value that might not be there and only find out it was empty when the program crashes, mid-run, in front of a real customer. Rust moves that whole worry to before you ever ship the code.

The whole idea

"Might be missing" is not a special case Rust papers over — it's just another shape a value is allowed to be, written right into its type. If a value can honestly be absent, its box says so, and the compiler makes sure you deal with that before anything else.

A frame for bad news, too: Result

One more sign is worth a quick look, because you'll meet it constantly: "Result." Where Option is about a value that might simply be missing, Result is about a job that might succeed or might genuinely fail — like trying to open the shop's cash register and finding it jammed. It's built the very same way, just with two signs that both carry something:

rust — success or failure, both carrying their own details

enum Result<T, E> {
    Ok(T),    // it worked — here's what you get
    Err(E),   // it didn't — here's why
}

Notice the pattern: Option and Result aren't two different ideas — they're the same trick, one small step apart. Both are just enums with a short, fixed list of signs, and both make Rust force you to look before you reach for what's inside. Result gets a whole chapter of its own later, once you've seen how to read a sign back out of its frame — which is exactly where we're headed next.

Reading the sign back out

Putting a sign in the frame is only half the job — you also need a way to ask "which sign is it right now, and what's written on it?" Rust's tool for that is called "match." You hand it every sign the frame could possibly hold, one by one, and it runs the matching bit of code for whichever one is actually there:

rust — checking every sign the door could show

fn greet_customer(sign: ShopSign) {
    match sign {
        ShopSign::Open => println!("come on in!"),
        ShopSign::Closed => println!("sorry, we're closed."),
        ShopSign::BackAt(minutes) => {
            println!("back in {} minutes.", minutes);
        }
    }
}

Look at that third line: BackAt(minutes) doesn't just notice which sign is hanging — it reaches in and pulls the number right out, into a name you can use, in the very same breath. And here's the part that's genuinely, wonderfully strict: if you left out one of the three signs — forgot to write what happens for Closed, say — Rust simply refuses to compile your program at all. It won't guess. It won't quietly do nothing. It stops you, right there, and tells you exactly which sign you forgot. The next chapter is about that promise in full — for now, just notice that it exists.

There's also a shorter tool, for when you only care about one sign and truly don't mind ignoring the rest — it's called if let, and it reads almost like plain English:

rust — checking for just the one sign you care about

if let ShopSign::BackAt(minutes) = door {
    println!("only {} minutes left to wait", minutes);
}

That's genuinely handy — less to type, less to read. But be honest with yourself about the trade: if let quietly drops the strict friend's promise to check every sign. Reach for match when you actually need every case covered, and save if let for the moments you truly only care about one.

Why this is worth it

Here's the part that can feel like a chore at first: writing out every single sign, every single time you read one back, is more typing than one loose bool ever asked for. That annoyance is real, and it doesn't fully vanish. What you get in trade for it is bigger than it looks.

Remember the door with three signs pinned up at once, quietly disagreeing with each other? An enum makes that door physically unable to exist. There is no way, anywhere in your program, to build a shop that is somehow open and closed and back-at-3:15 all at the same time — because there is only ever one frame, and it only ever holds one sign. You're not trusting yourself, or anyone else on the team, to remember to keep three separate little boxes in sync by hand. The shape of the box itself won't let the lie exist.

Enum
A box that can be exactly one of a short, named list of shapes at a time — never more than one, never a mix.
Variant
One entry on that list. A variant can carry its own extra data along with it, or carry nothing at all.
Option<T>
Rust's built-in enum for "might honestly be missing" — None or Some(value). A different type from the plain value, so you must check before you use it.
Result<T, E>
Rust's built-in enum for "might genuinely fail" — Ok(value) or Err(reason). Its own full chapter is coming soon.
Match
The way you read a sign back out of its frame, one possible sign at a time. Leaves no sign unaccounted for — more on this next.

Because a value can only ever be the one shape it says it is, you stop needing to guess which combination of loose little facts is actually true right now — the box itself simply won't let you build a lie.

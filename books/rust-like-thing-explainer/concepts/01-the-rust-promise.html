The Deal Rust Makes With You

Rust calls this: the Rust promise

Every tool you build things with makes you a deal. Rust's deal is a little unusual. Before you read one more page, it's worth knowing exactly what you're agreeing to.

Here's the problem

Picture a long table at a birthday party. In the middle sits one box of art supplies — markers, scissors, glue, glitter. Ten kids are sitting around it, and everyone wants to make something at the same time.

Nobody is in charge of the box. Kids just reach in and grab what they need. Most of the time that's fine. But sometimes two kids grab the same marker at once and it snaps in half. And sometimes a kid throws an empty glue stick in the trash — while another kid is still holding the cap, expecting the glue stick to still be there.

Both of those are the same kind of accident, really: someone touched a thing that was already gone, or two people fought over one thing at the same moment. In a computer program, this exact kind of accident is the difference between "it just works" and "it crashes" — or worse, someone quietly reading data they were never supposed to see.

Three ways to run the party

Every programming language has to pick one of three ways to handle a table full of shared things.

Some languages — C and C++ are the famous ones — pick the first way: no rules. You, the person writing the program, are fully in charge of remembering what's still around and what's already been thrown away. It's fast, because nobody's watching. But it's also exactly how kids end up snapping markers: one tiny slip, and something breaks in a way that's brutally hard to track down later.

Other languages — Python, Java, and Go, among others — hire a helper. She walks around the whole time the party is happening, watching what everyone's holding, and quietly cleans up anything nobody needs anymore. This is called a "garbage collector." It's much safer — nobody reaches for a thrown-away thing. But she has to keep working the entire time the party runs, which costs time and attention the party itself could have used.

The strict friend with the seating chart

Rust picks a third way. Before the party even starts, a strict friend sits down with a seating chart and writes out the sharing rules on paper: who's allowed to hold what, when they have to hand it back, and how many people are allowed to just look at something at the same time versus how many are allowed to actually write on it. Nobody sits down at the table until the chart says they're allowed to.

That strict friend has a real name. It's a program of its own, built right into the tool that turns your written code into something the computer can actually run. It's called the "compiler." The compiler reads every line of your code before any of it runs, and checks it against the sharing rules. If your code tries to hold something that's already been thrown away, or lets two people write on the same thing at the same moment, the compiler stops you right there — before your program ever runs, not after it crashes at someone's real party.

Why this is worth it

Because all the checking happens on paper, beforehand, nobody has to walk around watching the whole time the program actually runs. Rust people have a name for this kind of deal: they call it "zero-cost." You get the safety of the watchful helper, without paying a helper to keep working the entire time the party is happening.

The same seating-chart trick even works when many parties are happening in different rooms of the house at once — grownups call a party-in-its-own-room a "thread." Two rooms scribbling on the exact same shared piece of paper at the exact same moment is called a "data race," and Rust's strict friend catches that too, before either room starts, not after both crayons meet. Rust programmers have a fond name for this part of the deal: "fearless concurrency" — running many rooms at once without lying awake worrying about the shared paper. It doesn't promise every possible mix-up between rooms is gone (two rooms can still get stuck each waiting on the other), only that the scribbling-at-once kind is caught before it happens.

Here's the part that is famously annoying

Sometimes the strict friend says no to something you're completely sure is fine. Nearly everyone who has learned Rust has argued with this friend at least once — it can feel like being told you can't sit down at your own table. That frustration is real, and this book won't pretend it isn't.

Here's what that argument actually looks like. Say your code hands one box of markers to a friend named other, and then you try to use the box again yourself, as name:

rust — trying to use something you already gave away

let name = String::from("Sam");
let other = name;

println!("{}", name); // the strict friend stops you right here

The compiler doesn't just say no. It says exactly why, and exactly where:

what the compiler actually tells you

error[E0382]: borrow of moved value: `name`
 --> src/main.rs:4:20
  |
2 |     let name = String::from("Sam");
  |         ---- this value moves to `other` below
3 |     let other = name;
  |                 ---- moved here
4 |     println!("{}", name);
  |                    ^^^^ `name`'s value is gone — used here after the move

Read that again. It isn't a wall. It's a note from a friend who already checked your work: which line gave the box away, which line tried to use it anyway, and — once you learn to read it — usually a hint about how to fix it. That's the third part of the deal: the strict friend is also trying to be a genuinely helpful one.

The promise, in three words

Fast
No helper has to keep watching while your program actually runs — all the checking already happened on paper, beforehand.
Safe
Nobody can touch a thing that's already been thrown away, and nobody can scribble on the same thing at the same moment as somebody else. Rust people call this pair of guarantees "memory safety" and "thread safety."
Friendly
When the strict friend says no, it tells you exactly why — and, more often than not, exactly how to fix it.

What Rust asks of you in return

Here's the honest half of the deal: to get all three of those things, you have to convince the strict friend before your code is ever allowed to run. That means writing things the way the seating chart expects, not just any way that feels natural if you learned to code somewhere else. Every chapter after this one hands you one more piece of that seating chart — how giving something away for good works, how to borrow something without taking it home, how long a borrowed thing is allowed to stick around, and so on. None of it is hard on its own. It just all has to click into place at once, and that takes a little practice.

The one marked exception

Every now and then, you genuinely do know something the strict friend has no way to check on paper — talking straight to a piece of hardware, for instance. Rust has a single door for exactly that, clearly marked "unsafe." Walking through it is rare, it's always visible in the code, and it gets its own chapter later in this book.

Do a little more thinking before you hit run, and your program gets to skip almost every one of the accidents that make other programs crash, leak secrets, or earn someone a 3 a.m. phone call.

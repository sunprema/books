The Strict Friend Who Checks All the Sharing Rules

Rust calls this: borrowing

Last chapter, Sam handed the whole truck to Jo, and lost it for good. That's fine when you really mean to give something away. But most of the time, you don't. Most of the time you just want a friend to take a quick look — not move in.

Here's the problem

Say Jo just wants to know how long the truck is, so she can decide if it'll fit in her backpack. Handing over the whole truck, the way we did last chapter, feels like overkill — and worse, once it's moved to Jo, Sam can't use it anymore, even though Sam never meant to give it up.

You could make Jo a whole second truck instead — a real copy, with .clone(), like we saw last chapter. But that's wasteful too. Jo doesn't want to keep a truck. She wants to look at the one that's already sitting right there, for a second, and hand it right back.

Looking, without taking

Rust has a move for exactly this: a "borrow." A borrow doesn't move the truck and doesn't copy the truck. It hands out a look at the truck — Rust calls that look a "reference" — while Sam keeps right on owning it. You write it with a small mark, an ampersand: &sam instead of just sam.

rust — asking to just look, not to keep

fn look_at(t: &String) -> usize {
    t.len()
}   // t's turn ends here — but t never owned the truck, so nothing is thrown away

fn main() {
    let sam = String::from("truck");
    let n = look_at(&sam);      // hand over a LOOK, not the truck itself

    println!("{} is still sam's, and it's {} letters long", sam, n); // sam: fine!
}

Notice the last line still works. Sam's sticky note never got torn off. All that happened was Jo — well, look_at — got to peek at the truck for a moment and then let go. When Jo's little room ends, nothing gets thrown away, because Jo was never the owner. She was only ever borrowing.

And here's the nice part: a look-only borrow can be handed to as many friends as you like, all at the same time. Sam can let Jo, Ann, and Max all look at the truck together. Nobody's changing anything, so nobody gets in anybody's way.

The one strict rule

Looking is safe in a crowd. Changing is not. If Jo starts writing a new name on the truck's side while Ann is in the middle of reading that same name out loud, Ann gets confused, or wrong, or both. So Rust draws one hard line, and it's the whole idea of this chapter:

The whole rule

At any one moment, a value can be borrowed by any number of friends who only want to look — or by exactly one friend who wants to change it. Never both kinds of borrow, on the same thing, at the same time.

A changing borrow — Rust calls it a "mutable reference," written &mut — is like being handed the one pen that's allowed anywhere near the truck. As long as you're holding that pen, nobody else gets to look, and nobody else gets a pen. The instant you're done, the pen goes back, and it's free for the next borrow — one at a time.

rust — only one pen at a time

let mut sam = String::from("truck");
let jo = &mut sam;
let ann = &mut sam;   // error: sam is already lent out for changing, to jo

println!("{}, {}", jo, ann);

And a changing borrow can't overlap with a looking borrow either, in either order. If Ann is still reading, Jo can't grab the pen yet. Rust catches this the same way, before your program ever runs — it just shows a slightly different note on your screen depending on which borrow came first.

Meet the strict friend

We've mentioned "the strict friend" a couple of times now — that's Rust's compiler, checking your code before it's allowed to run. This chapter is where that friend does its most famous work. The technical name for this part of the compiler is the "borrow checker." Its whole job is walking through your code and making sure the one rule above is never broken, anywhere, ever — not "usually," not "almost always." Always.

That's a strange kind of friend to have. It won't let a mistake slide just because it probably would have worked out fine. But that strictness is exactly the point: a program that breaks the sharing rule doesn't always crash right away. Sometimes it runs fine for weeks and then gives a wrong answer, or crashes, at the worst possible moment — usually far away from wherever the real mistake was made. Rust's strict friend would rather stop you at your desk, today, with the mistake right there on the screen.

A borrow doesn't have to hang around

Here's a detail that trips people up, and it's worth seeing once, clearly. You might expect a borrow to last for the whole room (the "scope") it was born in, the same way an owner does. It doesn't have to. A borrow is only considered "in use" from the moment it's created until the last time anyone actually reads it. The moment nobody's going to look at it again, it's done — even if the room around it is still open.

rust — a finished look doesn't block the next one

let mut sam = String::from("truck");

let jo = &sam;
let ann = &sam;
println!("{} and {} are both looking", jo, ann); // jo, ann's last use — done here

let max = &mut sam;         // fine: nobody's still looking
max.push_str(", a red one");
println!("{}", max);

Jo and Ann's borrows are finished the moment that println! line runs — nobody's holding onto their "look" past that point — so Max is free to grab the one pen right after, in the very same room. If you've heard people online complain about the borrow checker being unreasonably strict, most of that complaining is old news: this exact kind of code used to be rejected, and isn't anymore, because Rust got better at noticing when a look was actually finished.

A borrow can't outlive the thing it's borrowing

One more thing the strict friend never allows, no matter what: handing someone a look at a truck that's already been thrown away. That sounds obvious when you say it out loud — of course you can't look at something that's gone — but it's a genuinely easy mistake to write by accident.

rust — trying to hand back a look at nothing

fn borrow_a_truck() -> &String {   // error: missing lifetime specifier
    let t = String::from("truck");
    &t
}   // t is thrown away right here — the look you tried to hand back would point at nothing

The truck t is born inside borrow_a_truck, and — same rule as last chapter — it's thrown away the instant that function's room ends. Trying to send a look at it back out to whoever called the function would be handing them directions to an empty spot. Rust's strict friend refuses to even compile this, on the spot, with a note about a missing "lifetime." We haven't met lifetimes properly yet — that's the very next chapter — but you've already met the problem they solve: making sure a borrowed look never outlives the thing it's a look at.

Borrow / reference
A temporary look at a value, written &value, that doesn't take ownership. Written with an ampersand.
Shared borrow (&T)
A look-only borrow. Any number of these can exist at once.
Mutable borrow (&mut T)
A changing borrow — the one pen. Only one can exist at a time, and never alongside a shared borrow.
Borrow checker
The part of the strict friend (the compiler) whose whole job is enforcing that one rule, on every line, before your program runs.

Here's the part that is famously annoying

Even with everything above, you will still, sometimes, write code that feels perfectly reasonable and watch the strict friend reject it anyway — often something like borrowing two different parts of the same box of things, where you can plainly see they don't overlap, but the checker isn't quite convinced. That's a real, occasional frustration, and it doesn't fully go away. What does change, with practice, is how fast you can see why the checker is worried, and how to rearrange a few lines so it can see what you already know.

Here's why it's worth it. Because Rust checks the sharing rule on every borrow, before your program ever runs, a whole family of bugs simply cannot happen: two threads scribbling on the same memory at once, one part of your program reading data while another part quietly changes it out from under it, a look sent back at something that's already gone. In most languages, those bugs show up as rare, awful, hard-to-reproduce crashes, months later, in someone else's program. In Rust, they show up as a note on your own screen, today, before you ever hit run.

Because Rust lets you look at something without taking it, and strictly limits changing it to one hand at a time, you get to share freely and safely — instead of choosing between never sharing and never being sure.

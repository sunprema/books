Teaching the Rulebook to Watch Many Hands at Once

Rust calls this: Send, Sync, thread, and channel — "concurrency"

Every rule in this book so far watched one pair of hands at a time. One owner. One writer or many readers, never both, in one place, one moment. That was already plenty to keep track of. Now we let more than one room work at the exact same time — and ask whether the very same rulebook can still keep everyone honest when it isn't even in the room to watch.

Here's the problem

Picture that house from a couple of chapters back — the one with several rooms, each one able to work on its own. Now imagine two rooms working at the exact same instant, both reaching for the same toy at the exact same instant. Not one after another. At once. One room is changing it while the other room is reading it, mid-change, and neither room has any idea the other is even there.

In most languages, nothing stops this from happening. The program might run fine a thousand times in a row and then, on the thousand-and-first try, come out wrong in a way nobody can easily reproduce — because it depends on the exact, unlucky timing of two rooms touching the same thing at once. That kind of bug is called a "data race," and it's one of the nastiest kinds there is, precisely because it hides.

Rooms working at once, for real

Rust calls a room a "thread." A thread is a genuinely separate, running copy of your program's work, handed off to the computer to run alongside the others, at the same time, not one after another. You start one with thread::spawn, and you hand it a small job to do — written the same way you'd write any other block of code.

There's one extra word you'll almost always see on that job: move. It means exactly what it meant the very first time this book used the word, back in the ownership chapter — the new room isn't borrowing the toy, it's taking it, for real, as its own. That has to be true, because the room you started might still be running long after the room that started it has finished and moved on to something else. A borrowed toy can't survive that. An owned one can.

rust — starting a room, handing it a toy, waiting for it to finish

use std::thread;

let toys = vec![1, 2, 3];

let room = thread::spawn(move || {
    println!("the new room has: {:?}", toys);
});

room.join().unwrap(); // wait here until that room is done

join is the waiting part — it pauses the room you're in until the new one finishes, and hands back whatever that room's job returned. Without it, your program might finish and shut the whole house down before the new room ever got to say a word.

A stamp for "safe to carry into a new room"

Here's the part that makes this chapter belong in this book at all. Not every toy is actually safe to hand to a new room. So Rust's rulebook keeps a stamp — one single stamp, checked before your program ever runs, not while it's running — that answers one plain question: "is this toy safe to carry, whole, into a new room?" Rust's name for that stamp is Send.

Almost everything gets this stamp, automatically, with nothing for you to write. The rulebook looks at what a toy is built out of, and if every single piece inside it already carries the stamp, the whole toy gets it too. You practically never type the word Send yourself.

But remember the ticket box from last chapter — Rc, the one that hands out tickets and keeps a running tally with plain pencil marks? Pencil marks are exactly the problem here. If two rooms ever tried to update that same tally at the same instant, each one scribbling its own mark, the tally itself could come out wrong — smudged, half-written, off by one. So Rust's rulebook simply refuses to put the Send stamp on Rc. Trying to carry one into a new room doesn't run and quietly misbehave — it doesn't compile at all.

rust — a toy the rulebook won't let you carry across

use std::rc::Rc;
use std::thread;

let ticket = Rc::new(5);
let second_ticket = Rc::clone(&ticket);

thread::spawn(move || {
    println!("{}", second_ticket);
});
// error: `Rc<i32>` cannot be sent between threads safely
// the trait `Send` is not implemented for `Rc<i32>`

That's not the rulebook being difficult. That's the rulebook catching, before your program ever runs a single instruction, the exact kind of mistake that would otherwise show up as a program that's usually fine and occasionally, mysteriously, isn't. Swap that Rc for last chapter's sturdier twin, Arc — the one whose tally is a click-counter instead of pencil marks, a counting move that can never be caught halfway — and the very same code compiles and runs cleanly. A click-counter can't be smudged by two hands at once. A pencil mark can.

A second stamp for "safe to look at together"

Carrying a whole toy into a new room is one question. Here's a different one: can two rooms both keep a look at the very same toy, at the very same time, without either of them ever taking it away from the other? For that, Rust's rulebook keeps a second stamp, called Sync.

Remember the whiteboard marker from a few chapters back — the rule that any number of rooms may look at once, but only one may ever write at a time? Sync is that exact same rule, just written down as a stamp instead of a sentence. And remember the trapdoor from that same chapter, Cell and RefCell, which let one room quietly change a toy through nothing more than a look? That trapdoor works fine with one room in the house. It is exactly the kind of thing that breaks the instant a second room gets the same look, because now two rooms could both be reaching through it at once with no one keeping watch. So the rulebook refuses the Sync stamp to Cell and RefCell, permanently, no matter what's inside them.

The whole idea

Send asks: "can this toy, whole, move to a new room?" Sync asks a different question: "can two rooms both keep a look at this exact same toy, right now, at once?" A toy can have either stamp, both, or neither — they aren't the same promise, and Rust checks each one on its own.

All of this — every stamp, on every toy, everywhere — gets decided by looking at what a thing is built out of. Build a toy entirely from already-stamped pieces, and it gets both stamps automatically, with nothing for you to write. Build it out of even one piece that isn't stamped, and it quietly loses the stamp too, automatically, with nothing warning you unless you actually try to carry it into a new room. There is a rare, careful escape hatch — writing unsafe impl Send by hand, to promise the rulebook something it can't check for itself — but that's a trapdoor for people building the deepest, most careful tools, not something you'll reach for while learning. It belongs with the rest of this book's trapdoors, two chapters from now.

A different answer: don't share the toy at all

Stamps solve the "can I carry this, or look at it together" question. But there's a whole different way to let two rooms work together that sidesteps the question almost entirely: instead of two rooms reaching for the same toy, one room seals it up and slides it through a "mail slot" to the other. Rust's real word for this is a "channel."

Here's what makes a mail slot different from a shared toy: the moment a toy goes through, it's gone from the room that sent it. Not borrowed. Not copied. Moved — the exact same word this book has used since the ownership chapter. The sending room can't reach for it anymore, because it doesn't have it anymore. There is never a single instant where both rooms are holding it. Nothing to race over, because there's never a moment with two hands on the same thing.

rust — one toy, sealed up, sent through the slot

use std::sync::mpsc;
use std::thread;

let (slot_in, slot_out) = mpsc::channel();

thread::spawn(move || {
    let note = String::from("hi from the other room");
    slot_in.send(note).unwrap(); // note is moved through the slot
    // note can't be used here anymore
});

let received = slot_out.recv().unwrap(); // waits here until it arrives
println!("{received}");

send pushes a toy through and hands it off completely. recv — "receive" — waits, patiently, until something has actually arrived, then hands it fully to the room that's waiting. Rust spells this out as its own little saying: "don't talk by sharing a toy; share by talking." Handing something all the way over, cleanly, beats two rooms trying to keep their story straight about who's touching it when.

One more small, useful fact: the sending side of a slot can be copied, so many rooms can each hold their own copy and all send into the same slot — that's what Rust means calling this "multiple producer." But there is only ever one receiving side collecting on the other end — "single consumer" — so there's never a question of which room gets which note. You can even treat the receiving side like a plain list of arriving notes, and loop over it until every sending copy has been let go and the slot itself closes.

rust — three rooms sending, one room collecting

use std::sync::mpsc;
use std::thread;

let (slot_in, slot_out) = mpsc::channel();

for id in 0..3 {
    let slot_in = slot_in.clone(); // each room gets its own copy of the sending end
    thread::spawn(move || {
        slot_in.send(id).unwrap();
    });
}
drop(slot_in); // let go of the original, so the loop below can end

for received in slot_out { // the receiving end acts like a list of arrivals
    println!("got {received}");
}

Being honest about what this doesn't fix

Rust's own name for all of this is "fearless concurrency," and it's a good name, but it deserves one honest footnote. The stamps catch one very specific, very nasty problem: two rooms touching the same toy at once with at least one of them changing it. That problem, the rulebook genuinely does catch, every time, before your program ever runs.

Here's what it doesn't catch

Two rooms can still get stuck forever, each one waiting on a lock the other is holding — nobody moves, nobody crashes, the program just quietly hangs. And two rooms can still send notes to each other in the wrong order and end up confused, logically, even though nothing ever raced. The stamps were never a promise that working with many rooms becomes simple. They're a promise that one specific, ugly kind of mistake — two hands on the same toy at once — can't slip through unnoticed.

Be honest about the cost, too: reading a "the trait `Send` is not implemented" message the first few times is genuinely confusing, and it can feel like the rulebook is being difficult about a program you are fairly sure would have worked fine. Sometimes it would have. But "fine, this time" is exactly the kind of promise a data race breaks without warning, on some other run, on someone else's computer, at the worst possible moment.

Why this is worth it

Every earlier chapter in this book taught the rulebook to watch one pair of hands, in one room, at one time. This chapter is that same rulebook, stretched to watch every room in the house at once — and it does it the exact same way it always has: quietly, before your program ever runs, by looking at what a toy is actually built out of.

Most languages hand you the rooms and leave the watching entirely to you — every shared toy, every lock, every note passed between rooms, is a promise you have to keep track of by hand, forever, with nothing double-checking you. Rust writes that promise into the toy itself, as a stamp the rulebook can actually check. You still have to build things carefully. You just don't have to build them carefully and remember every rule and get lucky, all three, at once.

thread::spawn
Starts a new, genuinely separate room, running at the same time as the rest of your program. Its job usually needs move, so it owns what it uses instead of borrowing something that might not last.
Send
The stamp for "safe to carry this whole toy into a new room." Almost everything has it automatically. Rc famously doesn't — its tally isn't safe for two rooms to touch at once.
Sync
The stamp for "safe for two rooms to keep a look at this same toy at once." Cell and RefCell never get it, because both let a look quietly change the toy underneath it.
Channel (mpsc::channel)
A mail slot between rooms. Sending moves a toy through completely — never shared, never in two rooms at once. Many rooms can send; exactly one room ever receives.
What the stamps don't fix
Data races, specifically. Deadlocks and message-ordering mistakes are still entirely possible, and still yours to watch for.

Because the rulebook checks who's allowed to carry a toy and who's allowed to look at it together before your program ever takes its first step, a whole category of bug that used to hide, waiting for the worst possible moment to show up, simply can't get past the doorway in the first place.

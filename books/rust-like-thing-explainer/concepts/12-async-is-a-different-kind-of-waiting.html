Waiting Without Standing Still

Rust calls this: Future, async, and .await — "asynchronous programming"

The last chapter taught the rulebook to watch many rooms working at once. This chapter is about something almost the opposite: one room, handling many things that are each just sitting there, waiting — and not wanting to freeze solid every time one of them makes it wait.

Here's the problem

Picture a waiter with one table. A guest orders soup. The kitchen needs ten minutes to make it. If the waiter just stands at that table for the whole ten minutes, staring at the kitchen door, doing nothing else — no other guest gets served, no other order gets taken, even though the waiter isn't doing any actual work. The waiter isn't busy. The waiter is just stuck.

A room in your program can get stuck the exact same way. Ask for a page from across the internet, or wait for a file to finish saving, or wait for a timer to go off, and the room can end up just standing there, doing nothing, until the answer comes back. Call that kind of waiting "blocking." The room is blocked — stuck in place — and it can't do one single other thing until whatever it's waiting on finally answers.

The last chapter's fix — starting a whole new room for every waiting guest — genuinely works. But every room costs something: real, fixed space, kept aside whether that room is busy or just sitting there waiting. Start ten new rooms and the cost is small. Start ten thousand, because ten thousand guests all happen to be waiting on soup at once, and the cost of all those rooms — mostly empty, mostly just standing around — starts to add up by itself, before a single order has even been taken.

A different kind of waiting

So Rust offers a second way to wait, built for exactly this shape of problem: not "a few rooms doing real, heavy work," but "a huge pile of waiters, almost all of them just standing around for an answer that hasn't come back yet." Rust's word for this whole way of working is "async," short for "asynchronous" — a fancy word for "not all happening in lockstep."

The idea is simple to say and a little strange the first time you see it: instead of one waiter per table, standing there the whole ten minutes, one waiter now covers every table. The moment an order goes to the kitchen, the waiter doesn't stand and stare — the waiter walks away and checks on someone else. Only when the soup is actually ready does the waiter come back to that table at all. Nobody is ever blocked standing at one spot. The waiting still happens — it just happens off to the side, instead of freezing the one waiter who could have been useful somewhere else.

A ticket that says "not ready yet"

Here's the piece that makes this actually work instead of just sounding nice: Rust needs some way to hand the waiter a note that says "this order isn't done — check back later," instead of the waiter just standing there hoping. That note is a real thing in Rust, with a real name: a "future." A future is a value that stands in for "an answer that isn't here yet, but will be, eventually."

Write a function with the word async in front of it, and calling that function doesn't run the function at all — not yet. It just hands back a future: a ticket saying "this work is described, but hasn't started." Nothing in the function's body actually runs until something later on checks that ticket and says "go." This surprises almost everybody the first time: in most languages, calling a function runs it right away. In Rust's async world, calling an async function is just handing out the ticket.

rust — calling this does nothing by itself

async fn get_soup() -> String {
    // this line does not run yet, just because get_soup() was called
    println!("kitchen is starting the soup...");
    String::from("tomato soup")
}

let ticket = get_soup(); // nothing has run yet — just a ticket, a "future"

So how does the ticket ever turn into an actual bowl of soup? You add the word .await after it. That's the whole trick, and it's why this chapter's title calls it a different kind of waiting rather than "waiting, but written differently." Writing .await does not freeze the room the way blocking did. Instead, it checks the ticket: is the soup ready? If yes, take it and move on immediately. If not, step aside — hand the room over to whatever else is waiting on a table — and come back to check this ticket again the moment there's real news.

rust — the word that actually does the waiting

async fn serve_table() {
    let soup = get_soup().await; // pauses here — but doesn't freeze the room
    println!("served: {soup}");
}

The whole idea

Calling an async function only writes out a ticket — a "future" — for work that hasn't started. .await is what actually asks "is it ready yet?" If it isn't, the room steps aside to check on someone else instead of standing frozen, and comes back the instant this ticket has real news.

Somebody has to actually run the tickets

One more honest piece, because pretending it away would just cause confusion later: a ticket, by itself, does nothing. Something has to go around actually checking tickets, over and over, moving each one forward the instant it can — and deciding, when a room is free, which waiting table to check on next. Rust's word for that something is a "runtime."

Here's the part that surprises people: Rust itself doesn't come with one built in. The language gives you async and .await and the ticket system underneath them — but the thing that actually walks around checking every ticket is a separate tool you add to your project, most commonly one called tokio. Without a runtime running somewhere, tickets just sit there, written out, never checked, and no soup ever gets served.

The one way this waiting still trips people up

There's a catch, and it's worth saying out loud rather than pretending async waiting is magic. The waiter can only step aside at the exact moment it checks a ticket — an .await. If part of a waiter's own job takes real, honest time — chopping vegetables right there at the table instead of sending the order to the kitchen, say — the waiter that whole time is genuinely busy, and every other table is stuck waiting on this one waiter, with no ticket-checking happening at all.

A real trap

The classic version of this mistake: writing the old, blocking kind of "wait ten minutes" — std::thread::sleep — inside an async function. It compiles. It runs. And it quietly freezes every other table this same room was supposed to be checking on, because the room never stepped aside to check a ticket — it just stood there, blocking, exactly like the very thing async was supposed to fix. The right tool is a version of "wait ten minutes" built to be checked as a ticket instead — most runtimes provide one, and you .await it just like any other ticket.

This isn't a flaw hiding in the design — it's the honest price of the whole idea. One waiter covering many tables only works if that waiter keeps actually stepping aside. Async doesn't make waiting free. It moves the waiting off to the side, out of the way, so it stops costing you a whole frozen room every time.

One word you'll see and can mostly ignore

Sooner or later, chasing a confusing compiler message, you might run into the word Pin. Here, in one short paragraph, is all you really need: when Rust turns your async function into its ticket, it sometimes has to keep a note inside that ticket that points at another part of the very same ticket. That's fine, as long as the ticket never gets physically moved somewhere else in memory — move it, and the note would be left pointing at the wrong spot. Pin is Rust's way of promising "this ticket is staying exactly where it is." You will almost never write Pin yourself — the compiler and the runtime handle it quietly, behind the scenes, every time you write a plain .await. It's mentioned here only so the word doesn't seem like a mystery if it ever crosses your screen.

Why this is worth it

A room with its own thread costs real space, kept aside, whether it's busy or just standing around — and a program with ten thousand waiting guests would need ten thousand of those rooms just sitting there, mostly idle, mostly waiting. Async tickets cost only a sliver of that, because they're not rooms at all — they're notes, checked in turn by however many real rooms you actually have. That's the whole payoff: the same handful of rooms can now cover an enormous pile of "waiting on an answer" work, instead of needing one expensive room for every single guest.

Threads, from the last chapter, and async, from this one, aren't rivals — they're two different tools for two different shapes of work. Reach for a thread when a job genuinely needs to keep the processor busy doing real work. Reach for async when a job spends almost all its time just standing around waiting for an answer to come back. Most real programs end up using a little of both, and now you know which shape of waiting each one is actually built for.

Blocking
Standing frozen at one spot until an answer comes back, unable to do anything else in the meantime. The problem this whole chapter exists to fix.
Future
A ticket for an answer that isn't here yet. Calling an async function only writes this ticket out — it does not start the work.
.await
Checks a ticket. Ready: take the answer and move on. Not ready: step aside to check on something else, and come back the instant there's real news — no frozen, standing wait.
Runtime (e.g. tokio)
The thing that actually walks around checking tickets and deciding what to work on next. Rust's language doesn't include one — a real program adds one as a separate tool.
Pin
A quiet promise, handled for you almost all the time, that a ticket holding a note pointing at its own insides won't be moved somewhere else in memory.

Because waiting gets handed off instead of freezing a whole room solid, a single, ordinary handful of rooms can now cover more waiting guests than a thousand rooms could ever afford to sit around for — and every one of those guests still gets served the moment their soup is actually ready.

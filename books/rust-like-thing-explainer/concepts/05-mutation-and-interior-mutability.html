Only One Hand on the Marker at a Time

Rust calls this: mutation and interior mutability

You've now seen the strict friend's whole rulebook for looking and changing: any number of hands can look at once, but only one hand may ever hold the marker that's allowed to write on something. This chapter is about that rule up close — why Rust makes it so strict, and about a small, careful trapdoor Rust leaves in the wall for the times the rule gets in your way for no good reason.

Here's the problem

Picture a whiteboard everyone in the room can see. If two people grab markers at the same moment and both start changing the same word, you get a mess — half of one edit, half of the other, and nobody can say what the board is actually supposed to say anymore. That's not a made-up worry. It's exactly what happens inside a running program when two parts of the code change the same piece of data at the same time: the final answer depends on pure luck about which change landed last.

So Rust hands out exactly one marker for any piece of data, at any one moment. You met the rule already in the last chapter: a value can be looked at by any number of hands, or changed by exactly one hand — never both, never two changing hands at once. This chapter is about what that really buys you, and about the cases where you genuinely need more than one hand near the marker, and how Rust lets you have that, safely, without breaking its own promise.

Changing is the special, watched action

In most languages, you can change almost anything, almost anywhere, just by writing to it. Rust flips that around. By default, a value can't be changed at all — not even by its own owner — unless you say so out loud with the small word mut. And even a mut owner can only be changed through one hand at a time, the same "one marker" rule from last chapter, now applied to plain ownership too.

rust — nothing changes unless you say mut

let price = 10;
price = 12;              // error: price isn't marked as changeable

let mut price = 10;      // now it's a changeable box
price = 12;              // fine — one hand, one owner, one change

This is why we call it "mutation." It just means changing a value after it's already been made. Rust doesn't forbid it — it just makes you write down, right where the value is born, whether it's ever allowed to happen. That one small word tells every reader of the code, and the strict friend, exactly which boxes might change later and which ones never will.

Why the rule is worth the trouble

Here's the part that's genuinely, famously annoying: sometimes you know, in your bones, that a piece of code is safe — two hands reaching for two different corners of the same whiteboard, say — and the strict friend still says no, because from where it's standing, it can't be sure the corners don't overlap. That friction is real, and it doesn't fully go away with practice. What you get better at is seeing why the friend is worried, and reshaping the code so it can see what you already know.

But step back and look at what "one marker at a time" actually buys you: it makes an entire family of bugs simply impossible to write. No part of your program can ever quietly change a value while another part is in the middle of reading it. No two threads can ever scribble on the same box at once by accident. In most languages, that class of mistake shows up as a rare, awful, hard-to-reproduce bug, weeks later, in someone else's program. In Rust, the strict friend catches it before your program is even allowed to run.

The trapdoor: a marker that lives inside the box

Sometimes the "one marker" rule really does get in the way for a reason that isn't about safety at all — it's about the shape of your code. Say you have a box that lots of other code only ever gets to look at, never change — but somewhere deep inside that box there's one small counter you genuinely need to bump every so often, like a little tally-mark tracker taped to the inside lid. The outside rule says "look only." The one thing inside the box says "let me change, just this one small part, just sometimes."

Rust has a small, careful trapdoor for exactly this: a box that keeps its own tiny rulebook inside itself, instead of leaving all the checking to the strict friend at the door. Rust calls this "interior mutability." It sounds fancy, but the picture is simple: instead of the strict friend checking the sharing rule once, before your program runs, the box itself checks the rule every single time someone reaches in — while your program is actually running.

The whole idea

Normally, whether you're allowed to change something is decided once, ahead of time, by reading your code. With interior mutability, a special kind of box decides it fresh, every time, while the program is running — by keeping its own little sign-in sheet of who's currently looking or changing.

The plainest trapdoor: a box with no sign-in sheet at all

The simplest version of this trapdoor is a small box called "Cell." A Cell doesn't hand out a look at what's inside it at all — so there's nothing for two hands to fight over. Instead, you either take a full copy of the small thing inside (get), or you swap in a whole new thing and throw the old one away (set). Since nobody's ever holding onto a look while someone else changes it, there's no sign-in sheet to keep, and no way for two hands to collide.

rust — a box you get and set, never peek inside directly

use std::cell::Cell;

let hits = Cell::new(0);
let counter_a = &hits;   // just a look at the box itself...
let counter_b = &hits;   // ...and here's a second look, at the same time

counter_a.set(counter_a.get() + 1);  // change it, through a mere look!
counter_b.set(counter_b.get() + 1);

println!("{}", hits.get());  // 2

Notice what just happened: counter_a and counter_b are both plain, look-only borrows — the kind that used to mean "you may never change this" — and yet .set() worked through both of them. That's the whole point of the trapdoor. Cell is only allowed to work this way because it never hands out a real look at the value sitting inside it; it only ever hands the whole thing over, or swaps in a new one. Small numbers, flags, and other tiny, cheap-to-copy things are the natural fit for Cell.

The busier trapdoor: a box that checks its own sign-in sheet

But sometimes you really do want a proper look inside the box — at a list, a piece of text, a whole other struct — not just a copy-out, swap-in deal. For that, Rust has a second, busier trapdoor, called "RefCell." A RefCell keeps an actual sign-in sheet — a running count of how many looks and how many changes are currently checked out — and it checks that sheet every single time you ask to look or ask to change.

rust — a box that keeps its own count of who's inside

use std::cell::RefCell;

let log = RefCell::new(Vec::new());

log.borrow_mut().push("first entry");   // checks out the ONE marker, uses it, checks it back in

{
    let a = log.borrow();               // checks out a look
    let b = log.borrow();               // checks out a second look — fine, looks can stack
    println!("{} entries so far", a.len());
}   // a and b check themselves back in here, automatically

log.borrow_mut().push("second entry");  // fine — the sheet is clear again

borrow() checks out a look, the same look-only kind you've always known. borrow_mut() checks out the one marker, the changing kind. And they let go automatically — the moment the little slip of paper you were handed (Rust calls it a "reference guard") goes out of scope, the sign-in sheet is updated, same as any other borrow ending.

Here's the trade Rust is making, and it's worth saying plainly. With an ordinary look or marker, the strict friend checks the sharing rule once, by reading your code, before anything ever runs — so a mistake never even gets the chance to happen. With RefCell, the checking moves to while the program is running. If you ever ask to change something that's already checked out for looking — or ask to look at something that's already checked out for changing — the box doesn't quietly allow it. It stops your whole program on the spot, with a crash Rust calls a "panic."

Here's the part that is genuinely a trap

It's easy to write code that holds onto a look (borrow()) a little longer than you meant to — across a function call, say — and then, deeper inside that call, ask the very same box to change (borrow_mut()). At compile time this looks completely fine; the strict friend at the door isn't checking RefCell's sign-in sheet, because that sheet doesn't exist until the program is actually running. The crash only shows up the moment that exact path of code actually runs — which can be rare, and can hide for a long time before it happens to you.

A trapdoor built for more than one room

Cell and RefCell's sign-in sheets are only honest inside one room at a time — one part of your program, running by itself, one line after another. The moment two rooms are running at once — what Rust calls "threads" — a sign-in sheet that isn't itself protected from being read and written at the same moment is exactly the same whiteboard-collision problem from the start of this chapter, just one level down. So Rust's strict friend simply refuses, at compile time, to let a Cell or RefCell cross into a second room at all. No runtime crash needed — the door is locked before your program even starts.

For that job, Rust hands you a sturdier trapdoor: "Mutex" (short for "mutual exclusion" — one turn at a time, no exceptions). A Mutex is a box with an actual lock on it. A room asks to .lock() the box, and waits — actually pauses, patiently, doing nothing else — until the lock is free. Once it has the lock, it's the only room allowed anywhere near what's inside, the same "one marker" promise as always, just enforced with a real lock instead of a sign-in sheet, because a sheet alone can't be trusted across rooms.

rust — one lock, shared safely across several rooms at once

use std::sync::{Arc, Mutex};
use std::thread;

let counter = Arc::new(Mutex::new(0));
let mut rooms = Vec::new();

for _ in 0..4 {
    let counter = Arc::clone(&counter);   // a new ticket to the SAME box
    rooms.push(thread::spawn(move || {
        let mut n = counter.lock().unwrap();  // wait for the lock, then hold it
        *n += 1;
    }));  // the lock is handed back the instant this room finishes
}

for room in rooms { room.join().unwrap(); }
println!("{}", *counter.lock().unwrap());  // 4, never fewer

That little Arc wrapped around the Mutex is its own small idea: it's how several rooms get to genuinely share the one box at all, since ordinary ownership only ever allows a single owner. We'll meet Arc properly, on its own, in a later chapter — for now, just notice the pairing: Arc hands out the shared box, Mutex guards the one marker inside it. If you'd rather let many rooms read at once and only lock out others while one room is actually writing, Rust has a close cousin of Mutex for that too, called "RwLock" — same idea as last chapter's "many readers, or one writer," just enforced with a real lock across rooms instead of a compile-time check inside one.

One more careful detail: a lock that remembers a bad ending

One small thing worth knowing, because it's easy to be surprised by it later: if a room crashes — panics — while it's still holding a Mutex's lock, Rust doesn't just quietly hand the lock to the next room as if nothing happened. It marks the lock "poisoned." The idea is simple: whatever that crashed room was in the middle of changing might have been left half-done, and Rust would rather warn the next room than let it walk in on a mess and assume everything's fine. The next .lock() still hands back the data — it just comes wrapped in a note saying "the last room here didn't finish cleanly," so you get to decide whether that matters for your situation.

Mutation
Changing a value after it's made. Off by default in Rust — a value must be marked mut before it can ever change.
Interior mutability
A small trapdoor: a box that keeps its own rulebook and allows changes through a mere look (&T), instead of requiring the one-marker look (&mut T) every other value needs.
Cell
The plainest trapdoor. Never hands out a look inside — only gets a copy or sets a whole new value. No sign-in sheet, because nothing to collide over.
RefCell
The busier trapdoor. Hands out real looks (borrow) and real markers (borrow_mut), and checks the "one marker, or many looks" rule itself, while the program runs — crashing ("panicking") if that rule is ever broken.
Mutex / RwLock
The trapdoor built for more than one room ( "thread") at once. A real lock, not just a sign-in sheet — one room holds it at a time, the rest wait their turn.

Here's why all of this is worth it. Rust could have just let anyone change anything, anywhere, the way most languages do, and left it to you to remember every rule about who's touching what. Instead, it makes the safe path the easy, default path — one marker, checked before your program even runs — and only asks you to reach for a trapdoor, and accept its small extra cost, on the handful of times you actually need more than that. The rest of the time, an entire category of "who changed this, and when" bugs simply can't exist in your program at all.

Because Rust makes changing something a marked, watched action — whether that watching happens once, before your program runs, or quietly, every time, inside a trapdoor you chose on purpose — you get to share data freely without ever wondering, later, who was allowed to change it and when.

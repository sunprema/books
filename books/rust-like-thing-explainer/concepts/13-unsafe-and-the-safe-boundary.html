The Locked Door Marked “You Are on Your Own”

Rust calls this: unsafe, invariants, and FFI — "unsafe Rust"

Every chapter so far has had the same quiet helper standing behind your shoulder: the strict friend, checking each line before anything is even allowed to run. This chapter is about the one door in the whole rulebook where the strict friend stops, holds up a hand, and says: I can't check this one. Past this door, it's on you.

Here's the problem

The strict friend is very good at one particular kind of checking: anything that follows Rust's own rules, written in Rust, the strict friend can trace through completely, every time, before a single line runs. But every now and then you need to do something the strict friend has genuinely never seen before — reach into a box built by a totally different toy company (a library written in another language), or flip a switch on a real physical machine (a piece of hardware), or hand two friends the same shared toy in a way no simple rule can describe.

You might know, for certain, that this one thing is fine. You checked it yourself. But the strict friend's whole job is only approving things it can verify — not things you merely promise are fine. So it says no. Not because you're wrong. Because it has no way to be sure you're right.

Rust doesn't leave you stuck outside a locked room forever with no way in at all. It builds exactly one door, and paints one word on it, so nobody can walk through by accident: "unsafe."

Five things, and only five things

Here's the part that surprises people, because the name sounds so big: walking through the door does not turn off the rulebook. Every rule from every earlier chapter — one owner at a time, the sharing rules, types matching what they're supposed to be — still applies, word for word, even on the other side of that door. The door only unlocks five specific things, and nothing else:

  • looking at a raw address in memory and reading or writing whatever is actually sitting there (a "raw pointer," instead of the normal, checked kind)
  • calling a function that comes with a warning label instead of a rule the strict friend already checked for you
  • touching one particular kind of shared box that lives for the whole life of the program, without asking anyone's permission first
  • promising, yourself, that a value really is safe to hand to another room at the same time — a promise the strict friend has no way to check on its own
  • reaching into a box built to hold more than one kind of thing in the exact same spot

The whole idea

unsafe does not mean "no rules." It means: these five things, and only these five things, are yours to get right by hand — the strict friend steps back just for them, and keeps watching absolutely everything else.

Writing an address down is safe. Walking up to the house is the risky part.

One small piece trips people up right away, so it's worth saying early: just writing down a raw address is not the risky part. You can copy someone's street address onto a piece of paper all day long — that alone can't hurt anyone. The risky part is walking up to that address and opening the door, without knowing for sure there's still a house there, still the same house, still safe to walk into. In Rust, making a raw pointer is completely ordinary, no unsafe needed at all. Reading or writing through it — actually opening that door — is the one part that requires the unsafe keyword.

rust — making the address: safe. opening the door: unsafe.

let mut num = 5;

let address = &raw const num; // writing down an address — safe

unsafe {
    // SAFETY: `num` is still alive right here, so this address is good.
    println!("{}", *address); // opening the door and looking inside — needs unsafe
}

The warning label, and the signature under it

Picture a toolshed with sharp tools inside, and a card on the door: read the safety card before you use anything in here. That card is what an unsafe function is — it's Rust's way of saying "this comes with rules I can't check myself, so I'm writing them on a card instead." Walking into the shed anyway — actually calling that function — means wrapping the call in its own unsafe block, which is your signature saying I read the card, and I'm holding up my end.

rust — a warning-label function, and the signature that lets you call it

unsafe fn dangerous() {
    // something the strict friend can't verify on its own
}

unsafe {
    // SAFETY: I've read dangerous()'s card and I've met every rule on it.
    dangerous();
}

That card has a real name too: Rust calls a promise like this an "invariant" — a plain word for it is just "a rule that has to stay true the whole time, or everything built on top of it stops making sense." A fence with a hole in it isn't much of a fence. An invariant is the fence. Whoever signs the door is promising the fence has no hole, here, right now, even though the strict friend can't walk the fence line itself to check.

Because the strict friend can't check the promise, people who write unsafe Rust have their own habit instead: pin a little note — a // SAFETY: comment — right above every unsafe block, spelling out exactly why it's fine, right here, right now. It's not a rule the compiler enforces. It's the trail of breadcrumbs a person — maybe you, months from now — will need to check the promise again without starting from nothing.

Why almost nobody ever has to open the door

Here's the good news, and it's most of why this door can exist at all without the whole language feeling risky: somebody at the toy factory can walk into the dangerous machine room once, check everything by hand, and then build one simple button on the outside — press here. Everyone else in the factory just presses the button. Nobody else ever has to go near the machine itself, and the button is exactly as safe to press as any other button in the building.

That's the standard shape of real, working unsafe Rust: a small bit of unsafe code, hidden on the inside, wrapped by a plain function whose outside is entirely ordinary and safe to call. This is called a "safe abstraction." Whoever calls the wrapped function never writes unsafe and never even needs to know it's happening underneath — the person who built the wrapper already carried that weight, once, on their behalf. Big, trusted, everyday parts of Rust are built exactly this way: the growable list Vec, the text type String, the locked box from a couple of chapters back, Mutex — unsafe on the inside, perfectly ordinary to use on the outside.

rust — the unsafe part stays inside; the outside is plain and safe to call

// calling this needs no `unsafe` at all — the promise was already kept, once, inside it
fn first_byte(bytes: &[u8]) -> Option<u8> {
    if bytes.is_empty() {
        return None;
    }
    // SAFETY: we just checked bytes isn't empty, so address 0 is inside it.
    Some(unsafe { *bytes.as_ptr() })
}

This is also the door talking-language libraries in other languages use — Rust calls it "FFI," short for "talking to a different toy company's code." A function written in C, say, has no strict friend of its own watching it, so calling it from Rust is one of the five unsafe things: the person calling it is vouching for it, the same way they'd vouch for any other unsafe call.

The one way this can quietly go wrong

It's worth being honest about the real cost here, instead of pretending the locked door is free. If the person who signs the door gets the promise wrong — the fence really did have a hole — the mistake doesn't always show up right there at the door. It can turn up somewhere else entirely, much later, in a part of the program that looks completely innocent, doing nothing unusual at all. That's what makes a broken unsafe promise so much harder to track down than an ordinary bug: the strict friend would have stopped an ordinary mistake on the spot. This kind slips past, quietly, until it doesn't.

A real trap

A program can compile with no complaints, and every test can pass, and an unsafe promise inside it can still be broken. Compiling clean and passing tests were never proof the promise was kept — they only prove nobody happened to trip over the hole yet. That's exactly why a separate helper called "Miri" exists: it actually runs your program, step by step, watching closely for a broken promise the whole time — the kind of checking the strict friend can't do just by reading the code before it runs.

None of this is a reason to be afraid of the word unsafe when you see it. It's a reason to notice it. Seeing unsafe in real code should feel like seeing a locked door with a sign on it: not "something is wrong here," but "someone made a promise here — it might be worth reading the note pinned next to it."

Why this is worth it

A rulebook with absolutely no exceptions would either have to reject real, useful work the strict friend simply can't see far enough to approve — or quietly stop checking anything at all, for everyone, all the time. Neither one is actually better. Rust's answer is narrower and, once you see it, obviously the right shape: one door, clearly marked, unlocking exactly five things and nothing more, so the rest of the rulebook never has to bend.

And because that door is small and marked and rare, almost every program you'll ever write can lean on someone else having already opened it, carefully, once — a button built by someone who checked the machine — while everything else you write keeps every promise the strict friend has been keeping since chapter one.

unsafe
The one marked door in the rulebook. It unlocks exactly five things — nothing about ownership, borrowing, or types turns off.
Raw pointer
A plain address, safe to write down and hold. Only reading or writing through it — dereferencing it — needs unsafe.
Invariant
A rule that has to stay true the whole time, like a fence with no hole in it, that the strict friend can't check on its own.
Safe abstraction
Unsafe code hidden on the inside of a function, wrapped by a plain, ordinary outside. Callers never need unsafe themselves.
FFI
Talking to code from a different language. Calling it is unsafe because it has no strict friend of its own watching it.
Miri
A helper that runs your program step by step, watching for a broken unsafe promise even when it compiled clean and every test passed.

Because that door is small, marked, and rare, everything outside it still gets to keep every single promise the strict friend has made since the first chapter — and the handful of times someone really did have to step through, they left a note behind explaining why, so the next person wouldn't have to wonder.

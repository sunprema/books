Part I · The Problem Iceberg Solves

From Locked Rooms to Open Fields

The data world calls this: the evolution from warehouses to lakes to lakehouses

Here’s the problem

Every night, new data lands in a big cheap storage place. Then a job picks up a part of it, loads it onto a truck, and drives it to a second, tidier place where the reports live.

One Friday night the truck breaks down. On Monday morning the revenue dashboard looks fine. It is not fine. It quietly shows Friday’s numbers, and nobody is told.

Why does the data need a truck at all? To answer that, we have to walk through how companies got here.

The idea: three kinds of places for data

First came the data warehouse (a tidy, guarded building where company data is kept for reports). It checks every row on the way in, keeps changes all-or-nothing, and answers report questions fast. The price is high. It keeps data in its own private format, storage and computing are tied together, and you are locked in. It also copes badly with machine learning and with messy data like text and images.

Then came the data lake (a huge cheap storage space full of plain files). First this meant Hadoop file systems, later the cloud’s storage services. Files are in open formats such as Parquet, so any tool can read them. That is the good part. The bad part: no all-or-nothing changes, weak consistency, and a long list of quality troubles.

Most companies could not pick one. They used both. Everything landed in the lake, and curated pieces were copied into a warehouse. That is the truck from our scene. It means two copies of the data, more jobs that can break, reports that run behind the lake, and two sets of security rules to keep in step.

How it works

In 2021 a group of researchers (Armbrust, Ghodsi, Xin and Zaharia) named a third way: the lakehouse (a lake that behaves like a warehouse). Keep the cheap, open files. Add a layer of records on top that makes the files act like trusted tables, with all-or-nothing changes, access rules, and good speed. They reported that lakehouses built on Parquet could keep up with cloud warehouses on a standard speed test.

That layer of records has a name you will hear a lot: an open table format (a published, vendor-neutral rulebook for how data files plus records make one table). Three of them appeared around 2017–2018: Iceberg, Delta Lake and Hudi. Because the rulebook is public, any engine that follows it can read and write the same table. No single company owns the door.

Where does Iceberg sit? It is that table layer, one of the open table formats. Warehouse engines and lake engines can both read the same Iceberg table, so the truck can be parked.

A short history

Iceberg began at Netflix. Ryan Blue and Dan Weeks built it to replace Hive tables on S3, whose split records could not promise correct, all-or-nothing results at Netflix’s size. It was shown at the Strata conference in 2018, given to the Apache Software Foundation in November 2018, and became a top-level Apache project in May 2020.

Then 2024 became the year the industry leaned in. In June, Databricks, maker of the rival Delta Lake, bought Tabular, the company started by Iceberg’s creators (reported near one billion dollars). Snowflake announced its open-source Polaris catalog for Iceberg the same week. In December, AWS launched S3 Tables with managed Iceberg built in. In 2025 the community approved Iceberg format version 3, with new ways to track deletes, row history and flexible data types, and Databricks described it as moving Delta and Iceberg toward one shape.

Design principle

Separate the data from the engine. Keep the files and the table rules open and public, and let any engine come and go. Then storage stops being a lock, and choosing a tool becomes a choice you can change.

Use case: retiring the nightly truck

A company lands sales events in cloud storage and copies them into a warehouse every night. Reports are a day behind, the copy job fails twice a quarter, and the data team keeps two sets of access rules. They move the sales data into Iceberg tables. Now the lake is the single home. A warehouse-style engine runs the reports on the same files that the data science team reads. The copy job, and its Friday-night failure, goes away.

Deciding: which place fits?

Watch out for

  • “Lakehouse” names a pattern, not a product. Vendors stick the word on very different systems, so check the properties: open format, all-or-nothing changes, many engines.
  • An open table format does not make the whole platform open. If the catalog, rules or upkeep tools are private, the lock-in just moves up a layer.
  • The warehouse did not die. Many lakehouse query engines are warehouse engines that learned to read open tables.
  • Lakes are not automatically cheaper. Storage request charges, growing records, upkeep computing and duplicate copies during a move all cost real money.

When the lake itself can be trusted, you can stop driving the truck, and Monday’s dashboard finally shows Monday’s numbers.

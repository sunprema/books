Part III · Architecture and Design Principles

One Key for One Room

The data world calls this: governance, security and trust

Here’s the problem

Think of a hotel. When you check in, the front desk does not hand you the master key. It cuts you a card that opens one room, for one night. Tomorrow the card is dead, and nobody had to chase you to get it back.

Now picture a hotel that gave every guest the master key and trusted them to behave. That is a lot of data lakes. Everyone holds the keys to the whole storage bucket, and the only rule is good manners. This chapter is about running your tables like the good hotel.

The idea: put the front desk at the catalog

In an open lakehouse, every program that wants a table must first ask the catalog for it, and must commit its changes through the catalog too. That makes the catalog the natural front desk. Checking who you are, deciding what you may do, and writing down what you did can all happen in one place, instead of being rebuilt separately inside every tool.

The trick that makes this real is credential vending (the catalog hands out a short-lived key made for just this request). The key opens only the files of one table, and only for the actions you were granted. If you may read, the key can fetch files but cannot add or delete them. Nobody needs a shared bucket password any more.

Who gets which key? Apache Polaris, an open catalog, uses role-based access control (people get roles, and roles carry the permissions). Programs and services hold one kind of role. Another kind of role holds the actual privileges, such as reading or writing a table, on a whole catalog, a folder of tables, or a single table. The same rules then apply to every engine that connects.

How it works: the key is cut at the desk

Be honest about the limits, though. As of 2026, open catalogs such as Polaris (through version 1.7) guard whole tables. They cannot hide single rows or blank out single columns. And once an engine holds a key to a table’s files, it can physically read every column in them. So finer rules live in the engines or in a policy layer on top.

Managed catalogs close that gap by keeping everything inside one vendor’s walls. Unity Catalog adds row filters, column masks, lineage and audit for access through Databricks. On AWS, Glue and S3 Tables pair with Lake Formation permissions. Today the strongest row and column control comes from staying inside one vendor’s path.

Audit and erasure pull in opposite directions

Iceberg tables are auditable by construction. Every change is a recorded snapshot (the table as it stood at one moment), with what was done, when, and the commit notes the engine supplied. An auditor can ask for the table as it was on a past date, with nothing to restore.

Privacy law asks for the reverse: forget this person. A plain DELETE only hides rows in new snapshots. The data stays in older snapshots and files until you expire those snapshots and clean up orphaned files. Real erasure takes three steps: delete, expire, purge.

That means a long time-travel window full of personal data cannot be kept whole once the records must be destroyed. Teams pick from three habits: keep short retention on tables with personal data, move personal columns into their own tables, or use crypto-shredding (lock each person’s data with its own key, then destroy the key to forget them).

Design principle

Build governance into the platform on day one, not on top of it after everyone has moved in. Once teams hold bucket paths and private keys, taking them back is hard in both the technical and the human sense.

Use case: a data product with an owner

Ownership fits the catalog’s shape neatly. A folder (namespace) groups one team’s tables. Grants say who may consume the product. The versioned schema is its public face, and snapshot history is its audit trail. The catalog becomes the one registry where a data product’s contract, rules and past all sit together, so a new reader can ask “who owns this, and who may use it?” and get one answer.

Deciding: where should the rules live?

SituationApproachTrade-off
Many engines, table-level rules are enoughOpen REST catalog with roles and vended keysRules are shared, but no row or column hiding
You must hide rows or mask columnsA managed catalog’s own enforcement, or engine and policy-layer rulesStrong control, but tied to one vendor’s path
Personal data under erasure rulesShort retention, separate tables, or crypto-shreddingLess history to audit against
Small, trusted team, low stakesSimple shared access may doCheap now, painful to tighten later

Watch out for

  • Anyone with direct bucket keys walks past every catalog rule. Lock down storage so vended keys are the only way in.
  • Believing DELETE answers an erasure request. Deleted rows stay readable by time travel until snapshots expire and files are purged.
  • Row and column rules set in one engine do not travel with the table. An engine added later may ignore them, so list every way in before you grant it.
  • Governance added late rarely sticks. Build the catalog-grant path in from the start.

Sources: Apache Polaris documentation; “A Brief Guide to the Governance of Apache Iceberg Tables”; Databricks Unity Catalog managed-tables documentation; Apache Iceberg Table Spec; Iceberg snapshot-expiration guides.

Because every reader gets a small key cut at one front desk, you can say exactly who saw what, and take that access away without hunting through a hundred copies of a password.

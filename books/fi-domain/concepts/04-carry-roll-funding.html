Chapter four

What Holding Something Pays You, and What Waiting Costs

Real name: “Carry, roll and funding”

Picture a farmer who buys a cow with borrowed money. Every day the cow gives milk, and every day the farmer pays a little for the loan. Even if the price of cows never moves, the farmer ends the month with more money or less. A bond is the cow. This chapter is about that milk and that loan.

Where it goes wrong

You ask the machine, “What happens to my book if nothing in the market moves?” It says, “Nothing.” But a lot happens. The pay on each paper keeps piling up. The loan you used to buy it keeps costing you. And each paper gets one day shorter, which changes its price even when the board of rates stays still. If your machine cannot see these, it will tell a trader that a quiet day lost nothing when it really lost money, or the other way round.

The milk and the loan

The pay you collect for holding a paper, minus what it costs to borrow the money for it, is called “carry”. Over a set stretch of time, such as one month:

Carry = pay that piled up on the paper − cost of the loan, over the stretch you pick.

If the paper pays more than the loan costs, carry is good: you are paid to wait. If the loan costs more, carry is bad: waiting costs you. Short papers on a flat board often have little of it. Lots of people trip on one thing here: the pay piling up is not a gain from the price moving. It is a gain from time passing. Keep those two apart.

Sliding down the slope

Now the second thing. Picture the board of rates as a hill, with short waits on the left and long waits on the right. When the hill slopes up, a longer wait pays a higher yield. You buy a five-year paper. A month later it is a four-year-and-eleven-months paper. It has slid a little to the left on the hill, where the yield is lower. A lower yield means a higher price. That gain, with the board unchanged, is “roll-down”.

Roll-down is biggest where the hill is steepest, and zero on a flat board. If the board slopes down, roll-down is a loss.

One month, with numbers

These numbers are made up. A five-year paper pays 4% and costs 100, and its yield is 4.00%. Say the board slopes up 0.20 percentage points for each year near that point. You borrow the 100 at 3.60% for a month.

Now ask the useful question: how far can the board go against you before this small gain is gone? One basis point of yield is worth about 0.0443 on this paper. So 0.1044 divided by 0.0443 is about 2.4 basis points. That is the “breakeven”. It is tiny, and that is the point: carry and roll-down are a small cushion, not a forecast. Yields in the Treasury market are jumpy, so the “board stays put” story is rarely true. It is a fair baseline to measure against, and nothing more.

Keep the pieces apart. If you only look at how the full price changed over the month, the pay piling up hides inside it. Take out the pay first; what is left from the unchanged board is roll-down. Mix them and your story of “why we made money” is wrong.

Where the loan comes from

Desks borrow cash by handing over the paper itself as a pledge for a short time and buying it back later at a slightly higher price. The extra is the interest. This is a “repo” loan. Because the lender holds the paper, the loan is cheap.

There are two kinds of price for this loan. If the lender just wants safe paper of any kind, the rate is the plain one, called “general collateral”, and it follows the cost of money. But if lots of people have sold a particular paper that they do not own and must find it, the owner of that paper can lend it out and ask for a lower rate on the cash. That paper is said to be “special”. The gap below the plain rate is its specialness.

For your carry, this matters in one direction: if you own the special paper, your loan is cheaper, so your carry is better. If your machine uses the plain rate for everything, it will understate the carry of the special paper and overstate the cost of shorting it. A short seller who cannot find the paper at all fails to deliver, and the market has a penalty for that: a charge of 3% minus a reference rate, never below zero. Papers have at times traded through that penalty, so treat it as a rough guide, and check the current rule before relying on it.

Loans come in lengths too. An overnight loan has to be renewed every day, so its cost moves. A “term repo” fixes the rate for weeks or months. When you carry a position over a long stretch, the machine should say which one it assumed, because a position funded overnight carries a risk that the loan gets dearer.

The same idea for the other two stalls

The handshake on a far-off day

A futures price already has the loan and the pay folded in. Chapter three showed that the seller can choose which paper to hand over. Ask: if I buy the paper now with borrowed money, sell the handshake, and hand the paper over on the last day, what loan rate would make me break even? That is the “implied repo rate”. Nobody quotes it; it hides in the prices. The paper with the highest one is the cheapest to hand over.

The same story in another form: the gap between the cash price and the handshake price (scaled by the paper’s conversion number) is the gross basis. Take away the carry and what is left is the net basis. The paper with the lowest net basis is usually the same one that has the highest implied repo. If the implied repo is above what you really pay to borrow, buying the paper and selling the handshake looks good, before costs and before the seller’s choices are counted.

Swapped payments

A swap has no cash to borrow, but it still has carry. If you receive the fixed rate and pay the floating one, then at an unchanged board you get the fixed rate and expect to pay what the board says the floating rate will be. The carry for a period is fixed minus that expected floating rate. If the board slopes up, expected floating rates are higher than today’s, so carry on a receive-fixed swap is smaller than a glance at the overnight rate suggests. A swap rolls down the hill too, as its remaining life shortens.

The cost of a safety belt

A trader who owns a long paper and fears rates will rise may sell a hedge: a short in a similar paper or a handshake. The hedge protects against a move, but it pays milk the wrong way. Short a paper and you owe its pay, while the cash from selling it earns only the loan rate. That slow bleed is the “carry drag” of the hedge.

Traders like to see it as money per month. Made-up example: you short 50 million of a paper that pays 4.2% and you earn 3.6% on the cash. The gap is 0.6% a year, which is 50,000,000 × 0.006 ÷ 12 = 25,000 a month. A trader can weigh that against what the hedge saves them on a bad day. Show the drag, and show that it can flip sign if the loan rate moves.

Rolling the fork forward in time

The engine works by making a copy of today’s book, a fork, and changing something in the copy. Moving the clock is one such change, a “time-roll” shock. Move the date ahead by a month, keep the board fixed, price again, and subtract. What is left after taking out the pay piling up is roll-down; the pay minus the loan is carry. This is the same split as the table above, done for every position at once.

  • Say which board you hold fixed. “Same yield at each wait” and “the future the board already implies” give different answers, and neither is a forecast.
  • Say which loan rate you used, plain or special, overnight or fixed.
  • Move the dates, not just the price. Pay dates, rolls of futures at quarter-end and fixing dates all pass as the clock moves.
  • Keep the leftover small. If the parts do not add up to the whole, that is a bug to chase, not noise to hide.

Once the machine counts the milk, the loan and the slide, a quiet day stops lying to you.

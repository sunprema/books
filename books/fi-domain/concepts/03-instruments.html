Chapter three

The Three Things You Can Hold: Loans to the Government, Promises on a Date, and Swapped Payments

Real name: “Treasuries, futures, SOFR swaps”

Think of a market with three kinds of stall. At the first, you lend money to the government and get a paper back. At the second, you shake hands today on a trade that happens on a day far off. At the third, you and a friend agree to swap one kind of payment for another. Almost everything a rates desk holds is one of these three.

Where it goes wrong

You build a machine that says, “These three stalls all sit on one board of rates, so they all move together.” Then one day the new paper from the government is cheaper to hold than the old one next to it, the handshake paper acts as if it were a different paper than the one you thought, and the swap and the government paper drift apart. Each stall has its own small rules, and the rules are where the surprises live.

Lots of people find the handshake stall the most maddening, and that is fair. It has a strange rule that lets the seller choose what to hand over. We will go slowly there.

Stall one: loans to the government

The government borrows by selling papers. The market calls them “Treasuries”. They come in sizes by how long you wait to be paid back:

KindHow longHow it pays
“Bills”A year or lessSold for less than face; you get face back, and the gap is your pay
“Notes”2 to 10 yearsSmall payments twice a year, then face at the end
“Bonds”20 and 30 yearsSame shape as notes, just a longer wait
“STRIPS”Any wait a note or bond hasA note or bond cut into single pieces, one per payment; each piece pays once

A ten-year note with twenty small payments and one big one can be cut into twenty-one pieces. Each piece is its own paper, and each is still a promise by the government. Cutting happens through a dealer, not through the government’s own web page. Groups that know the day they must pay someone, such as pensions, like these pieces because one piece lands on one day.

How a new paper is born

The government sells new papers at an “auction” on days it announces ahead of time. Bills go on a steady weekly beat. Buyers say how much they want and at what yield. Small buyers who just say “give me whatever price comes out” are filled first. Then the rest are filled starting from the lowest yield, which is the highest price, until the sale is full. Everyone who wins pays the same yield: the highest one that was accepted.

Between the day the sale is announced and the day the paper really exists, dealers already trade it. This is called “when-issued” trading. It is a way for bidders to see a price before they bid.

The newest paper and its older twin

The most recently sold paper of each length is called the “on-the-run”. When a newer one is sold, the old one becomes “off-the-run”. The newest paper is where most of the trading goes, so it is easiest to buy and sell and usually costs a little more (it has a slightly lower yield). The extra price you pay for ease is the “liquidity premium”. It is small on calm days and can grow when people are scared and everyone wants the easy paper. This matters to a machine: if you build your board only from the newest papers, an older paper you hold will not sit exactly on it.

Stall two: the handshake on a far-off day

A “futures contract” is a deal made today to trade a government paper on a set day later. You do not hand over the full price now. You put up a small deposit and the money differences are settled each day. Most people close the deal before the day comes, but a few really do hand over papers, and the rules for that day shape the price all the way along.

The exchange sells six kinds, each aimed at a different wait. The brief names the codes TU, FV, TY and US, then two “ultra” ones. As the exchange lists them:

ContractPapers that may be handed overOne contract is

They run on a March, June, September and December beat. A price tick is a small fraction of a point: a thirty-second for the bond contracts, half of that for the ten-years, and smaller still for the five-year. These figures come from the exchange’s own pages and have changed before, so a machine should read them from a source it can update, not from a file written once.

Many papers, one contract

Here is the strange part. A ten-year contract does not name one paper. Many papers fit. The seller, called the “short”, picks which one to hand over. This group of papers is the “deliverable basket”.

To make the papers fair to swap, the exchange gives each one a “conversion factor”. Picture it as a fairness weight. It is what the paper would be worth per 100 if it paid a 6 percent yield. The buyer pays the contract price times that weight, plus the interest the paper has built up. A paper with small payments gets a weight under one; one with big payments, over one.

The weights are never quite fair. The 6 percent rule leans toward some papers. So one paper in the basket is the cheapest for the seller to hand over, the “cheapest-to-deliver”, or CTD. That one paper mostly decides how the contract behaves.

Measuring the cheapness

  • Gross basis (the “basis”): the paper’s price, minus the contract price times its weight. It is how much more you pay to hold the paper than to be promised it.
  • Net basis: gross basis minus the carry of holding the paper until the day (interest it pays, less the cost of borrowing to buy it). What is left is the price of the seller’s choices. The paper with the smallest net basis is the CTD.
  • Implied repo: pretend you buy the paper, sell the contract and hand it over. What borrowing rate would make that trade break even? That is the “implied repo rate”. The paper with the highest one is the CTD. A high number means the paper is cheap to hand over. Sources word the highest-lowest part differently, so check the sign in whatever tool you read from.

What the contract is worth when rates move

Because the contract follows the CTD, its one-nudge number (from the last chapter) comes from the CTD:

Key idea

Futures DV01 ≈ CTD DV01 ÷ conversion factor.

Example, with made-up numbers: a CTD loses 0.0700 per 100 for one step in rates, and its weight is 0.8000. The contract loses about 0.0700 / 0.8000 = 0.0875 per 100, so one contract of 100,000 face loses about 87.50 dollars per step. The weight makes sense: a lighter-weight paper counts for less of the contract, so the contract moves more than the paper does.

That number holds only while that paper stays the CTD. When rates move a lot, a different paper can become cheapest. Often the new one has a different wait and so a different one-nudge number, and the contract suddenly acts like a longer or shorter paper. This is “CTD switch risk”. A machine that finds the CTD once in the morning and never again will mis-hedge on a day with a big move. Test it with a shove up and a shove down and see whether the CTD changes.

The seller’s free choices

The short’s freedom is worth something, and the buyer pays for it through the price. Three choices stand out:

  • Quality: pick which paper in the basket.
  • Timing: hand over on any day of the month it is allowed, and use the last days after trading stops.
  • Wild card: the contract price is fixed at the close of trading in the afternoon, but the short can still say “I will deliver today” for several more hours. If the paper falls in price after the close, the short can lock in the old price. Sources disagree on the exact hour (7 or 8 in the evening, Chicago time), so read the exchange’s rule for the year you care about.

The simple rule: model the contract as following the CTD, and treat these choices as a small extra cost to the buyer that you do not have to price to get a good hedge.

The roll

Contracts end, so a trader who wants to keep an exposure must move from the near one to the next, a step called the “roll”. Most of the trading moves a bit before the first day deliveries can start, and the old contract becomes thin. A machine should make a rule for the roll (which day the book switches) and for what happens to risk numbers on that day, because the CTD of the new contract is often a different paper.

Stall three: swapped payments

In an “interest rate swap” two sides agree on a size and a length. One side pays a fixed rate on that size. The other side pays a rate that changes. No one hands over the size; only the small payments go back and forth, and usually only the difference.

Today the changing rate is “SOFR”, a rate set by what banks really pay to borrow overnight against government papers. It is a one-day rate, so the changing side pays the result of compounding every day’s rate over the period, like interest on interest.

The usual terms for a standard dollar SOFR swap:

  • Both sides pay once a year, counting days on the actual days divided by 360. (The chapter on curves told you why the day count matters.) The older dollar swap often paid fixed twice a year on a 30-day month; do not mix the two.
  • The New York calendar is used for business days.
  • The payment comes two business days after the period ends. This “payment lag” exists because the last day’s SOFR is only published the next morning, and time is needed to add it all up.

Where a swap lives matters. A “cleared” swap goes through a central house (the big ones are CME and LCH) that stands in the middle of both sides. A “bilateral” swap stays between the two sides. The central house asks each side for a deposit, the “initial margin”, sized to cover losses if one side fails and its position has to be closed. The house also swaps small cash amounts every day as the swap’s value moves. The time the house assumes it needs to close a swap is longer than for a future, which is one reason deposits on swaps tend to be higher than on matching futures. The houses moved their daily cash and discounting to SOFR in 2018 (CME) and 2020 (LCH), which is why the board you discount on, from chapter one, is a SOFR board.

The gap between a swap and a government paper

Take a ten-year swap’s fixed rate and subtract the ten-year Treasury yield. The result is the “swap spread”. For many years, at the long end, it has been below zero: the fixed rate on the swap is lower than the yield on the government paper. That looks odd, since the government is the safest borrower. But the credit risk of the two is about the same now, and the gap mostly comes from other things:

  • Room on the banks’ books. Dealers who hold a lot of Treasuries have to hold extra safety money against them, whatever the paper’s risk. The rule was eased in late 2025, which sources say may help pull spreads up, though some think not by a lot.
  • Who wants what. Pensions and insurers who owe money far ahead often prefer to receive fixed in a swap rather than buy a bond.
  • How much the government is selling. More new papers push their yields up, which pushes the spread down.
  • The switch from the old rate to SOFR, which took away an extra credit part the old swaps carried.

Why does an engine care? A hedge made with swaps against a Treasury position is not a perfect hedge. If the spread moves, you lose or gain even if the whole board stays still. The next chapters return to this when they ask what to hedge with.

Know the small rules of each stall, and you will not be surprised when three papers on one board stop moving as one.

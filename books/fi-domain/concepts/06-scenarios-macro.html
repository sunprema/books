Chapter six

What If the Big Meeting Surprises Everyone?

Real name: “Scenarios and the macro context”

Picture a town where everyone waits for the mayor to speak. Before the talk, people guess. After the talk, prices on every stall in town move, but not all by the same amount. Your machine cannot know what the mayor will say. It can ask a better question: “If the talk goes like this, what happens to my papers?”

Where it goes wrong

A trader asks, “What if they surprise us on Wednesday?” If your machine can only shift every price up by the same small step, it has not answered. Real days do not move like that. Short papers jump, far-off papers barely stir, or the other way round. A test that moves everything evenly misses the way people actually lose money. Lots of people find this part hard, and that is fair: you must know a little about the people behind the prices.

The big meeting

A group of the country’s money leaders meets eight times a year. The real name is the “FOMC”. They set the price of borrowing for one night. In 2026 the dates are late January, mid March, late April, mid June, late July, mid September, late October and early December. Each meeting runs two days.

On the second day at 2:00 in the afternoon (New York time) they post a short note with their choice. Half an hour later the leader of the group answers questions from the press. Four of the meetings (March, June, September, December) also come with a page of guesses. Each member marks a dot for where they think the one-night price will be at the end of this year, next year, and far ahead. That page is called the “dot plot”. Careful: the dots are single people’s guesses, not a promise from the group.

Now the part your machine needs. The one-night price pins down the short papers hard, because they are only a few months long. A long paper cares about the whole road ahead, plus what people ask to be paid for the worry of waiting. So a surprise about the road moves short papers the most and long papers less.

Hawk, dove and the shape of the line

Traders call a surprise toward higher prices for borrowing “hawkish”, and a surprise toward lower prices “dovish”. Your machine needs those words as pictures of the line that shows yield against length. Two small words say two things: bull means paper prices go up (yields fall); bear means paper prices go down (yields rise). Flatter means the gap between long and short yields shrinks; steeper means it grows.

Do not use one word for every rise. If long yields rise because the Treasury plans to sell more paper, that is not a hawkish flatten. It can steepen the line. Say what you think caused it.

Other days that move the town

  • Prices in the shops (“CPI”): comes out once a month at 8:30 in the morning. A hotter number makes people expect higher borrowing prices, so short yields jump.
  • Jobs report (“payrolls”): usually the first Friday of the month, same hour. More jobs than guessed pushes yields up; fewer pulls them down.
  • Government plan to sell paper (“refunding”): in early February, May, August and November the Treasury says how much it will sell in the coming months. More long paper than people hoped can push long yields up even when the one-night price does not move.
  • Sales days (“auctions”): the Treasury sells new three-, ten- and thirty-year papers in a sale. If buyers pay less than the market expected, the sale is said to “tail”, and the whole stretch of the line can drift up.

These are on the calendar, so a desk can set a test for each one. A good machine keeps the dates in one list and lets the trader pick “the day after the next jobs report.”

Replaying days that really happened

The safest test is to take a real stretch of time, write down how each length moved, and apply those same moves to today’s papers. Nobody can say it was silly: it happened. Two to know:

Replays have a hole: they only contain shapes that have already happened. And you must say which dates you used, because a taper tantrum starting on May 2 looks different from one starting on May 22.

Made-up days that still make sense

To ask about something that has not happened, you build a shape. The hard way is to pick a move for each length by hand: two-year up 40, five-year up 10, ten-year down 30. That makes jagged lines that the real market would never draw, and your repricing may make odd things like negative forward rates.

A calmer way: look at years of daily moves and ask, “Which few patterns explain most of the movement?” The method is called “principal component analysis”. For Treasuries it keeps finding the same three patterns, first shown by Litterman and Scheinkman in 1991:

  • Level: every length moves the same way. By far the biggest share, roughly 85 to 95 parts in 100 depending on the years and lengths you pick.
  • Slope: short and long move opposite ways, so the line tilts.
  • Curvature: the middle moves against both ends, so the line bends.

Together they often cover over 99 in 100 of daily movement. Now a scenario is three dials. “Level up 50, slope flatter by 20, no bend” becomes a smooth move at every length, found by multiplying each dial by that length’s weight. Size the dials in how many typical moves they are over your time window, so you can say a test is a “two-sigma slope move over a month” and be understood. The three dials also tell you what a test is not checking: the leftover pieces are small but not zero.

Three checks before you call a made-up test plausible. Does the line stay smooth? Do the forward rates stay sensible? Can you tell a story, in one sentence, of what would cause it?

The word list your bank buyers use

Big banks must run yearly tests that the Fed sets. The names are “CCAR” and “DFAST”. The Fed posts a calm path (baseline) and a hard one (“severely adverse”) for rates, jobs, house prices and more. The paths are new each year and are made to be hard to survive, not to be a best guess.

For 2026, the severely adverse path has the three-month yield falling from 3.7% at the end of 2025 to 0.1% within two quarters, joblessness rising by 5.5 points to 10%, and stock prices dropping 58%. The 2026 baseline keeps the ten-year near 4.1%. I did not find the ten-year number for the hard path in the sources I checked; read it from the Fed’s scenario file before you use it.

You do not need to run their tests. You need to speak the language: a buyer will say “Can you run our severely adverse rate path?” and mean “Let me type in the Fed’s yearly list and see what your tool does.” Keep a place in the machine to load a path of rates by quarter and reprice at each step.

When the machine can say what a surprise would do to each stall in town, a trader can prepare before the talk instead of after.

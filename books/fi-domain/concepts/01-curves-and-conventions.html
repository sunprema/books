Chapter one

The Lines That Tell You What Money Is Worth Later

Real name: “Curves and conventions”

Picture a big board in a market hall. For every wait, from one night to thirty years, it shows what a dollar you will get later is worth today.

Where it goes wrong

Two friends, Ana and Ben, each look at the same loan paper. Ana says it is worth 101.20 today. Ben says 101.31. Nobody is lying. Ana counted the days in the year one way, and Ben counted them another way. Over one small loan it is a small gap. Over a book of ten thousand papers it is a lot of money that is not there.

Lots of people find this part of money maddening, and that is fair. The rules for counting days feel like arguing about how long a ruler is. But a machine that tries out many possible futures needs every paper counted the same way as the market counts it, or every answer it gives is a little bit off.

Rule one: count the days the way the market does

Each kind of paper has its own way to count how much of the year has gone by. The market calls this the “day count”: the rule for turning real days on a calendar into a share of a year.

PaperDay countIn plain words
Treasury notes and bondsACT/ACT (ICMA)Real days passed, over the real days in this pay period, times two pay periods a year
The floating side of a SOFR swapACT/360Real days passed, over 360
The fixed side of a SOFR swapACT/360, paid once a yearSame as above. Older swaps used 30/360, where every month counts as 30 days
Careful

Lists of conventions often say the fixed side of a swap is 30/360. That was true for the older style of swap, paid twice a year. For today’s SOFR swaps, the common market way is ACT/360 paid once a year. Cleared trades allow other choices too, so read the paper for each trade and do not guess.

A small example for the first row. A bond pays every half year. The current half year has 182 real days, and 61 days have passed. The share of the year that has passed is 61 divided by (182 × 2), which is 61 over 364. Notice the bottom is 364, not 365. A half year that has 181 days gives a different bottom. That is the whole trick.

Rule two: only count days when the market is open

Payments cannot land on a day the doors are shut. So every machine needs a “calendar”: a list of days the market is open. For Treasuries this is the US government securities calendar. On some days before a holiday, the trade body called SIFMA says to close early, which matters when you take a picture of prices at the end of the day.

If a payment day lands on a shut day, a “roll rule” says where to move it. The common one is “modified following”: move to the next open day, unless that jumps into the next month, and then move back to the day before instead.

Also, a trade does not finish the moment you shake hands. Treasuries normally finish on the next open day. The market calls that “T+1” (trade day plus one). The money and the paper change hands then, so that is the day your machine should price from.

Rule three: the price on the screen is not the price you pay

Say a bond paid its last coupon 61 days ago. The seller has held it since then and earned part of the next coupon. So the buyer pays for that part too. The price on the screen leaves it out; the market calls that the “clean price”. The part earned so far is the “accrued interest”. Together, they make the “dirty price”, which is what really moves between the two friends.

Dirty price = clean price + accrued interest. Accrued uses the settle day, not the trade day.

If you forget this, the price of a bond seems to jump down on the day a coupon is paid. Nothing was lost. The part that was earned was paid out.

Three ways to draw the same line

Back to the board in the market hall. The same facts can be drawn in three ways, and you can turn one into another:

  • The “par curve”: for each wait, the coupon at which a new paper would sell at exactly its face value. This is what the Treasury posts every day.
  • The “zero curve”: for each wait, the yearly rate on a paper that pays one lump at the end and nothing before. The cleanest picture of what a wait costs.
  • The “forward curve”: for each future moment, the rate for lending for just a short time starting then. It is the price of waiting, seen from today.

The link is simple. A zero rate is a kind of average of the forwards before it. When the zero line goes up, the forwards must go up faster. That is why small wobbles in a zero curve become big wobbles in forwards.

Building the board from the few papers you can see

The market only gives you some points: a few bills, a swap at two years, one at five, and so on. To fill the board, the machine goes from short to long. Each new paper tells it one more number, so that the paper prices back to exactly what the market says. The market calls this “bootstrapping”.

Between the points you must pick a way to join the dots, called “interpolation”. Your choice changes the forwards, even if it barely changes the prices of papers you used to build the curve:

Way to join dotsWhat forwards look likeGood / bad
Log-linear on discount factorsFlat steps that jump at each pointSimple, keeps forwards positive, but jagged; a bump at one point moves forwards unevenly
Monotone convex (Hagan & West)Smooth, positive if the inputs areKeeps forwards sensible and local, but harder to build and explain
Why a risk engine cares

When you nudge one point on the board to see what a book loses, the joining rule decides how far the nudge spreads. Different joining rules give different answers for the same nudge. Pick one, write it down, keep it fixed.

Two boards you actually need

For swaps, the board for “what is a later dollar worth today” comes from “SOFR OIS” swaps. SOFR is an overnight rate that the New York Fed posts each day. An OIS swap trades a fixed rate against that rate added up day by day. Cleared swaps are discounted on this board.

For Treasuries there are two ways. The quick way uses only the newest paper at each wait (the “on-the-runs”) and joins them. The careful way fits one smooth line through every Treasury, so an old, thinly-traded paper does not pull the line off. The quick way is easy to explain; the careful way is steadier. Some papers cost a little more than their neighbors only because they are easier to trade, and a good fit has to decide whether that matters.

In the machine: QuantLib’s parts

QuantLib, a free toolkit for this work, has a few parts you will meet again and again. A “YieldTermStructure” is the board itself. A “SimpleQuote” is one number from the market that you can change. A “RelinkableHandle” is a plug that points at a board; swap the board and everything plugged in sees the new one.

q = ql.SimpleQuote(0.0425)            # one market number
h = ql.QuoteHandle(q)
helper = ql.OISRateHelper(2, ql.Period('5Y'), h, sofr_index)
curve = ql.PiecewiseLogLinearDiscount(today, [helper], ql.Actual365Fixed())
plug = ql.RelinkableYieldTermStructureHandle(curve)
q.setValue(0.0430)                    # the board redraws itself

Walk through it. Line one makes a number we can change. Line two wraps it so others can watch it. Line three says “a 5-year swap has this rate.” Line four builds the board from it, joining dots with the log-linear rule. Line five makes the plug. The last line changes the number; because the pieces “observe” each other, the board knows it is out of date, and builds itself again only when someone asks it a question. That is called being “lazy”, and it saves a lot of work when you try thousands of nudged boards.

Careful

Class names and details change between QuantLib versions, so check them against the one you install. Also, a plug is shared by everything holding it. If two scenarios share one plug, one can change the other’s answers.

When every paper is counted, dated and priced the way the market does it, your machine and the trader finally argue about the world, not about the ruler.

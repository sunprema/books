Chapter eight

Getting the Right Numbers In, Every Morning

Real name: “Data and operations”

A cook can be great and still make a bad meal from bad food. The risk machine is the same. Its math can be perfect, and if the numbers going in are old, missing, or about the wrong thing, what comes out is wrong in a way that looks sure of itself. This chapter is about the shopping and the sniffing that happen before the cooking.

Where it goes wrong

Picture a trader who opens the machine at seven. It says her pile will lose very little if rates jump. She relaxes. Later someone finds that the ten-year price in the machine was from last Thursday, because the feed for that one paper broke and nothing said so. Lots of people find this kind of failure maddening, and that is fair: nothing crashed, nothing turned red, and the answer was still wrong. The fix is not a smarter machine. The fix is a morning routine that checks every ingredient and says out loud when one is bad.

Every paper needs a name tag

First, the machine needs one list that says what each paper is: when it ends, how much it pays and when, and what name tag it wears. The real name for the list is the “security master”. The name tag for a US paper is a nine-character code (the real name is “CUSIP”), and the last character is a check character, so a typo is easy to catch. The world-wide cousin is a twelve-character code (“ISIN”) that starts with a country and wraps the nine-character one. Match papers by tag, never by a description someone typed.

Two things trip people here. When the government sells more of a paper it already sold (a “reopening”), the new ones wear the same tag and are the same thing as the old ones. When it sells a brand-new paper, that one gets a new tag and must be added to the list before any trade or price can use it. A paper that shows up in a position with no entry in the list should stop the morning, not be guessed at.

What do we hold, and since when?

Next, what the desk holds. It comes from the systems that record trades (the order and portfolio systems). Two ways to count the same pile give different answers. A trade-date count includes everything you have agreed to buy or sell, even if the papers and money have not changed hands. A settlement-date count includes only what has really moved. For a government paper that is usually one business day after the deal. Risk wants the first count, since a deal you made today already moves your loss. Cash and money-lending want the second. Pick one on purpose, write it down, and label every number with it.

Positions also sit in books and desks, like drawers inside bigger drawers. The machine should add up cleanly from the small drawer to the big one, and each paper in a drawer should belong to exactly one drawer. If the big total does not equal the sum of the small ones, something was counted twice or dropped.

The end-of-day price tag

Each night the desk needs one agreed price for each paper (a “mark”). It can come from a data company that builds a price from many trades and quotes (Bloomberg’s version is called “BVAL” and comes with a score saying how much data stood behind each price; ICE has its own), or from the desk itself when no outside price is good. Do not mix times. The Treasury’s own curve is built from quotes the New York Fed collects at about 3:30 in the afternoon, while a data company takes pictures of the market at several set times a day. If half your curve is from one hour and half from another, the line you draw has a bend that is not real. Write down which time each price came from, and check the data contract for the exact times.

Sniff every ingredient

Before the cooking, run checks. Each one should give a plain answer: pass, warn, or fail, with the name of the paper and the reason.

  • Old prices. Compare each price’s time stamp to the cutoff. A price older than your limit, or the same as yesterday’s on a day the market moved, is suspect.
  • Holes. If the ten-year length is empty, do not quietly draw a line over the gap and carry on. Say which length is missing, and whether you filled it and how.
  • Wild jumps. A paper that moved far more than its neighbors did since yesterday is more likely a bad tick than real news. Flag it for a person.
  • Line sanity. Build the curve, then look at the forward rates it implies. A forward rate below zero, or a sawtooth that zigzags up and down, usually means a bad input or a bad way of joining the dots, not a real market. In some markets low rates are real, so flag it and let a person decide.
  • Name tags and totals. Every held paper has a list entry; every drawer adds up; the same trade is not in two systems.

The start-of-day routine

Put it all in order, like a morning checklist on a kitchen wall. There is a cutoff time: the last moment prices and trades may enter for the day’s run. Then the steps run in the same order every day. The figure shows one way.

The hard question is what to do when a check fails. There are three honest answers, and the machine should say which it chose. Stop: no run, a person is told, for things like a missing paper in the list. Use a backup and mark it: take yesterday’s price or a second source, and stamp every number that depends on it. Run, but warn: for small problems, with the warning shown next to the answer, not buried in a log. What it must never do is quietly use a bad number and look sure. Banks also expect that inputs are checked and changes to them are controlled, which is part of the model-risk rules in the next chapter.

A stale number must wear a label. If any input was old, filled in, or swapped, the answer should carry that fact with it, down to the single trader’s screen.

When the morning routine checks every ingredient and says so when one is bad, a trader can trust the answer, or knows exactly why not to.

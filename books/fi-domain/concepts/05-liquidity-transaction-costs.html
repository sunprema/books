Chapter five

How Easy It Is to Sell, and What Selling Costs

Real name: “Liquidity and transaction costs”

Say you have a table full of toys and a street market on a busy day. Selling one toy is quick. Selling a thousand takes all afternoon, and by the end buyers have seen you dumping and offer less. The price on the sign was never the price you got. This chapter is about the gap.

Where it goes wrong

Your machine says a book of papers is worth a lot and shows how the hedge would cut the risk. A trader looks at it and asks, “Fine, but what does it cost me to do that, and how long will it take?” If the machine can only say “the mid price,” the answer is a shrug. Lots of people find this part annoying, and that is fair: nobody posts one true price for a big trade. But a plan that ignores the cost of doing it is a plan that looks better on screen than it ever will in the real world.

Two prices on every sign

A dealer at the market holds up two prices: one they will pay to take a thing from you, and a higher one they will charge to sell it to you. The gap is the “bid-ask spread”. Buy and sell straight away and you lose the whole gap. Do just one side and you lose about half of it. That half is the first, smallest cost of any trade.

The gap is not the same everywhere. It is tight where lots of people trade the same thing, and wide where few do.

Some numbers from the New York Fed, using the public trade record: the newest papers are under 4 in every 100 of the debt owed, yet they get about 65 in every 100 of the day’s trading. As a paper ages, trading and trade count fall, pieces get bigger, and the real gap widens. One twist: papers that are likely to be handed over in the futures trade more than other old papers of the same age. Chapter three told you why.

A tight sign can lie. In bad days the quoted gap can stay thin while the amount anyone will trade at that price shrinks to almost nothing. Always look at the size next to the price, not just the gap.

For swaps, the gap is in rate, not in price. Turn it into money the same way as any rate move: the gap in basis points times the swap’s money-per-basis-point from chapter two.

What happens when you are big

Back to the toy table. The sign price is good for a few toys. For a thousand, buyers hold back, and you move your price down to find more of them. That is “market impact”: your own selling pushes the price against you.

Two things set the size of this push. One is how big you are against a normal day: the “average daily volume” is how much usually changes hands in a day. The other is how jumpy the market is. A common rule of thumb says the push grows with the square root of your size against a normal day. Double your size and you pay about 1.4 times more push, not twice. The usual form is: push ≈ a number near one × a normal day’s wobble × the square root of (your size ÷ a normal day’s trade amount). That rule comes mostly from stock and futures studies. Nobody should treat it as proven for Treasuries or swaps; use it as a first guess and fix the numbers with your own fills.

One sale, with numbers

These numbers are made up. You must sell 100 million of a ten-year paper. Say each million is worth 800 dollars for each basis point of yield, so the whole pile is worth 80,000 dollars per basis point.

The push is the big one. 6 × square root of 0.04 is 6 × 0.2, which is 1.2 basis points, and 1.2 × 80,000 is 96,000. That is the cost at the end of the sale; the average over the sale is lower, and your own data should say by how much. The lesson is not the exact number. It is that the gap alone said 8,000 and size made the true cost many times more.

A score a trader can read

A machine often needs one number per position so it can sort and warn. People call it a “liquidity score”. The trick is to keep it from becoming a mystery number that no trader can argue with. Three rules help.

  • Pick inputs a trader already believes. The gap, the amount shown at the best price, the normal daily trade amount, the age of the paper (newest or not), and your size against that normal day.
  • Make everything the same unit. Turn each input into money or days, not into points on a made-up scale. “Cost to sell it all now: 104,000 dollars” and “days to sell it if we are at most one-tenth of each day: 0.4” mean something. “Score 62” does not.
  • Always show the raw pieces next to the total. If the total changes, the trader must be able to say which piece moved. Put a plain reason beside any bucket you assign, and say which inputs were missing and what you guessed in their place.

If a rank is still needed, rank by cost to exit or days to exit, with the same inputs in the same units on every line. A weighted blend can sit on top, but only as a second column.

Checking what it really cost

After a trade, desks compare the price they got with where the market stood when they decided. That check is “transaction cost analysis”. Your model’s guesses only get better if you do it, because the real fills tell you whether the gap and the push you assumed were right.

Where does the data come from?

  • Dealer price runs: lists of prices and sizes that dealers send around. Fast and rich, but each dealer sees only themselves, and the prices are offers, not trades.
  • The public trade record (“TRACE”): trades in Treasuries are reported to the industry regulator and published for the public. Right now it is next day, only for the newest papers, and very large sizes are only marked as large, not shown. The Treasury has been asking whether it should come out sooner, so check the current rules.
  • Your own fills: the best source. They show what you, with your size and your dealers, really paid. Keep the time of decision, the price then, the size, the dealer and the place.

Where the trades happen

Think of two kinds of market. In one, dealers trade with each other all day through an open board where anyone can post a price and anyone can take it. The main one for cash Treasuries is run by CME under the name “BrokerTec.” In 2025 it added a second board in Chicago with smaller sizes and finer steps, aimed at people who trade the cash paper against the futures.

In the other, a client asks several dealers at once, “What will you pay for this?” and takes the best answer. This is a “request for quote”. The two big places for it are Tradeweb and Bloomberg. Swaps work the same way on regulated swap platforms. Futures are different again: one public board at CME, where everyone sees the same prices.

The venue changes the cost. A client who asks four dealers shows their hand to four. A very big trade may be done off the board in a block, with a size minimum set by the exchange. Those minimums are not the same for every contract and change over time, so read them from CME when you need them. The same goes for market-share numbers: the ones that are easy to find are old.

Whole contracts only

A futures contract is one fixed lump. You cannot buy 0.77 of one. So when the machine proposes a hedge, it must round to whole contracts, and the rounding leaves a small piece of risk behind. Ten-year contracts move in steps of half a thirty-second of a point, worth 15.625 dollars; the bond contract moves in a thirty-second, worth 31.25 dollars. Chapter three listed the rest.

Made-up example: the hedge needs to cancel 80,000 dollars per basis point, and one contract is worth 62 dollars per basis point. 80,000 ÷ 62 is 1,290.3 contracts, so you trade 1,290 and are about 20 dollars per basis point short. Tiny here. For a small book, one contract can be a big share, and the machine should show the leftover plainly.

Say it with the hedge. Show the exact answer, the rounded answer, the risk left over after rounding, and the cost of the trade (half the gap plus push) in dollars.

Once the machine knows that selling a lot costs more than selling a little, its plans stop being nice on screen and start being doable on the floor.

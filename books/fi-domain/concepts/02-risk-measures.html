Chapter two

How Much Do You Lose If the Numbers Move?

Real name: “Risk measures”

Picture a mixing board with one slider for every wait on the board of rates, from one night to thirty years. You own some papers. If a slider moves, what happens to what you own?

Where it goes wrong

A boss walks up and asks one thing: “If rates go up a little, how much do we lose?” You say, “About 80 thousand.” Then rates go up a lot, and you lose 95 thousand on one pile and gain on another, and the boss is cross, and so are you. The answer was fine for a nudge. It was never an answer for a shove, and it never said which slider moved.

Lots of people find the many numbers in this part tiring, and that is fair. Each one answers a different question. Learn which question each one answers, and you will know when to trust it.

The one-nudge number

Push every slider up by the smallest step the market talks about, one hundredth of one percent. How many dollars does your pile change? The market calls this “DV01” (the dollar value of one step). Some places say “PV01”; it is the same idea.

Take a ten-year paper that pays 4 each year per 100 and is priced at 100. One step up in rates makes it lose about 0.082 per 100. Hold ten million of them and one step costs you about 8,200 dollars.

Careful: signs

Some desks say the number is positive when you lose if rates rise. Others say it is negative. Neither is wrong, but a machine that mixes them will tell you to hedge the wrong way. Pick one, write it on the wall, and check it at every door the number passes through.

To add up a whole pile, you may add one-nudge numbers only when every paper was nudged by the same slider rule on the same board. A paper that pays in another money, or sits on another board, does not just add. And a total of zero can hide a lot: a long pile of five-year papers and a short pile of thirty-year papers can add up to nothing, and still hurt you when the board tilts.

Which slider moved?

So we nudge one slider at a time. This is the “key-rate DV01”: the change in value per step at one chosen wait, say two years, five years, ten years, thirty years. Those chosen waits are the “buckets”.

Why tents and not flat blocks? A block moves one wait and leaves the next unmoved, which makes a cliff on the board. A cliff makes strange forward rates. A tent rises and falls smoothly, so the board stays sensible, and the tents add up to one flat shift, so the pieces add up to the one-nudge number.

Two more choices matter, and teams argue about both:

  • What to nudge. Nudge the quotes you see on the screen (swap rates, bill and note yields) and build the board again. This tells you what to trade, because you can trade those papers. Or nudge the board’s own zero rates. That is cleaner to compute but harder to hedge with. Say which one you did.
  • How to pick buckets. Use the waits the desk really trades. Too few and risk hides; too many and the numbers jump around and nobody can act on them.

Measuring by nudging, and measuring by formula

There are two ways to get a number. In “bump and reprice” you move the slider, price the pile again, and take the difference. It works for any paper, even odd ones, and it uses the same pricing code as everything else. In the formula way, called “analytic”, you write down the exact rule for how value changes. It is fast and clean but you must write one for every kind of paper, and it can drift away from what the pricer does.

Bump size is a Goldilocks choice. Too big, and you measure a curve, not a slope. Too small, and tiny rounding noise in the pricer gets blown up. One step is a common choice, but it is a habit and not a law. Do the nudge both ways, up and down, and use half the gap. That cancels most of the curve and is called a “central difference”. A one-way nudge on a bendy paper gives a number that leans.

Why one number fails for a big shove

The one-nudge number is the slope of a hill at the spot where you stand. But the hill bends. For papers that pay fixed money, the hill bends up: you lose less than the slope says when rates rise, and gain more when they fall. The market calls the bend “convexity”.

Here are real numbers for our ten-year paper (worked out by price formula). Loss per 100 when rates rise:

ShoveRe-price fullyOne-nudge number onlyWith convexity
+0.25%−2.019−2.044−2.019
+1%−7.795−8.176−7.781
+2%−14.877−16.351−14.773

At a quarter of one percent the one-nudge number is close. At two percent it is off by 1.5 per 100, which is ten percent of the loss. Add the bend and you get much closer, but still not exact, because there are more terms past the bend.

Key idea

A hedge that makes the one-nudge number zero only removes the first-order risk. The bend, and any tilt of the board, are still there.

Two ways to price a what-if

Given a what-if story, like “rates up one percent and the long end up more,” you can find the loss two ways:

  • Full re-price. Build the shocked board, price every paper again, subtract. Slow, but right for any paper and any shape of shove. The market calls it “full revaluation”.
  • Use the numbers you have. Multiply the key-rate numbers by the moves, and add the bend. Fast, and good for small shoves on plain papers. This is a “Taylor approximation”, which just means “slope plus bend plus a little more.”

When is the fast way fine? For small moves in plain papers, and when you want thousands of quick answers on a screen. Sources differ on where the line is, from about a quarter to half a percent, so test it on your own pile instead of trusting a rule. Use full re-price for big shoves, for story shapes that tilt or twist the board, and for any paper where someone can choose to pay early or late. A good machine does the fast way for the screen and the full way for the answers that matter, and shows the gap between them.

The shapes a board can move

Boards rarely move flat. People name the common shapes:

NameWhat moves
ParallelEvery wait moves by the same amount
“Steepener”Long waits rise relative to short ones; the gap gets wider
“Flattener”Long waits fall relative to short ones; the gap gets narrower
“Butterfly”The middle moves against both ends

Two more words say which way the whole board goes. A “bull” move is rates falling (papers gain), a “bear” move is rates rising (papers lose). Put them together: a “bear flattener” is rates rising, with the short end rising more. Researchers often find three shapes explain most of what boards do: level, slope and bend (the butterfly). Key-rate numbers let you price any of these shapes by adding up the pieces.

Where did yesterday’s gain come from?

At the end of a day your pile changed in value. A trader asks why. The answer is split into parts, called “P&L attribution”:

  • Carry: what you earned from interest, less what it cost to hold the paper.
  • Roll-down: your paper got a day closer to its end, so it slid along an unchanged board.
  • Curve move: the board itself moved. Here the key-rate numbers do their work.
  • Spread: your paper moved against the board, for example because it is harder to trade.
  • Residual: whatever is left.

Do not hide the leftover. If it is large, something is wrong: a wrong date, a stale price, a bend that you left out. A small, steady leftover is a sign that your parts fit together. The next chapters return to carry and roll-down.

Know which question each number answers, and a big shove stops being a surprise and becomes a line in a table you already read.

Sources: Tuckman & Serrat, Fixed Income Securities; MetricGate, Key-rate duration (bucketed) and Bond duration and convexity. The table values were computed for a 10-year, 4% semiannual par bond.

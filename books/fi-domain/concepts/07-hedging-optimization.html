Chapter seven

Picking the Right Counter-Bets

Real name: “Hedging and optimization”

Say you own a big pile of long-dated government papers. If prices in town fall, you lose. So you make a second bet that wins when the first one loses. That second bet is a “hedge”. The hard part is not the idea. The hard part is picking which second bets, and how many.

Where it goes wrong

A trader holds papers that lose $50,000 every time the whole yield line moves up by one small step. She sells enough of another thing to win $50,000 on that same step. Done? Not quite. Tomorrow the line does not move evenly: the short end sits still and the far end jumps. Her second bet reacts to a different part of the line, so she still loses. Lots of people find this part annoying, and that is fair. Matching one number feels like safety, but it is only safety against one kind of day.

Two ways to match

The first way is one-number matching: make the total loss per small step equal to the total win. The real name is a “DV01-neutral” hedge. It is quick, and anyone can check it. It covers one kind of day: the whole line sliding up or down together.

The second way is matching at every length. You split the line into parts (two years, five, ten, thirty) and make the loss and win equal in each part. The real name is a “key-rate-matched” hedge. It costs more trades, but it also covers days where the line tilts or bends, as long as the bends happen between your parts.

Same pile, same plan, two answers. In the made-up numbers below, one-number matching takes a single contract type. Matching at every length needs several.

How many contracts

For a futures hedge the count is a plain division: the loss per step in your pile, divided by the loss per step in one contract. A contract is a promise to hand over one paper from a short list. The list has a cheapest member (the real name is “cheapest to deliver”), and the contract moves like that one. So the contract’s own step-loss is that paper’s step-loss divided by a fixed number the exchange assigns to it (the “conversion factor”, covered in chapter three).

With made-up numbers: your pile loses $50,000 per step. One long-end contract loses $200 per step. You need 50,000 / 200 = 250 contracts. Easy. But if the cheapest paper changes next month, the $200 changes with it, and the 250 is wrong. One published example had a pile with a duration of 8.574 against a cheapest paper at 7.969, and the count came out at 50 contracts instead of what a naive match would give. The point is that the gap is real, so rerun the count whenever the cheapest one could switch.

The hedge is not the same thing

Your second bet is a cousin of the first, not a twin. The gap between them is called “basis risk”: the chance they move differently. Two big cases:

  • Contract versus real paper. The contract follows its cheapest paper, plus the choices the seller has about what and when to hand over. If you hold a different paper, the two drift apart.
  • Swap versus government paper. Hedging a government paper with a swap leaves you holding the gap between the swap rate and the government yield (the “swap spread”). That gap has been negative at long lengths for years and moves with how much room big dealers have on their books, how much long-dated protection pension funds want, and how much paper the Treasury sells.
A swap hedge of a government paper trades one risk for another. It may be the right trade. Say so out loud, and show the gap risk as its own number.

What goes in the shopping basket

For far-away lengths, traders usually reach for a short list: the longest government-bond contracts (the real names are the Ultra Bond future, code “UB”, and its partner “WN”; check the exchange page for which code does what), swaps that run twenty or thirty years, and the real papers themselves. The Ultra Bond contract was made so that people could get much more long-end exposure per contract; at launch it carried more than half again as much step-loss as the older bond contract. In 2026 its open interest grew about a fifth, against a few parts in a hundred for the whole group of Treasury contracts, as long yields rose.

The house rules

Every desk has rules. Some papers may not be sold short at all. Each trader may hold only so much loss-per-step, or so much total size. A mandate may forbid a whole kind of thing. A good machine does not hide these. It writes them as plain limits on how much of each tool it may use: a floor of zero for papers that cannot be shorted, a wide range either way for futures and swaps, and caps on size. Then it works inside them.

Letting the machine search

With ten tools and six lengths, nobody can guess the best mix by hand. So you ask the machine to search for the mix with the least leftover risk and the least cost. The kind of search that works well here is called “convex optimization”. Picture a smooth bowl: put a marble anywhere and it rolls to the one lowest spot, never stuck on a side hill. That is why the answer is trustworthy and fast.

  • Straight-line goals (the real name is a “linear program”) are good when the goal is just cost or total size.
  • Squared goals (a “quadratic program”) are good for leftover risk, because squaring makes big misses count far more than small ones.
  • Absolute-value costs (“L1”): adding up each trade’s size, without caring about direction, tends to give answers that use only a few tools. Traders like that, because ten tiny trades are worse than three real ones.

In Python, a library called cvxpy lets you write this almost as you would say it. Here is a tiny version. Read the lines one by one on the right.

w = cp.Variable(n)                          # how much of each tool
left = A @ w + book                         # risk at each length, after
goal = cp.sum_squares(left) + lam * (c @ cp.abs(w))   # leftover + cost
prob = cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(goal), [w >= lo, w <= hi])  # house rules
prob.solve()
ok = prob.status == "optimal"               # check before you trust it

Line one: the unknowns, one number per tool. Line two: each column of A is one tool’s step-loss at each length, so this is what is left. Line three: leftover risk squared, plus a price on each trade. Line four: bounds are the house rules. The last line asks whether the machine really found a best answer.

The library ships with a few engines and normally picks one that suits the shape of the problem. Two common ones are called Clarabel and OSQP. You can force one with prob.solve(solver=cp.CLARABEL). Which one is the default has changed between versions, so ask the finished problem which engine ran, and scale your numbers (use thousands of dollars per step, not raw dollars) so the engine does not give a shaky “inaccurate” answer.

When there is no answer

Sometimes the rules cannot all be true at once: the pile needs far-end protection, but the only tools allowed are short ones. The status comes back as infeasible. Do not quietly loosen a rule and hand back a number. Say it: “No mix meets your limits. The far-end risk could only drop to X before you hit the cap on tool Y.” Then the trader can decide whether to raise the cap.

Whole contracts only

The machine’s answer will say “sell 247.38 contracts.” You cannot. Round to whole contracts, then work out the leftover again: rounding can push a length over a limit or leave a bit of risk you thought was gone. Searching for the best whole-number answer directly is a harder kind of problem, no longer a smooth bowl, so most desks solve first, round, and check.

Telling the trader why

A trader will not take a trade list they do not understand. The machine should say, in plain words, four things: what it picked, why these tools (they cover the lengths where the pile hurts, at the least cost), what it traded off (“I left $2,000 of risk at ten years because fixing it cost more than it was worth”), and what is still open (swap-spread gap, cheapest-paper switch, rounding). Numbers in the explanation must come from the solver and the risk engine, not from the words.

A good hedge answer has a receipt. What was left, what it cost, which limit was binding, and which risk the hedge added.

When the machine can pick counter-bets and tell you what each one buys and costs, a trader can say yes or no in a minute instead of guessing for an hour.

Chapter nine

The Rules for Trusting a Machine With Money

Real name: “Governance and model risk”

Say you run a big shop, and a stranger offers you a new scale. It looks fine. It is quick. But you will price everything you sell with it, and your name is on the sign. You would ask a lot of questions first. A big bank asks the same kind of questions about any machine that touches its money, and it asks them in writing. This chapter is the list of those questions, so your team can answer them before they are asked.

Where it goes wrong

A machine does not need to be wrong to cause harm. It only needs to be trusted without anyone checking it. Maybe it was right when it was built, and then the market changed. Maybe one person wrote it, one person tested it, and that person was a friend. Maybe it was used for a job it was never made for. Lots of people find the paperwork around this maddening, and that is fair. But each form on the pile exists because someone once lost real money without it.

The big rule about machines that give numbers

In the United States, the central bank wrote a short letter that banks treat as the rule book for this. Its code name is “SR 11-7”, and it is about “model risk”: the chance of loss because a number-making machine was wrong, or was used the wrong way. Do not assume your engine is too plain to count. The letter says a “model” is any method that takes in data and makes a number, and it can have a mix of math and human judgment inside. Your engine takes in prices and positions and puts out loss-per-move numbers. It counts.

The letter asks a bank to look after its machines in three ways, which we can picture as three keys on one ring:

  • Build and use it well. Say what it is for, what goes in, what the limits are, and show the tests. Keep the notes so a stranger could follow them.
  • Have someone else check it. A person who did not build it looks at the idea, the math, the code, and the results. The letter calls the spirit of this “effective challenge”: a check that is fair, from someone who knows enough and is free to say no.
  • Run the whole thing under rules. Who owns the machine, who may change it, how changes are approved, and a list of every such machine the bank uses.

After it goes live, the watching goes on. Someone compares its numbers to what actually happened, and to a second way of getting the same number. If it drifts, there is a written plan for who is told and what happens next.

What a bank will ask you

When a bank looks at your engine, expect questions like these. Have the answers ready in plain pages, not in people’s heads.

They askYou show
What is it for, and what is it not for?A one-page statement of use, with the jobs it must not be used for.
How does it make each number?The chapters of this book, written as your own notes: the line-drawing choices, the bump sizes, the day-count rules.
Where do its inputs come from?The morning checks from the last chapter, and a record of every bad input and what was done.
How do you know it is right?Tests against hand-worked cases, and against a second method.
What happens when it is wrong?A change log, a way to roll back, and a named owner.

Do our numbers match theirs?

A bank already has an official machine for risk. Your engine will give different numbers sometimes, and the bank will want to know why. This is the job of matching up: line the two machines side by side, one length at a time, and explain each gap. Before you blame the math, check the dull things first. Do both count positions the same way (trade day or settle day)? The same price time? The same curve? The same bump size? A gap that is just two different clocks is not an error, but it must still be written down.

The same idea applies to prices. A bank wants the prices that set its profit to be checked by a team that sits apart from the traders, at least once a month in the common rule. The real name is “independent price verification”. Traders may set the daily marks, but someone with no stake in the answer checks them against outside sources. If a price rests on one lone quote, expect the bank to shave it down to be safe.

Fences around the trader

A trader is allowed to hold only so much risk. The fences are called “limits”. Two common ones in this book’s world: a limit on the total loss for one small move of the whole line, and limits on the loss at each length (two years, five, ten, thirty), so that a pile cannot hide a big bet in one corner. The bank’s risk leaders and the people above them set the fences, not the trader, and they look at them again from time to time. Each fence needs three things written down: who owns it, what number trips it, and the steps when it is tripped.

When a fence is passed, a good rule book gives a clear ladder: who must be told, how soon, what facts they need, and what may be done (cut the position, ask for more room, or wait with a good reason). Your engine can help by checking every pile against every fence as it runs, and by saying plainly which fence is near. It must not move the fence, and it must not hide a pass.

Three people, three jobs

Think of a school trip. One child suggests a stop. A teacher says yes or no. A driver turns the wheel. If the same person does all three, nobody is checking them. Banks keep these jobs apart: the person who studies and suggests, the one who approves, and the one who does the trade. The real name is “segregation of duties”. Your engine sits in the first job. It suggests; it does not order.

Write it down, keep it

After a trade goes wrong, or even right, someone will ask: why did you do that? The only good answer is a record made at the time. Keep what the engine was shown, which version of the engine ran, what it said, who read it, who approved, and what was done. In the United States, firms that deal in papers have rules on how long records must be kept (often several years, with the early years kept where they can be found fast), and the records must not be possible to change or erase. Ask the bank’s own record team which rule binds them; yours must be at least as strict.

When a talking helper is in the room

If a program that talks in sentences (an “AI helper”) explains the engine’s output, the checking gets harder, not easier, because you cannot easily challenge something that cannot show where it got its answer. So the rules are simple, and you should state them up front:

  • A person approves. The helper may suggest, never act. Nothing is sent to market on its word alone.
  • Every number comes from the engine. The helper may explain a number, rank choices, and write plain sentences, but it may not do arithmetic or make up a figure. Each number it shows must be one the engine gave, and the page should show which run it came from.
  • It shows its reasons. Why these trades, what was traded off, and what it was not able to check. A trader should be able to say “no, because…” and have that written down too.
  • It is on the list, and it is watched. The helper, its settings, and its version are part of the machine list, and its answers are sampled and checked like any other output.
The helper talks. The engine counts. If a number on the screen did not come out of the engine, it does not belong on the screen.

When the machine is on the list, checked by someone else, kept inside fences, and every step is written down, a bank can say yes to it, and everyone can sleep.

Rules and the buyer’s own policy decide what binds you; confirm with the bank’s model risk team.

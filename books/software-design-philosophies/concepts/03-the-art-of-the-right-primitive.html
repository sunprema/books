The Art of the Right Primitive

Finding the fundamental objects and operations everything else falls out of

If the mental model is the picture a product draws, the primitive is the single shape the whole picture is drawn with. Pick it right and features you never planned fall out for free. Pick it wrong and every feature is a fresh act of carpentry.

What makes a primitive “right”

A primitive is the smallest unit a system commits to: the object everything is made of, plus the handful of operations on it. Ousterhout gives us the measuring stick: the best modules are deep — a lot of capability hiding behind a small interface. A primitive is the depth bet taken to its limit. You’re wagering that one small surface — append to a log, commit a snapshot, query a relation — can carry a whole product category on its back.

Rich Hickey’s vocabulary from “Simple Made Easy” sharpens the bet. Simple, he insists, is objective: one fold, one braid, not intertwined — and it is not the same as easy, which just means familiar. A right primitive is simple in Hickey’s sense: it does not complect — braid together — concerns that could stand apart. The wrong primitive almost always turns out to be two ideas fused: delivery fused with storage, naming fused with location, state fused with time.

Three primitives that carried products

Git: the content-addressed object

Git’s entire object store has four types — blob, tree, commit, tag — and essentially one rule: an object’s name is the hash of its content. Every command you’ve ever run is a walk or a rewrite of that little graph. branch is a 41-byte file naming a commit. stash, cherry-pick, bisect, rebase — none of these were in the original design, and none needed the model to change. That’s the signature of a right primitive: the surprising features cost almost nothing.

Kafka: the partitioned log plus an offset

Kafka’s primitive is the append-only, partitioned, replicated log — and, just as importantly, what the consumer is: nothing but an offset, a number marking a position in that log. Not a subscription, not a delivery contract, not an inbox the broker must tend. Because a reader is just a position, readers are nearly free: add ten consumer groups and the log doesn’t care. Replay is moving a number backwards. The dumbness of the primitive is precisely its depth.

SQL: the declarative query over relations

The relation is the oldest right primitive in our cast, and the most instructive, because it decouples what from how. A SQL query names the answer, and the engine owns the plan. That one separation is why the same interface spans SQLite’s single-file B-tree and DuckDB’s vectorized columnar executor — fifty years of implementation revolutions, one surviving surface. A primitive that fixes the question language instead of the answer machinery ages astonishingly well.

You know the primitive is right when the features you didn’t plan are the cheap ones.

Field tests for a candidate primitive

When we’re designing — and we will be, in Part III — we need to judge a primitive before building a product on it. Four tests, each cheap to run on paper:

  • The reach-around test. List the ten most common operations in the domain. If any of them must bypass the primitive to get done, the primitive is misdrawn. (Pre-Git tools made “compare two whole histories” a reach-around; Git made it a hash comparison.)
  • The free-feature test. Sketch three features you have no intention of building. If the primitive makes them nearly free, that’s depth showing. If each needs a new concept, you’ve built a feature, not a primitive.
  • The complection test. Ask Hickey’s question: is this one idea, or two braided? “A message” braids payload with delivery; “a log entry” does not. Braids are where future redesigns hide.
  • The count test. Count the kinds. Git holds at four object types; Unix holds at “a file.” Every added kind multiplies the interactions every tool must understand. One kind too few is a constraint you’ll feel; one kind too many is a tax everyone pays forever.
Careful

A primitive is not an abstraction layer. Layers add vocabulary on top of a system; a primitive replaces the system’s vocabulary. If your “primitive” sits beside twelve other concepts of equal rank, it’s just another concept. The power comes from everything else being expressible in it.

What the bet costs

Honesty requires saying what the depth bet costs. A primitive this load-bearing is nearly impossible to change later — Git’s SHA-1 transition has been underway for years precisely because the name-by-hash rule touches everything. And a primitive can be right for a decade and then drift as the domain moves. The relation survived; plenty of elegant primitives (remember object databases?) did not. The bet is still worth making — but it’s a bet, and Part II is about the philosophies that help you place it well.

So what did we actually gain? The second lens: products are carried by one deep, simple (un-braided) unit, and we now have four paper tests — reach-around, free-feature, complection, count — to judge one. Part II puts both lenses to work on the recurring philosophies.

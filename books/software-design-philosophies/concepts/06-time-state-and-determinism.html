Part II · The core design philosophies

Time, State, and Determinism

Logs, immutability, and replay — history as the source of truth

Ask a database what the balance is and it answers. Ask it what the balance was last Tuesday, or why it is what it is, and most will shrug — they overwrote the evidence. Our third philosophy refuses the shrug: treat history as the real data, and treat current state as something you can always recompute from it.

State and history are duals

The core insight is almost embarrassingly small. Jay Kreps calls the log “perhaps the simplest possible storage abstraction”: an append-only sequence of records, ordered by time. And then the claim that carries the chapter: a log of every change to a dataset, from the beginning, is the dataset. Fold the log forward — apply each change in order — and you get the current state. Keep the log, and you also get every state in between, for free. State and history are two representations of the same information; the philosophy simply says: make history the primary one.

If this sounds exotic, here’s the demystifying part: your database already works this way inside. The write-ahead log is the authoritative record every serious storage engine writes first; tables and replicas are downstream folds of it. Kreps’ move — and Kafka’s product — was to take the trick databases use privately and promote it to the architecture between systems.

Kafka: replay as a first-class operation

Kafka’s design makes the philosophy operational through one separation: the log’s retention is independent of its consumption. Records don’t vanish when read; they expire on a policy you chose. A consumer is an offset — so re-processing history isn’t a disaster-recovery procedure involving backup tapes and apologies, it’s moving a number backwards. Deployed a mis-counting aggregator on Monday, discovered it on Friday? Fix the code, rewind the offset, replay the week. The error is embarrassing; the recovery is routine. Systems that overwrite state have no equivalent of this sentence.

Git: the same philosophy, offline

Now look back at chapter 2 with this chapter’s eyes. Git is the log philosophy applied to source code: commits are immutable, content-addressed events; the repository is an append-and-repoint structure where nothing in the past is ever edited in place. Every Git power-tool is a history operation that would be impossible otherwise — revert appends a counter-event rather than destroying evidence; bisect replays history to binary-search for a bug; the reflog un-loses “lost” work because even abandonment was recorded. Two systems with no code in common, one design commitment: the past is data.

Overwriting state destroys evidence. Appending events preserves the ability to change your mind.

Determinism: the fine print that makes replay real

Replay only works if replaying is safe, and that requires a property the brochure glosses over: the fold must be deterministic. Same log in, same state out, every time. Two disciplines follow, and they’re where real systems earn or lose the philosophy’s benefits:

  • Keep the fold pure. If your consumer sends emails or calls external APIs mid-fold, replaying the week replays the emails. Side effects must be pushed to the edge — separated from the state-building, made idempotent, or gated so a replay can run with them disarmed.
  • Capture decisions as events, not as ambient context. If the fold consults the clock, a random generator, or a config flag, that input is invisible to the log and replay diverges. The fix is mechanical: record the decision into the event at write time, so history carries everything the fold needs.
Key idea

Hickey’s diagnosis from chapter 3 names the disease this philosophy cures: mutable state complects value and time. An overwritten cell is a value braided with an unanswerable question — “as of when?” Immutability un-braids them: each event is a value honestly married to its moment.

What it costs

The bill, honestly itemized. History is bigger than state, so the philosophy spends storage and compaction effort to buy reasoning — usually a bargain now, never free. Reading “the current value” needs a maintained fold (a table, a cache, a materialized view), which is machinery. Deletion becomes a design problem rather than a DELETE — privacy law doesn’t care that your architecture finds erasure inconvenient, which is why log-centric shops end up with encryption-based erasure and compaction policies. And note the model/machine split from chapter 2 holding here too: Kafka compacts and expires, Git packs and prunes. Append-only is the reasoning model; the storage underneath is allowed to be cleverer — what’s preserved is the promise that history, where retained, is primary and never silently edited.

So what did we actually gain? The third philosophy as a working rule: record events, derive state, keep the fold deterministic — and replay turns from catastrophe into a tool. Next: the philosophy of what you promise — invariants, stable cores, and open interfaces.

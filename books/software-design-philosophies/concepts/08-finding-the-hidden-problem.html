Part III · Designing the next great product

Finding the Hidden Problem

Where the next abstraction is hiding in plain sight

Part II gave us four philosophies for shaping a product. None of them tells you which product. So let’s study how our case-study products were found — because it turns out none of them was found by asking users what they wanted.

Why the real problem hides

Here’s the mechanism, and Ousterhout named its engine: complexity is incremental. No single step in a workflow’s decay is objectionable — one more pipeline, one more sync script, one more “that’s just how it works” — so each is accepted, and the accumulated weight becomes invisible precisely because everyone carries it daily. Users then do something that should fascinate every designer: they report the symptoms and defend the disease. Ask them what they want and you’ll hear requests shaped entirely inside the current model — a faster sync script, never “why do we sync at all?” The hidden problem is almost always a sentence about the model, not a complaint about a feature.

Four finding moves, from the record

Count the recurring cost nobody itemizes

LinkedIn’s road to Kafka began with arithmetic, not inspiration. Each new system was being wired to each other system with a bespoke pipeline — and Kreps’ essay does the unglamorous math: point-to-point integration trends toward O(N²) connections, each with its own semantics and its own failure pager. Individually, every pipeline was a reasonable decision; summed, they were an absurdity nobody had priced. The move: find the cost that recurs per-instance, total it, and ask what single abstraction would replace the N² with N. The answer — one central log everything writes to and everything reads from — looks obvious only after the counting.

Draw the map and stare at the empty cell

The DuckDB paper’s opening is a masterclass in two sentences: SQLite’s enormous deployment proves people want in-process data management; everything in-process was row-oriented and transactional; nothing served analytical workloads in-process. Two axes — deployment shape, workload shape — three full cells, one empty. The crucial detail is that the axes came with evidence: the empty cell borrowed demand-proof from its neighbor. The move: map an existing category on the two properties that actually segment it, and treat a populated row plus an empty cell as a hypothesis worth interrogating — not more. Some cells are empty because nobody needs them; the map proposes, the next chapter disposes.

Attack the premise everyone’s pricing in

Tigani’s “Big Data is Dead” move was neither counting nor mapping — it was calling a bluff. An entire industry priced in the premise “your data is too big for one machine.” He’d spent a decade inside BigQuery watching actual workloads, and the premise didn’t match: most data small, most queries recent. The move: find the assumption a whole category charges you for, and check whether it’s still true — especially if it was true once, because hardware curves quietly repeal old truths every few years. (And carry chapter 5’s caveat: he was selling the alternative. Premise-attacks demand the most evidence discipline, because contrarianism feels like insight even when it isn’t.)

Use the forced re-examination

Git’s origin is the involuntary version. When BitKeeper’s license was pulled in 2005, the kernel community lost its tool overnight — and Torvalds, re-deriving the problem from scratch, found that the incumbents’ model had never actually matched the job. Distributed, verifiable exchange of history among mutually untrusting strangers wasn’t what centralized version control was — so he built the data model that was that, in about a week. The move for the rest of us, who shouldn’t wait for a license crisis: schedule the re-examination. Take a workflow your team treats as weather — given, eternal, nobody’s fault — and re-derive it from the actual job, as if the incumbent didn’t exist.

Users report symptoms and defend the disease. The hidden problem is a sentence about the model.

The discipline: signal versus mirage

Signals worth chasing

A cost paid per-instance that nobody has summed · a “temporary” workaround now in its fifth year · a premise priced into every product in a category · expertise required for something that is, on re-derivation, bookkeeping · the same glue code written independently by every team you’ve ever seen.

Careful

Each move has a mirage twin. Counting finds an N² that a config file would fix — not every recurring cost deserves a new abstraction. The empty cell is empty because it’s worthless. The “false” premise is actually still true for the customers who have money. A real finding survives the question: if the new model existed, would the old workflow look absurd in retrospect? Git makes emailing version-numbered zip files look absurd. That’s the bar — retrospective absurdity, not incremental improvement.

So what did we actually gain? Four repeatable finding moves — count the unsummed cost, stare at the evidenced empty cell, attack the priced-in premise, force the re-examination — and a bar for what counts as found. A found problem is still just a hypothesis, though. Turning it into a product someone adopts is the next chapter’s job.

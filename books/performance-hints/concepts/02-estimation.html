Chapter Two

Estimation: Arithmetic Before Code

Before you measure anything, you should be able to guess — and be roughly right. Not because guessing beats measuring, but because a design you can't estimate is a design you don't understand.

Why guessing is a skill

Measurement tells you what a program does. Estimation tells you what a program would do — and that's the one you need before it exists. If someone proposes storing this index on disk instead of in memory, you want to be able to answer on the whiteboard, in about ninety seconds, whether that's a fine idea or an absurd one. You can, if you carry a small table of numbers in your head.

The numbers

Here is the table. Don't memorize it as trivia — look at the ratios. That's where the intuition lives.

Read down that column and the shape of a machine appears. Main memory is a hundred times an L1 hit. A disk seek is ten million times an L1 hit — the difference between reaching for a pen on your desk and mailing a letter across the country. And a branch the CPU guesses wrong costs about the same as ten L1 hits, which is why the second worked example below surprises people.

On the absolute numbers

These are anchors, not measurements of your laptop. Hardware moves; the ratios move far more slowly. Use them for arithmetic, never for a claim.

Worked example: quicksorting a billion numbers

One billion 4-byte numbers. That's 4 GB of data, and a quicksort does roughly 30 passes over it (log₂ of a billion is about 30). Now, what does that cost? Most people compute one term and stop. Let's compute two.

The memory traffic. One pass reads 4 GB sequentially. At the table's rate that's about a quarter of a second per pass. Thirty passes: about 7.5 seconds. Fine. That's the number you expected.

The branches. Every element, on every pass, hits a comparison whose outcome the CPU cannot predict — it's data-dependent and effectively random. One billion elements × 30 passes is 15 billion coin flips the branch predictor loses about half the time. Fifteen billion mispredictions × 5 ns is about 75 seconds.

The estimate

7.5 seconds of memory traffic, 75 seconds of mispredicted branches: about 82.5 seconds. The term nobody thinks about is ten times the term everybody thinks about.

Worked example: a page with 30 thumbnails

Now a systems question rather than a CPU one. You are generating a web page that shows 30 image thumbnails. How long does fetching the images take?

  • Serially, from spinning disk: each image costs a seek plus a read — call it about 15 ms. Thirty of them, one after another: about 450 ms. That's a page that feels slow, and no amount of clever code inside the loop will fix it, because the loop isn't the problem.
  • In parallel, across many disks: the thirty reads overlap, so the total is roughly the cost of one — about 15 ms. Thirty times faster, and you changed no algorithm at all. You changed the dependency structure.
  • Serially, from SSD: about 30 ms, because the seek that dominated the first case has essentially vanished.

Three architectures, ninety seconds of arithmetic, and you have ruled out the bad one before writing a line. This is the whole point of estimation: it moves the decision from after the implementation to before it.

Where does this code live?

One last piece of the estimate, and it isn't about nanoseconds. Before you spend effort, ask where the code sits:

  • Initialization code — runs once. Almost nothing here is worth optimizing.
  • Hot-path code — runs constantly. Nanoseconds multiply into machines.
  • Library code — runs inside programs you have never seen, on paths you cannot profile, for people who cannot fix it. This is where care compounds the most.

Same three lines of code, three completely different budgets. Estimation is how you find out which budget you are spending, and the next chapter is about what to do once the thing actually exists and can be watched.

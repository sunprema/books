Chapter One

Why Bother Thinking About Speed

There is a sentence that has done more damage to more codebases than any bug I know of, and the remarkable thing is that it is a true sentence. It just isn't the whole sentence.

The half-quote

You have heard it: premature optimization is the root of all evil. People say it the way you'd say a law of nature. What Knuth actually wrote was that we should forget about small efficiencies, say about 97% of the time — and that we should not pass up our opportunities in that critical 3%. The clause everybody drops is the one that tells you what to do.

Alongside it sits a line that I find even more useful, because it puts a number on the thing: a 12% improvement, easily obtained, is never considered marginal in engineering. Think about that. In bridge-building, in engine design, in anything that has to actually work, twelve percent for free is a triumph. Only in software have we talked ourselves into calling it beneath us.

The actual advice

Ignore small efficiencies 97% of the time. Do not ignore them in the critical 3%. The entire skill is knowing which 3% you are standing in — and the rest of this book is mostly about that.

What happens if you defer everything

Here is the trap, and it is a subtle one. The plan "write it simply, profile later, fix the hot spots" sounds impeccable. It even sounds humble. But run it to its conclusion and picture what the profiler shows you when you finally open it.

If every function was written with no thought for cost, then no single function is unusually expensive. The waste is spread evenly across two thousand of them. The profile is flat. There is no peak to attack. You went looking for a mountain to level and found a desert — same total volume of sand, nothing to point at.

Three bills that come due later

The document names three specific reasons that deferring is more expensive than it looks. They are worth separating, because they fail in different ways.

  1. Diffuse problems are harder to find and harder to fix. That's the flat profile. A cost you could have avoided in one line while writing becomes a week of archaeology later.
  2. Your users usually can't fix it themselves. If you wrote a library, the slowness is behind your interface. The person paying for it has no access to the code that costs them, and often no way to even name the problem.
  3. A system in heavy use resists change. Once a thing is depended on, the representation is frozen, the interface is frozen, and the fix that would have been trivial on day one is now a migration.
And yet

None of this is a licence to micro-optimize everything you touch. That is the other failure mode, and it is just as real: unreadable code, defended by a benchmark nobody runs, in a function called twice at startup.

The posture

So what do you actually do while typing? The document's answer is pleasingly modest, and I think it is exactly right:

The rule

When you have two ways to write something, choose the faster one if it does not significantly hurt readability or complexity. That's it. Not "optimize". Not "benchmark everything". Just: when the cost is free, don't pay it.

Notice how little this asks of you. It doesn't ask you to predict the future or to know where the hot path will be. It asks you to stop actively choosing the slower option out of habit — reaching for a map when an array would do, copying a string you could have viewed, taking a lock nobody needs. Each of those is a coin you can decline to spend, at no cost in clarity, at the moment you write the line.

And where should you spend real thought? On the code that runs a lot. It matters enormously whether a function runs once at initialization, on a hot path executed a billion times a day, or inside a library that a hundred other teams depend on. Same lines of code, wildly different budgets. Knowing which one you're writing is most of the job.

Which raises the obvious question, and it is the subject of the next chapter: how do you know what anything costs, before you have written it?

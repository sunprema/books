Sheet 11 · The short sheet

The Whole Book on a Few Pages You Can Pin to the Wall

Network people call this: the cheatsheet / quick reference

Ten sheets, squeezed. First, each sheet in the time it takes to drink a glass of water. Then the numbers worth knowing by heart, the header fields and port numbers you keep looking up, and the mistakes that bite people who already know a lot. Print it; it is made to be printed.

Each sheet in thirty seconds

01 · Layers, and who gets to be clever. Each layer gives a promise to the one above and hides how it keeps it. The Internet's real shape is an hourglass with IP as the thin waist, not the 7-layer drawing. The end-to-end argument: if only the ends can check a job fully (did the whole file arrive?), the ends must check it; the network may help only to go faster. Keep a conversation's memory at the ends (fate-sharing) so a router dying loses nothing. Three planes: data (move packets), control (decide the rules), management (watch and set up).

02 · Inside the box. Every packet goes: read headers → look up → change (TTL, MAC, labels) → wait in a line → send. The longest matching prefix wins. TCAMs check every entry at once but cost power and space; hash tables in SRAM do exact matches. One line per input loses throughput to head-of-line blocking (≈58.6% for big switches); one line per output at each input (VOQ) fixes it. Fast chips give every packet a fixed number of steps, which is why P4 has no loops.

03 · Learning the way. Distance-vector: tell neighbours your distances (can count to infinity). Link-state (OSPF, IS-IS): everyone floods a map of their own links and runs Dijkstra. Path-vector (BGP): carry the list of networks the route crossed, drop routes with your own number in it, and pick by policy first, length second. Leaks and hijacks spread because the most specific prefix wins. RPKI checks only who started a route, not the path.

04 · Sending fast without flooding. Throughput ≤ window ÷ round-trip time, so the window must reach the bandwidth-delay product. Loss-based control (Reno, CUBIC) fills the buffer before it backs off; BBR models bandwidth and the smallest RTT and paces; DCTCP reacts to how many packets got ECN marks. QUIC puts transport in user space over UDP with TLS 1.3 built in, separate streams, and connection IDs that survive a change of address. 0-RTT can be replayed.

05 · Taking turns. A line forms whenever packets arrive faster than they leave, and waiting grows without limit as load nears 100%. Big dumb buffers turn into standing delay (bufferbloat). AQM drops or marks early: CoDel watches how long packets waited (5 ms target, 100 ms interval); FQ-CoDel also gives every flow its own little line. Schedulers (WFQ, DRR, HTB) decide who goes next. DiffServ marks a class at the edge; the middle only reads the mark. QoS can't make capacity, only choose who waits.

06 · Who to trust. Start with a threat model: on-path, off-path, insider. TLS 1.3 is one round trip, always ephemeral keys, and the server proves who it is by signing with its certificate's key; any trusted CA can sign for any name, so CT logs make mistakes visible. DNSSEC proves DNS answers are true; DoH/DoT only hide them. Spoofed sources plus big replies give amplification attacks. NAT is not a firewall; zero trust drops "inside means trusted."

07 · Thinking in one place. SDN moves the control plane off the box behind an interface; "logically central" still means replicated (2f+1 copies survive f failures). OpenFlow fixed the fields; P4 lets you describe the parser and tables. NETCONF/YANG and gNMI set and stream state. Updating switches one at a time can loop or black-hole traffic, so use two-phase, version-tagged updates. Check configs before pushing them (Batfish) and rules as they go in (VeriFlow).

08 · Pretend networks on a real one. A VLAN tag has 12 bits (4094 usable). VXLAN wraps a whole Ethernet frame in UDP with a 24-bit VNI (~16 M networks) and adds 50 bytes, so mind the MTU. Hash the inner flow into the outer UDP source port so the underlay can spread load. EVPN (BGP) replaces flood-and-learn. The VNI is just a number in a cleartext header: isolation lives in the host's virtual switch. Plan address space before you ever need to connect two clouds.

09 · Air, movement, small batteries. Capacity = bandwidth × log₂(1 + SNR): more spectrum beats more power. Wi-Fi takes turns by listening (CSMA/CA), so one slow station slows everyone unless the AP shares airtime fairly. Wireless loses packets to fading, not only crowding. 5G: AMF (access), SMF (sessions), UPF (forwarding); slices are named by S-NSSAI. The edge helps only if the session's anchor is local too. LPWAN radios may talk about 1% of the time.

10 · Watching and what's next. Passive (counters, flows, samples) shows what users got; active (STAMP, ping, Pingmesh) shows a path even when nobody is on it. Five-minute averages hide microbursts. SLI is measured, SLO is the target, SLA is the contract; error budget = 1 − SLO. Most big outages come from changes, and the repair tools must not depend on the broken network. Coming next: post-quantum keys, entanglement networks, AI fabrics, space links.

Mistakes that bite people who already know a lot

Each line below is a thing that is true, that clever people get wrong, and that has broken a real network. Read them before a change, not after.

Design. The end-to-end argument does not say "never put anything in the network"; it says the network can't replace the check at the ends. "Two paths" is not two independent paths if they share a conduit, a software version, or a config push. SDN's split is about interfaces, not one central server.

Forwarding. Line rate is usually quoted for big packets; 64-byte packets on every port is the hard case. The FIB in hardware is not the RIB in software: overflow (remember "512K day") means slow-path or dropped traffic. Same ECMP hash on every tier → polarization; a few elephant flows can collide on one link.

Routing. MED is not compared across different neighbour ASes by default. BGP takes tens of seconds to minutes to settle, not IGP-style sub-second. Route reflectors hide alternative paths unless you use add-path. Two-way redistribution at two points without tags makes loops. Loose ROA maxLength invites forged-origin sub-prefix hijacks.

Transport. Three duplicate ACKs aren't proof of loss on fabrics and radios that reorder. Blocking all ICMP breaks nothing visible until big packets vanish (PMTU black hole; use PLPMTUD). BBR v1 can starve CUBIC in shallow buffers and lose to it in deep ones. Never put a payment or a POST in 0-RTT. DCTCP does not belong on the public Internet. QUIC still keeps order inside one stream.

Queues and QoS. Bigger buffers don't cut loss for loss-based TCP; they add delay. AQM only helps at the queue that actually builds (usually the access uplink or Wi-Fi), so shape to about 95% of the real bottleneck to move the queue there. Strict priority without a policer starves everyone else. MPLS-TE reservations are bookkeeping unless policed.

Security. Accepting any certificate or ignoring an SSH host-key warning protects only against people who listen, not people who change things. The VPN box and firewall are themselves internet-facing code and a leading way in. Zero trust is a way of building, not a product; a VPN with MFA is still "inside means trusted."

Overlays and cloud. Guests at MTU 1500 over a 1500-byte underlay with VXLAN: SSH and ping work, big transfers hang. The underlay only sees outer headers; without source-port entropy everything between two VTEPs takes one path. Mixed VTEPs on 8472 vs 4789 silently don't talk. Overlapping CIDRs make later peering impossible. A strange 63-second delay is six doubling SYN retries (1+2+4+8+16+32).

Wireless and edge. Fading losses aren't congestion, but loss-based control backs off anyway. Packet fairness on a shared radio isn't airtime fairness. Peak PHY rates aren't throughput. Edge compute doesn't help if the UPF anchor is far away. 802.11r can lock out old clients; 802.11v is only advice. Starlink's 15-second latency spikes are scheduler reconfigurations, not just handovers.

Operations. Averages hide microbursts and tails; use percentiles and event-driven telemetry. Small flows under heavy sampling are noise. A slow ping hop may just be a router that answers ICMP last. Traceroute through ECMP invents links unless the flow ID stays fixed (Paris traceroute). Plan capacity for the day one link is gone. Post-quantum hybrids add ≈1.2 KB to the ClientHello and can trip middleboxes that expect one packet.

These numbers go stale first: RFC and draft status (BBRv3, ASPA, digital twins), RPKI coverage (≈56% of IPv4 in Aug 2025), the BGP table size (passed 1 M IPv4 prefixes in 2025), DDoS records (31.4 Tb/s, Nov 2025), post-quantum share of TLS, Wi-Fi and 3GPP release dates, switch-chip speeds (102.4 Tb/s Tomahawk 6), and cloud MTU limits (AWS: 9001 in a VPC, 1500 through an internet gateway or VPN). Check before you quote them.

Keep the end-to-end check, leave spare room in every line, trust nothing because of where it sits, and test every change before you push it: that is the whole book, on one sheet.

Who You Can Trust When Anyone Can Touch the Wire

Network people call this: network security & trust

The first five sheets built a machine that passes boxes between strangers and mostly assumed every stranger plays fair. This sheet stops assuming that. It asks who could be lying, where they stand, what they can touch — and then looks at each lock, wall, badge and flood gate the network has grown to answer them. "Add a wall" turns out to be the smallest part of the story.

Picture a town where you can't carry your own letters. You hand each one to a neighbour, who hands it to the next, who hands it to the next, until — some fifteen hands later — it reaches your friend across town. You've met none of those people. Any of them could open the letter and read it. Any of them could change a word, drop it in a ditch, copy it, or write a new letter and sign your name.

Worse, the town's own helpers can be fooled. The board in the square that says which street leads where (Sheet 03's routing) can be painted over. The book at the town hall that turns names into house numbers (the DNS) can be given a false page. And on a bad day, a thousand people can show up at your door at once, all saying "a letter for you," so your real friends can't get in.

That's the whole problem. Security on a network is not one wall around one house. It is a set of answers to four questions you have to ask about every letter: Who could be in the chain? What can they do? What do I need to stay true — secret, unchanged, from the right person, or simply delivered? And who am I trusting to tell me so?

First, say who the bad guy is and where they stand

Before you pick a lock, write down who you're locking out. This written-down list is called a threat model: who might attack, where they sit, what they can and can't do. It sounds like paperwork. It is the most useful step on this sheet, because every tool that follows works against some attackers and does nothing at all against others. A lock with no threat model is a lock on the wrong door.

For networks, where the bad guy stands decides almost everything. Three places matter.

Sheet 03 already showed the third row happening. In 2008 one network in Pakistan announced a slice of YouTube's addresses that was more exact than YouTube's own, and longest-prefix matching pulled the world's YouTube traffic to it for about two hours. That was a mistake made while trying to block a site at home. Run the same trick on purpose, then pass the traffic on to the real owner after reading it, and the victim sees nothing wrong but a little extra delay. The routing system's checks on that — origin checks, roles, path checks — live on Sheet 03. This sheet's job is what you do when you assume someone will get on your path anyway.

Design every lock as if the bad guy is already on the path. Routing and naming checks make that rarer; they never make it impossible. If the lock is still safe with an attacker in the middle, the network under it is allowed to fail.

A lock that knows who is on the other side

Back in the town. You and your friend want to send letters nobody in the chain can read. The trick that makes this possible feels like magic the first time: you can agree on a secret with someone while everyone watches you talk. Each of you picks a private number, sends a public number made from it, and each mixes the other's public number with their own private one. You both end up with the same secret; the watchers, who saw only the public numbers, can't build it. This is Diffie–Hellman key exchange.

Now the catch, and it's the most important sentence on this sheet. A shared secret with a stranger is worth nothing if you don't know who the stranger is. If someone in the chain catches your public number and sends back their own, you'll agree a secret with them; they'll agree a second secret with your friend; and they'll sit in the middle, opening, reading and re-sealing every letter. Both of you will see a perfect lock. Locking out an on-path attacker needs two things at once: agree a secret and prove who the other end is. Doing both, tied together, is called authenticated key exchange.

How the web does it: one round trip, then quiet

TLS (version 1.3, RFC 8446) is the lock on nearly every web page, mail server and app. Its handshake does the swap and the proof in one round trip.

Walk it through. Your hello carries a public number. The server's hello carries one back, and from that instant both sides hold the secret — so everything after, including the server's certificate, travels locked, out of sight of watchers. The server then signs a summary of every word said so far, using the private key that matches its certificate. Your app checks two things: that the certificate really names the site you asked for, and that the signature fits. A middle-man who swapped the public numbers would have changed the summary, and he can't sign it with the real server's key. That check is the whole lock.

Version 1.3 also cut things out. The old way, where your app picked the secret and mailed it under the server's long-lived key, is gone: steal that key a year later and you could open a year of recorded letters. Now each conversation's public numbers are thrown away when it ends, so a stolen key opens nothing already sent. This is called forward secrecy. And the 0-RTT shortcut from Sheet 04, sending data in the very first letter on a return visit, comes with the warning you met there: a copy of that first letter can be replayed, so it should only carry requests that are safe to run twice.

Who vouches for the certificate?

A certificate is a note saying "this key belongs to this name," signed by someone your app already trusts. Those someones are certificate authorities, CAs, and your phone or browser ships a built-in list of many dozens of them. This is the deep part people skip: every one of them can vouch for every name. Your bank's safety is no better than the worst-run CA on that list.

In production · one CA, every name

DigiNotar, 2011

An intruder inside a Dutch CA, DigiNotar, made a certificate for *.google.com, dated 10 July 2011. Signed by a trusted CA, it passed every normal check. It was used to sit in the middle of Gmail for users in Iran; the investigators counted about 300,000 different addresses that asked for it. It was spotted only because Chrome had Google's own keys built in and complained when a different key turned up. Browsers pulled DigiNotar from their lists within days, and the company went bankrupt that year.

The fix that grew out of it is not a better lock but a public record. Certificate Transparency (RFC 6962) makes CAs write every certificate into public, add-only logs, and browsers refuse certificates that weren't logged. A wrong certificate can still be made — but now the real owner can see it.

The other quiet fix is making certificates die young, so a stolen key stops working sooner. The browser makers and CAs agreed in 2025 to cut public certificate lives from 398 days to 200 days (since March 2026), then 100 days (2027), then 47 days (2029). Lots of operators find this maddening, and that is fair: anything renewed by hand will break. That's the point. At 47 days, only renewal by machine survives, and machines don't forget.

One more change is already under your feet. A big enough quantum computer could, in the future, solve the math behind today's public numbers. Someone recording locked traffic now could open it then. So since late 2024, Chrome and Firefox mix a classic exchange with a new one built to survive quantum computers — the pair is named X25519MLKEM768 — and by late 2025 Cloudflare counted more than half of human web traffic to it using that mix. The secret is safe if either half holds. The signatures on certificates are still the old kind; changing those is a harder, later job.

The remote-control lock: trust the first time

SSH (RFC 4253), the lock you use to log into a far-away machine, does the same public-number swap but usually skips the CA. The first time you connect, it shows you the server's key and asks "is this right?" — and most people say yes. From then on it remembers that key and shouts if it ever changes. This is called "trust on first use." It's great against an attacker who shows up later, and useless against one who's there on day one. Large fleets fix that by having their own SSH CA sign host keys. OpenSSH also moved early on the quantum problem: a quantum-safe mix has been its default since version 9.0 in 2022, and version 10.0 (April 2025) switched to the same ML-KEM mix the web uses.

"Encrypted" is not "safe." A lock that skips the identity check — an app that accepts any certificate, a person who clicks through the SSH "key has changed" warning — keeps out listeners and lets in the middle-man. Most real TLS failures are this, not broken math.

Locks at different floors of the building

TLS locks one app's conversation. Sometimes you want to lock everything between two places — every box, from every program, between two offices. That lock lives lower down, on the IP floor from Sheet 01, and it's called IPsec.

IPsec comes in two parts, the same split as TLS. A talking part, IKE (version 2, RFC 7296), meets the other end, proves both sides' identities and agrees the keys, over UDP port 500. A carrying part, ESP (RFC 4303), then wraps each box. In "tunnel" mode it wraps the whole original box, address and all, inside a new one addressed office-to-office — the usual site-to-site private network. In "transport" mode it keeps the original address on the outside and locks only what's inside.

Notice what's missing: port numbers. A home router doing address sharing (§6.5) rewrites ports to tell its users apart, and ESP has none to rewrite. So when IKE notices a sharing box on the path, both sides move to UDP port 4500 and put each ESP box inside a plain UDP one (RFC 3948). It works, but it is the kind of workaround Sheet 01 warned about: the network's middle forced a change on the ends.

IPsec's biggest problem is choice. Each side offers lists of locks and ways to prove identity, and the two must agree; a mismatch, or a weak old option left switched on, is how real IPsec setups break. WireGuard took the other road. It has one set of locks and no menu. Each side is known only by its public key; the handshake is one round trip; and a box from anyone without a known key gets no reply at all, so a scanner can't even tell it's there. Its "cryptokey routing" ties each allowed address range to one peer's key, so the routing table and the access list are the same list.

Lock at the ends vs. lock in the middle

Lock in each app (TLS, QUIC)

The lock sits with the program that knows who it's talking to. It follows the user anywhere — café, home, office — and the network can be anything. This is Sheet 01's end-to-end argument, applied to trust.

Lock the pipe (IPsec, WireGuard)

One lock covers every old program that never learned to lock itself, and the network team controls it. But it protects only between the two pipe ends; past them, the box is open again.

Most serious networks do both: app locks because only the ends can check identity properly, pipe locks for the old systems and for keeping private address space private. Neither one makes the other unneeded.

Making the town's phone book honest

Every lock in §6.2 checks that a certificate matches a name. Before you get there, the name had to be turned into an address, by the DNS. If the DNS lies, you'll knock on the wrong door. TLS will usually catch that — the wrong house can't show the right certificate — but plenty of things trust DNS with no lock at all: mail routing, CA checks that a name is yours, old internal apps.

Poisoning the phone book from outside

Your network's name helper (the "resolver") asks other servers questions over plain UDP and believes the first answer that fits. To "fit," a forged answer needs only the right question, the right return port, and the right 16-bit question number. In 2008 Dan Kaminsky showed how an off-path attacker wins that race. Make the resolver ask about a name that doesn't exist — a1.bank.example, then a2, then a3 — and for each, flood it with forged answers saying "I don't know a1, but the server for all of bank.example is this one" (the attacker's). There's no waiting for a cached answer to run out; every new made-up name is a fresh try. One lucky guess and the whole name, not just one entry, belongs to the attacker.

Signing every page of the phone book

DNSSEC (RFC 4033–4035) signs DNS answers. Each zone signs its records with its own key. The zone above holds a short note, a "DS" record, saying "this is the right key for the zone below," and that note is signed by the zone above's key, and so on up to the root. A resolver that knows one key — the root's — can check any signed answer by walking down the chain.

Two honest limits. First, DNSSEC keeps answers true, not secret: anyone on the path still sees which names you look up. Second, it only helps where both sides do the work, and they've moved at very different speeds. APNIC's measurements in October 2026 put about 1.7 billion of 4.3 billion users — around 39% — behind resolvers that check signatures, but most names are still not signed, so most of those checks have nothing to check.

In production · changing the key at the top

The root key roll, 2024–2027

Every checking resolver in the world has the root's key built in. Changing it is like changing the lock on the town's main gate while everyone holds a copy of the old key. The new key, KSK-2024 (key tag 38696), was published in the root zone on 11 January 2025, giving resolvers almost two years to learn it. On 11 October 2026 the root began signing with the new key only; the old KSK-2017 is due to be marked revoked on 11 January 2027. ICANN reported that more than 95% of resolvers sending reports had picked up the new key, and told operators not to assume their automatic updates had worked.

A trust root is a single point of failure on purpose. The cost of the one key everybody trusts is that changing it is a slow, global, carefully staged project — and a resolver that missed the change stops answering every signed name at once.

The other half of the phone book's problem — people reading your lookups — is answered by locking the pipe to the resolver: DNS over TLS (RFC 7858, port 853) and DNS over HTTPS (RFC 8484). They hide what you ask from the café Wi-Fi. They do not prove that the answer is true; you're simply trusting the resolver you chose instead of the one the café handed you.

Hide the question vs. prove the answer

Locked pipe to a resolver (DoH, DoT)

Easy to switch on in one browser or phone. Stops local snooping and tampering. But it moves trust to one big resolver, and takes away the local network's view — which some networks used for filtering and fault-finding.

Signed data (DNSSEC)

Proves the answer came from the name's owner, whoever passed it along. But every zone has to sign, every resolver has to check, and keys have to be rolled without mistakes. Slow to deploy, brittle when done wrong.

They answer different questions, so "which one?" is the wrong question. A careful setup uses both: DNSSEC so the answer is true, a locked pipe so nobody nearby reads it.

Walls, doormen and badges

Now the part everyone starts with. A firewall is a doorman at the edge of a network with a list: which boxes may come in, which may go out. The simple kind reads each box alone — address, port, protocol — and says yes or no. The kind everyone actually uses is stateful: it remembers conversations, so it lets in replies to things you started and nothing else uninvited. Under the hood that's a per-conversation table, exactly like the flow tables of Sheet 02, with the same limits: it can fill up, and a flood of fake "new conversations" is one way to knock it over.

Sharing one address is not a wall

A home router shares one public address among every device in the house by rewriting addresses and ports on the way out and remembering the mapping, so it can undo it on the way back. This is NAT, address translation. Because a box arriving from outside with no matching memory has nowhere to go, NAT happens to block uninvited traffic. People often mistake that for security. It isn't a rule anyone wrote; it's a side effect of bookkeeping. Peers can deliberately "punch through" it (RFC 4787 describes the behaviours that allow this), and on IPv6, where there's no address shortage, there's often no NAT at all. If you want a wall, write a wall: a stateful firewall rule that says "replies only."

The doorman who opens your letters

A proxy stands in the middle on purpose: you hand your request to it, and it makes the request for you. That lets a company check what goes out and what comes back. But with TLS everywhere, a proxy sees only locked letters. So some companies have the proxy break the lock: it makes its own certificate for every site, and company machines are set to trust the company's CA. Look at that through §6.2's eyes. It's a man-in-the-middle, built by the owner. It works, and it is also one more machine that holds the keys to everyone's banking, mail and health records — and a machine that can get TLS checking wrong on everyone's behalf.

No trust for standing inside

The old plan was a castle: hard wall outside, soft and trusting inside, and a VPN as the drawbridge for people working from home. The problem shows up the day anything inside goes bad — a phished laptop, a hacked printer, a cheated-on contractor. Inside the wall, it can reach everything, because "inside" was the only check. Moving sideways from the first broken-into machine to the valuable ones is called "lateral movement," and it's how most serious break-ins turn small into large.

Zero trust (NIST SP 800-207, 2020) throws away the idea that where you stand earns trust. Every request to every resource is checked on its own: who is the user, is this device healthy and known, is this request normal for them — and the answer is "yes, for this session, to this one thing." The check is made by a "policy decision point" and carried out by an "enforcement point" sitting in front of the resource, often a proxy that does the identity check before passing anything on. Google's BeyondCorp, described from 2014 on, is the famous early example: staff reach internal apps from any network the same way, with no VPN, because the network was never the check.

The network-side half of this is microsegmentation: allow-lists not just at the outer edge but between machines inside, so the web servers may talk to the database on one port and nothing may talk to the backup system except the backup job. In a data centre this is usually enforced in each server's own virtual switch or host firewall — Sheet 08 builds the machinery — because pushing every inside conversation through one big central firewall would make that firewall the bridge from Sheet 05.

In production · the wall was the way in

Ivanti Connect Secure, December 2023 – February 2024

A VPN gateway sits on the open Internet by design; it is the drawbridge. In Ivanti's, two flaws chained together — one that skipped the login check (CVE-2023-46805) and one that let the caller run commands (CVE-2024-21887) — let anyone on the Internet run code on the box with no password. Attacks date back to at least 3 December 2023. In January 2024 the US agency CISA ordered federal agencies to fix them, and then, as more flaws followed, to disconnect the appliances from their networks by 2 February 2024.

Every wall is also code facing the whole Internet, sitting at the spot of greatest trust. A design where breaking the gateway hands over the inside is a castle design, whatever the box is called. Keep the edge box simple, patched and watched, and make sure getting through it still earns no trust past it.

Zero trust is a set of rules, not a product you can buy. Its whole content is one sentence: decide every access by who and what is asking, never by where the request came from.

When the crowd itself is the weapon

Every lock so far protects what's said. None of them stops a thousand strangers from crowding your doorway so your real friends can't reach it. That is a denial of service attack, and when the crowd is spread over many machines, a "distributed" one — DDoS. It has three shapes, and each one hits a different part of the machine.

Fill the road. Send more bits than the victim's link can carry; the line forms at the last bridge before them (Sheet 05) and real boxes drown in the yard. Fill the table. Send cheap requests that make the victim keep expensive notes: a flood of TCP "hello"s (SYNs) with fake return addresses fills the server's half-open conversation table. Fill the worker. Send real-looking requests that each cost the server real work: pages to build, searches to run.

Making someone else shout for you

The biggest road-fillers don't come from the attacker's own machines. Remember that UDP has no handshake: a server answers whatever address the request says it came from. So the attacker sends small questions with the victim's address written as the return address to thousands of open servers, and those servers send their much bigger answers to the victim. This is reflection and amplification. The size ratio is the weapon. The US-CERT alert on this (TA14-017A) lists DNS at 28–54 times, NTP's old "list recent users" command at about 557 times, and memcached — a cache server never meant to face the Internet — at 10,000 to 51,000 times.

In production · ten thousand times louder

GitHub and memcached, 28 February 2018

Memcached servers left open to the Internet on UDP answered tiny spoofed requests with huge stored values. GitHub took a flood peaking at 1.35 Tb/s — the largest seen up to then. About ten minutes in, GitHub moved its traffic onto Akamai's scrubbing network, which soaked up the flood; the site was up and down for under ten minutes in all. Memcached afterwards switched UDP off by default.

The fix for an amplification vector is boring and global: close the open reflectors, one server at a time. The defence for the victim is to borrow a much bigger road than any attacker can fill — someone else's.

The defences, from the source to the door

Stop fake return addresses at the source. If every network dropped outgoing boxes whose sender address wasn't one of its own, reflection would die. This rule has been best practice since 2000 — BCP 38 (RFC 2827) — and it is still far from everywhere, because it costs the network that does it a little work and protects only other people. That is the hardest kind of rule to get followed.

Keep no notes for strangers. Against table-filling, the server can refuse to keep any memory of a half-open conversation. With SYN cookies (RFC 4987), the server folds what it needs to remember into the starting sequence number it sends back. A real client returns that number in its final ACK; only then does the server build its notes. The fake addresses never answer, so they cost nothing.

Limit how fast anyone may ask. The token bucket of Sheet 05 returns as a rate limiter: so many requests a second per address, per user, per API key. Here's the trap, and it's real. A per-address limit does little against a crowd of a million addresses each asking politely. And the cleverest recent floods hide inside protocols. In 2023, "HTTP/2 Rapid Reset" (CVE-2023-44487) opened a request and cancelled it at once, over and over, on a few connections; the "how many open requests at once" limit never kicked in because each one closed instantly. Cloudflare saw just over 201 million requests a second, Google 398 million. The fix was inside every web server: count cancellations, and close connections that cancel too much.

Spot what's not normal. You can't block a flood you can't tell from real traffic, so defenders keep a picture of normal: flow records (Sheet 10's telemetry) of how many boxes, from where, to which ports, at what sizes. "Anomaly detection" means raising an alarm when that picture changes fast — a hundred times more UDP from port 11211 (memcached's port), say. Simple thresholds catch the loud floods quickly; the quiet request floods need per-request signals and still produce false alarms.

Use the routing system. When a flood is too big for your own link, the fight moves upstream, and Sheet 03's BGP becomes a defence tool. The bluntest is black-holing: announce the attacked address to your providers with a special tag (the BLACKHOLE community, 65535:666, RFC 7999) and they drop all traffic to it at their edge. Your link is saved; the attacked address is dark — you have finished the attacker's job for them, on purpose, to save everything else. Finer: Flowspec (RFC 8955) sends match-and-drop or match-and-slow rules ("UDP from port 123 to this /32, cap at 10 Mb/s") as BGP messages, so upstream routers filter for you.

Borrow a bigger road. Finally, the scrubbing service: a provider with enormous capacity in many places announces your addresses (by BGP, or answers your names by DNS), takes in all traffic, throws away the flood, and passes the clean boxes back to you through a tunnel. Because the flood is spread across all of the provider's sites by anycast routing, no single site faces all of it.

Where do the attackers' machines come from? Mostly from things nobody thinks of as computers. In 2016 Mirai scanned the Internet for cameras and video recorders still using their factory passwords — its list had about sixty-two — took them over, and used them to hit Krebs on Security at about 620 Gb/s and then the DNS company Dyn on 21 October 2016, making big sites across the US hard to reach. Through 2025, record after record — 7.3, then 22.2, then 31.4 Tb/s — was blamed on the same kind of crowd, now huge numbers of hacked home devices. The attack on your network is often run from someone else's badly set-up gadget.

Security vs. speed

Check more

Deep inspection, per-request identity checks, TLS everywhere, signature checks on every DNS answer. Each one catches something. Each one also adds work at the box that does it — and a box that does heavy work per request is exactly what a flood aims at.

Check less, faster

Stateless filters at line rate, SYN cookies, rate limits in the switch chip (Sheet 02). Cheap enough to survive a flood; blind to anything clever.

Put cheap, stateless checks first, where the volume arrives, and expensive checks behind them, where only what survived the cheap ones reaches. The order matters as much as the checks.

The danger already in the building

Look back over this sheet. Every lock and wall is a piece of software running on some machine, set up by some person, built from some code somebody else wrote. Each of those is a place to attack, and none of them is on the wire.

In production · the helper who was never a helper

The XZ Utils back door, March 2024

XZ is a small compression library that some Linux systems load into the SSH server by a roundabout path. Over about two years, a contributor earned the trust of its one tired maintainer and became a co-maintainer. Releases 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 then carried hidden code — in the release packages, not in the visible source history — that hooked into the SSH server's key checking so that whoever held the attacker's key could get in (CVE-2024-3094). It was found on 29 March 2024 by Andres Freund, a database developer who noticed his SSH logins taking about half a second too much CPU and chased it down. It had only reached a few test-stage Linux versions.

Every lock on this sheet assumed the code doing the locking was honest. Here the attack came through the lock's own parts, made by someone who had patiently been trusted. Nothing on the network would have shown it. Tracking what you build from, checking that release packages match their source, and funding the people who maintain small, load-bearing projects are now part of network security.

Set-up mistakes are attacks without an attacker. Sheet 03's 2019 route leak and Meta's 2021 outage didn't need anybody bad: one wrong setting, sent out, did the damage at Internet scale. The same is true on the security side — a firewall rule that says "any," a storage bucket left open, a management port facing the Internet, a test certificate trusted in production. Treat the network's configuration like code: reviewed before it goes out, checked by a machine against rules ("no rule may allow any-to-any," "no management port on a public address"), and easy to roll back. Sheet 07 shows how that checking can be automated.

Keep the control room away from the street. Sheet 01 split every network into the part that carries boxes, the part that decides where they go, and the part people use to manage it. The management part is the most powerful and should be the least reachable: on its own network, "out of band," reachable only from a few hardened machines, with every person's actions logged. An attacker who reaches a router's management login doesn't need to break any of this sheet's locks; they can simply switch them off.

Keys are a living thing. Every lock has a key, and keys leak, expire, and belong to people who leave. Short-lived certificates issued by machine; SSH access by short-lived signed certificates instead of keys copied to servers forever; a written plan for rolling every key, including the ones at the top of a chain. The root key roll in §6.4 is the slow, careful, everyone-watching version of a job every network should be able to do on a quiet Tuesday.

A network is as safe as its weakest daily habit. The math in the locks almost never breaks; the people, the set-ups and the parts they're built from break all the time.

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01 · threat modeling

A hospital's patient-records app runs inside the hospital network over plain HTTP, "because it's internal." Write a two-line threat model and say what the first fix should be.

Attackers: an insider or any broken-into device on the hospital network (Wi-Fi, a nurse's PC, a medical device) — standing on the path, so able to read and change records in transit; plus anyone who steals one staff login. "Internal" is a castle assumption. First fix: TLS on the app with a certificate from the hospital's own CA, so the path can't read or change anything; then put the app behind an identity-checking proxy so each request is tied to a user and a known device, and segment the records servers so only the app tier can reach them.

Check yourself — 02 · quantitative

A botnet can send 2 Gb/s of spoofed requests, and 60% of it sits in networks that drop fake sender addresses. Your link is 40 Gb/s. Is DNS reflection (take ×50) enough to fill it? Is NTP (×557)?

Escaping requests: 2 × 0.4 = 0.8 Gb/s. DNS: 0.8 × 50 = 40 Gb/s — just fills the link, so real traffic starts losing boxes. NTP: 0.8 × 557 ≈ 446 Gb/s, more than 11× the link; no local filter helps, because the line forms upstream. You need your provider's Flowspec or black-holing, or a scrubbing service. (Note that 60% filtering only cut the flood by 2.5×; the reflector's multiplier mattered far more.)

Check yourself — 03 · configuration review

Review this firewall in front of a web server: (1) allow TCP 443 from any; (2) allow TCP 22 from any; (3) allow UDP 11211 from any; (4) allow all from 10.0.0.0/8; (5) deny all. What would you change, and why?

(1) Fine. (2) SSH open to the world: restrict to a jump host or management network, keys only, no passwords. (3) Memcached on UDP open to the world — a ready reflector for other people's floods and a data leak; remove it, bind memcached to localhost or the app network, UDP off. (4) "Anything inside may reach everything" is the castle assumption; replace with the specific services and ports the app and monitoring need from specific subnets. (5) Good, and make sure it logs. Also check it's stateful, so replies to the server's own outbound calls get back in without broad allow rules.

Check yourself — 04 · protocol analysis

An attacker has hijacked a bank's IP prefix by BGP for an hour, and the bank's site uses TLS 1.3 with a certificate from a well-run CA. What can the attacker do to users, and what can't they? What changes if the bank's domain-name checks at the CA are done only by looking up DNS over plain UDP?

They receive the traffic, so they can drop it (an outage) and see who connects and when. They can't read or change the TLS sessions or pretend to be the bank: they don't hold the key the certificate names, so the handshake signature fails and browsers refuse. But during the hijack they may be able to get a certificate: a CA checking "do you control this name?" by connecting to the hijacked address would reach the attacker. That's why CAs must now check from several places on the network at once (CA/Browser Forum ballot SC-067), and why Certificate Transparency logs matter — the bank can spot the new certificate. If the CA's DNS lookups can be forged too, the attacker has two routes to a real-looking certificate; DNSSEC on the bank's zone closes the DNS one.

Check yourself — 05 · system design

Sketch DDoS defence for an online shop with a 10 Gb/s link, a login page, and a search page that costs 50 ms of database time per request. Put the defences in order.

Outermost: a scrubbing/CDN provider in front (announcing the addresses or fronting the names) so volumetric floods never reach the 10 Gb/s link; agree black-hole and Flowspec signalling with the upstream as a fallback. At the edge: stateless filters (drop UDP to web servers entirely), SYN cookies on. At the web tier: per-client and per-session token-bucket rate limits, tighter on login (guessing) and search (expensive); cache search results; a queue with a cap in front of the database so overload sheds requests rather than melting it. Behind it all: flow and request telemetry with baselines, so you see the flood starting.

A company says "we're safe from the Internet, we use NAT." What's the best reply?

Once you stop asking "is there a wall?" and start asking who could be in the chain, what they could touch, and whose word you're taking for who is who, the whole toolbox — locks that prove a name, signed phone books, doormen who check every badge, borrowed roads for floods, careful habits for keys and code — falls into place, and you can hand a secret to fifteen strangers and still sleep at night.

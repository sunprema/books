Taking Turns When Everyone Wants the Road at Once

Network people call this: congestion, scheduling & quality of service

Sheet 04 was the sender's side: how one baker guesses how much to put on a road it can't see. This sheet stands at the bridge itself and asks the questions the baker never gets to: how long should the waiting yard be, when should the keeper turn a truck away, who goes next, and can anyone buy a place at the front?

Go back to the narrow bridge from Sheet 04, but this time stand next to it. Trucks from many bakeries arrive, never evenly: three at once, then nothing, then five. The bridge takes one truck at a time, each for the same short moment. In front of it is the waiting yard, and next to the yard stands a keeper.

The keeper has exactly three powers. Size the yard — how many trucks may wait. Turn a truck away — when, and which one. Pick who goes next — the truck that came first, or someone else. That's all. The keeper can't make the bridge wider. Every idea on this sheet is a smarter way to use those three powers, and every one of them is a choice about who waits, because when the bridge is busy, somebody has to.

Why a line forms on a road that isn't full

Here is the first surprise. Say trucks arrive, on average, at 80% of what the bridge can carry. The bridge is idle a fifth of the time, so there should be no line, right? Wrong. Trucks don't arrive evenly. When three come at once, two wait — and the bridge can't "catch up" on the idle moments that already went by. Waiting time lost is lost forever.

The study of this is called queueing theory: the math of lines. For the simplest honest picture — trucks arriving at random times, each taking the same time on the bridge — the average time a truck spends in the yard plus on the bridge is 1 + load ÷ (2 × (1 − load)), counted in "box-times" (the time the bridge takes for one truck). At half load that's 1.5 box-times. At 80%, 3. At 95%, 10.5. At 99%, 50.5. The closer you get to full, the faster the line grows, and at full it never stops growing.

Three things people measure all come from this line. Delay: how long a box takes, waiting plus crossing. Jitter: how much that delay changes from box to box — a line that keeps growing and shrinking. Loss: boxes turned away because the yard was full. Play with the load and the yard size below and watch all three.

One more rule is worth carrying in your pocket, because it works for any line, however the trucks arrive. It's called Little's law: the average number in the line equals how fast things arrive times how long each one stays. If a 100 Mb/s link moves about 8,333 full-size boxes a second and the switch says its queue holds 50 on average, each box is spending 50 ÷ 8,333 ≈ 6 ms there. You can read delay off a queue-length counter without timing a single packet.

Random arrivals alone rarely hurt. What hurts is a sender from Sheet 04 that only stops when the yard overflows: it keeps the yard full on purpose, and every other box waits behind it. A yard sized "to avoid loss" becomes a yard that adds a third of a second to everything. This is "bufferbloat," and the fix is not a smaller bridge — it's a smarter keeper.

Turning trucks away before the yard is full

The plain keeper turns trucks away only when the yard is completely full: the newest truck to arrive is the one dropped. This is called drop-tail. It has two problems. It lets the yard sit full, so everyone waits the full length. And when the yard does overflow, it drops boxes from many bakers in the same moment, so they all slow down together, the bridge goes quiet, and then they all speed up together again — a crowd breathing in step.

The better idea: turn away a few trucks early, at random, while there is still room — a quiet hint to slow down, given before the yard is full. Doing this on purpose is called active queue management, or AQM. The IETF's current advice (RFC 7567, 2015) is blunt: deploy AQM, widely. What it no longer says is which one, because the first famous one turned out hard to live with.

The first try: a guess based on the average line

RED ("random early detection," Floyd and Jacobson, 1993) keeps a slow-moving average of the line's length. Below a low mark it never drops. Above a high mark it drops everything. In between, the chance of dropping grows in a straight line up to some top chance. The average moves slowly on purpose — the paper suggests a weight of 0.002, so it takes about 500 arrivals to really move — so a short burst passes and only a line that stays gets punished.

The trouble is those four knobs. The right low mark depends on the link's speed, on how many senders share it, on how far away they are — and those change every hour. Set it wrong and RED either does nothing or drops too much. Lots of operators found it fiddly enough to switch off, and that is a fair reaction. It was the right idea with the wrong ruler.

A mark instead of a drop

Turning a truck away to send a hint wastes the bread. Better: stamp the truck "the yard is getting busy" and let it through. The receiver tells the baker, and the baker slows down as if the box were lost — without losing it. This is ECN ("explicit congestion notification," RFC 3168), and it lives in the last two bits of the byte drawn in Figure 5.7: 00 means "I can't read stamps," 10 or 01 mean "I can," and 11 is the stamp itself, "congestion experienced." Any AQM on this sheet can mark instead of drop for boxes that say they can read stamps. Sheet 04's DCTCP is built on exactly this.

The better ruler: how long a box waited

In 2012, Kathleen Nichols and Van Jacobson asked a sharper question. Not "how long is the line?" — a line of 50 is nothing on a 10 Gb/s link and an age on a slow uplink — but "how long did this box actually wait?" Write the time on every box as it enters the yard; read it as it leaves. That is a ruler that means the same thing on every link.

Their keeper is CoDel ("controlled delay," RFC 8289). It also knows there are two kinds of line. A good line is a burst that comes and goes — it soaks up the bunching and empties out. A bad line is one that never empties: a standing line, which is just waiting with no purpose. So CoDel watches the smallest wait it saw over a stretch. If even the smallest wait stays above 5 ms (the "target") for a full 100 ms (the "interval," about one long round trip), the line is bad. Only then does it start dropping — one box, then the next after 100 ms ÷ √2, then 100 ÷ √3, and so on, a little faster each time, until the line clears.

// when a box leaves the yard
waited = now − box.arrived
if waited < 5 ms:  all_clear()
elif above for 100 ms:  start dropping
next_drop = now + 100 ms ÷ √count

Line one reads the stamp. Line two forgets everything the moment one box gets through quickly — the line has been short at least once, so it's a good line. Line three only fires after a whole interval of slow boxes. Line four is the speeding-up: the more it has had to drop, the sooner it drops again. That square root isn't random; a loss-based sender's speed goes as one over the square root of its loss rate (Sheet 04), so this spacing ramps the hint up smoothly.

Two numbers, and they rarely need changing: that's the win. Because the ruler is time, the same 5 ms and 100 ms work on a 1 Mb/s line and a 10 Gb/s line. RFC 8289 is still labelled "Experimental," as many queueing RFCs are — the label means the IETF wants more experience, not that the code is shaky.

Picking who goes next

Now the keeper's third power. So far there's one line and the rule is "first come, first served" — this is called FIFO. It is simple and it is blind. Picture one bakery sending a hundred trucks of flour, and a second sending a single ambulance-sized van of medicine. In a FIFO yard, the van waits behind all hundred trucks. Nothing the van does can fix that; it isn't the one causing the line. That's the deep problem with one shared line: no protection. Whoever sends the most decides how long everyone waits.

Let some go first

The obvious fix: two lines, and the keeper always takes from the "important" line first. This is strict priority. The van now crosses almost at once. The danger is just as obvious: if the important line is never empty, the other line never moves. This is called starvation, and it is what happens when someone marks a big backup job "important." So a priority line must always come with a cap — a rate it may not go past, enforced where traffic comes in. Remember that, because DiffServ in §5.5 is built on it.

Fair shares, in bytes

The fairer idea is one line per bakery and taking turns. But turns of what? If one bakery sends big trucks and another small ones, "one truck each" gives the big-truck bakery more bridge. The ideal is to take turns a single bit at a time — every bakery gets the same slice of bridge, whatever its truck size. You can't really cut a truck into bits, so fair queueing (Demers, Keshav and Shenker, 1989) pretends: for each arriving truck it works out when that truck would have finished under the bit-at-a-time game, and always sends the truck with the earliest pretend finish. Give some lines bigger slices and it's weighted fair queueing, WFQ.

WFQ comes with a real promise. Parekh and Gallager showed in 1993 that if a flow is held to a burst size and a steady rate at its entrance (a "token bucket," explained in §5.4), and WFQ gives it a guaranteed share at least that rate, its worst delay at that box has a hard ceiling — no matter what anybody else sends.

Fair shares, cheaply

Keeping every pretend finish time in order costs work that grows with the number of lines, and a fast switch has a few nanoseconds per box (Sheet 02). In 1995 Shreedhar and Varghese found a trick that costs the same tiny amount whatever the number of lines: deficit round robin, DRR. Each line has a little purse. Each time its turn comes round, the purse gets a fixed allowance of bytes, the "quantum." The line sends trucks as long as the front truck's size fits in the purse, paying for each. What's left over stays in the purse for next time. An empty line's purse is emptied, so nobody saves up allowance while idle.

Worked numbers · three lines, allowance 1,000 bytes a turn

each line has sent ≈ 1,000 bytes per turn

Fair in bytes, with no sorting. The price: A's big truck waited a whole extra turn, so DRR's delay promise is looser than WFQ's. That's why real keepers set the allowance to about one full-size box — no line ever has to wait a turn just to afford its truck.

The keeper most home routers should have

Now put the pieces together. FQ-CoDel (RFC 8290) sorts arriving boxes into many small lines by a hash of who is talking to whom — 1,024 lines by default in Linux — serves the lines with DRR, an allowance of 1,514 bytes (one full Ethernet frame), and runs a separate CoDel on each line. So a big upload's line gets its own drops and only its own waiting.

And it adds one lovely touch. A line that has just appeared — one with no backlog, like the van's, or a voice call that sends one small box every 20 ms — goes on a "new" list that is served before the "old" list of lines that have built up a queue. Boxes that are rare get to skip ahead, and the moment they start sending enough to build a line, they join the old list like everyone else. Nobody has to mark anything. The keeper just notices who is gentle.

Same link, same traffic, same bytes delivered. A thousand-fold difference in what the voice box feels — chosen entirely by the keeper's rule for who goes next.

Fair to flows vs. fair to people

One share per flow

Simple, needs no setup, protects every conversation from every other. FQ-CoDel does exactly this, and it is a huge step up from FIFO.

One share per person, or per customer

A program that opens 50 connections gets 50 shares of a per-flow keeper. Your neighbour's file-sharing app can out-vote your one video call.

Per-flow fairness is a good default, not a moral law. Where it matters who is paying, operators stack the keepers: first split by customer, then by flow inside each customer's share. That's the next section.

Lines inside lines

An internet provider's link serves many customers; each customer's share serves many programs. Hierarchical scheduling draws this as a tree. Linux's "hierarchy token bucket," HTB, is the common example: every branch gets a guaranteed rate and a ceiling. When a branch is using less than its guarantee, the leftover goes back up the tree and its brothers and sisters may borrow it, up to their own ceilings.

In production · the keeper is in the wrong place

"I turned on FQ-CoDel and nothing changed"

A home router has a 1 Gb/s port toward the cable modem; the cable plan gives 20 Mb/s up. The line never forms in the router — the router's port is fifty times faster than the plan. It forms in the modem, behind a deep FIFO yard nobody can configure, and calls stutter whenever someone uploads photos.

A keeper only helps at the bridge where the line actually builds. Put a shaper in the router set a little under the real upload rate — say 19 Mb/s — and the line moves into the router, where FQ-CoDel (or its descendant, CAKE) can manage it. The cost is 5% of top speed. The gain is that the upload stops hurting everything else.

Promises about how much you'll send

Every promise a network makes about "quality" needs a matching promise from the sender about how much it will send, or the network can't keep its side. The common shape for that promise is a token bucket. Picture a jar that fills with tokens at a steady rate, up to a size limit. A box may pass only if there are enough tokens for its bytes, and takes them. So over any stretch of time, what passes is at most the jar's size + the rate × the time: short bursts allowed, steady excess not.

A box that finds the jar empty can be handled two ways. A policer drops it, or re-marks it as less important — fast, no memory needed, but rough on the sender, which sees sudden losses. A shaper holds it in a line until tokens come back — smooth, but it is itself a line, with its own waiting, and it needs a keeper of its own. Policers sit at the edge of networks; shapers sit just before a slow link, like the home router in the last case.

Can anyone buy a place at the front?

So far the keeper has been fair and blind. "Quality of service," QoS, is the idea of telling the keeper on purpose that some trucks matter more. The Internet tried two very different ways to say that. One reserved room on every bridge for each trip. The other painted a number on each truck. Only one survived, and why is worth knowing.

Booking a seat on every bridge

IntServ ("integrated services," RFC 1633) offered real promises per conversation: a "guaranteed" service with a hard delay ceiling (exactly the WFQ promise above) or a "controlled-load" service that feels like an empty road. The booking was done by RSVP (RFC 2205). The sender sends a PATH message down the route, leaving a note at every router saying where it came from. The receiver answers with a RESV message going back up the same route, and each router on the way sets aside the room — or says no. The bookings are kept alive by re-sending them, every 30 seconds by default; stop re-sending and they fade away. This is called "soft state," and it means a broken router doesn't leave stale bookings behind forever.

You can already see the problem. A backbone router carries millions of conversations. Booking each one means millions of notes to keep, refresh and check, and a per-conversation line in the keeper. The RSVP rulebook's own deployment advice (RFC 2208, 1997) said backbones would probably not book each stream separately and pointed at the edges instead. They didn't. IntServ never ran across the Internet. It is the cleanest promise networking ever designed, and it lost to arithmetic.

Painting a number on the truck

DiffServ ("differentiated services," RFC 2474 and 2475) gave up promises per conversation and kept only classes. The old "type of service" byte in every IP header was given a new meaning: six bits name a class, the "DSCP," and the last two are the ECN bits from §5.2.

The work is split in two. At the edge of a network, where traffic comes in, a box does the hard part: works out what each packet is, checks it against a token bucket for that customer's class, and paints (or re-paints) the number. In the core, routers do only the easy part: read six bits and treat the box the way that class is treated. That treatment is called a "per-hop behaviour," PHB. No notes per conversation anywhere in the middle. The middle stays fast and simple — the same shape as Sheet 01's argument for keeping the clever parts at the edges.

Book a seat vs. paint a number

Reserve (IntServ + RSVP)

A real promise for each conversation, with a provable delay ceiling. Every router must remember every conversation, which does not scale to a backbone.

Mark (DiffServ)

Scales to anything: the core keeps no notes. But it promises only "this class is treated better than that one" — and only if the edges police honestly and nobody sells too much of the good class.

DiffServ won because it fits the Internet's grain: thin middle, work at the edge. The price is that "quality" became a matter of planning — keep the good class small enough that its line never forms — rather than a promise you can prove.

What really happens to the paint

Inside one company's network, DiffServ works well and is used everywhere: voice phones paint their boxes EF, switches give them a short line, and calls sound fine when the office upload is busy. Across the public Internet it is a different story. A 2018 study sent marked boxes over about 25,000 Internet paths and found that on more than 70%, something along the way changed the number — often wiping it to zero, sometimes re-painting it to a different class, occasionally lower than a box marked "less important." Providers have good reasons: a number painted by a stranger is a request, not a right, and honouring it would let anyone mark everything EF.

So the honest rule is: DSCP is a local language. Trust it where you set it or agreed on it. At every border, decide — trust, re-paint to your own scheme, or wipe — and police whatever you trust. Wi-Fi is one more border inside your own building: RFC 8325 says how to turn the numbers into Wi-Fi's four "access categories" (voice, video, best effort, background), which fight for airtime with different waiting rules before sending.

Bookings that survived: one per tunnel

RSVP did survive, in a different job. Sheet 03's MPLS traffic engineering uses RSVP-TE (RFC 3209) to set up tunnels across a backbone and book bandwidth for each one. The trick is the same as DiffServ's: book for a bundle, not for each conversation. A backbone might have a few thousand tunnels — fine — rather than millions of calls. Each tunnel's booking is checked against what the links have left, so the paths fit together like a packed suitcase.

A traffic-engineering booking is a number in the routers' bookkeeping, not a wall around the traffic. If a tunnel booked 2 Gb/s and 5 Gb/s shows up, the routers still send all 5 unless someone set a policer. Bookings plan the roads; keepers and policers enforce them.

Three kinds of traffic, three kinds of wanting

Everything above is a tool. Which tool depends on what the traffic wants, and it wants three different things.

Notice what jitter does to a call. A voice app keeps a small "jitter buffer": it holds incoming sound for a few tens of milliseconds so it can play it out smoothly. A box that arrives later than that buffer is as good as lost. So jitter turns into delay (a bigger buffer) or into loss (a box too late to play) — you can choose which, but not neither.

A short line for everyone who asks politely

The newest idea gives up on asking the network for priority at all. L4S ("low latency, low loss, scalable throughput," RFCs 9330–9332, 2023) asks for a short line instead. A sender that promises to react gently and often to ECN stamps — the DCTCP-style "trim a little" behaviour from Sheet 04 — sets its ECN bits to 01. The keeper puts those boxes in a second, shallow line that starts stamping when a box has waited about a millisecond. Ordinary senders stay in the classic line with a normal AQM.

The clever part is how the two lines stay fair. The keeper ties their signals together — "coupled": the L4S line's stamping chance is set to twice the square root of the classic line's drop chance, which is what makes a gentle-reacting sender and a halve-on-loss sender end up with about the same speed. So L4S gets no more bridge, just less waiting. Nobody pays, nobody is policed, and the classic traffic loses nothing.

In production · a cable network goes first

Comcast turns on L4S, January 2025

After field trials from 2023, Comcast began switching on L4S-based low-latency service in its cable network in January 2025, starting with Atlanta, Chicago, Colorado Springs, Philadelphia, Rockville and San Francisco. The launch apps were FaceTime (Apple had shipped L4S support in iOS 17 and macOS Sonoma), NVIDIA's GeForce NOW, Meta's headsets and Valve's Steam games.

It works only where both ends and the bridge where the line builds take part — here the cable link, the usual bottleneck. That's the old deployment lesson again, and why it started with one company that owns that bridge and a few apps that own their senders.

The keeper can never make the bridge wider. Every tool on this sheet only chooses who waits. Mark too much traffic as special and it's just the ordinary line again, with a fancier name.

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01 · quantitative

A switch port runs at 10 Gb/s. Its queue counter averages 400 full-size (1,500-byte) boxes. How long does a box spend in that queue on average? If the load is random and steady, about what load does that suggest?

Little's law. One box takes 1,500 × 8 ÷ 10¹⁰ = 1.2 µs, so the port moves about 833,000 boxes/s at full load. Time in queue ≈ 400 boxes × 1.2 µs ≈ 480 µs (exactly, 400 ÷ arrival rate, which is a bit more below full load). An average of 400 waiting under random arrivals with fixed service needs load very close to 1 — about 99.9%. More likely the arrivals are bursty or a loss-based sender is filling the queue on purpose: look for a standing line, not bad luck.

Check yourself — 02 · configuration review

An office router has two lines: strict priority for anything marked EF, FIFO for the rest. A new video tool marks all its traffic EF. What happens to email and web at 9 am, and what's the one-line fix?

Starvation: when the video tool fills the priority line, the FIFO line never gets served, and email and web stall. And the calls get worse too, because they now wait behind video. Fix: police EF at the edge to a fixed rate (enough for the real voice calls) and re-mark or drop the excess; move the video tool to AF4x with its own fair share.

Check yourself — 03 · protocol analysis

CoDel's target is 5 ms. A 1 Mb/s satellite uplink sends one 1,500-byte box every 12 ms. Why would CoDel misbehave here, and what does RFC 8290 / the Linux code do about it?

One box alone takes 12 ms to send, so even a single waiting box exceeds the 5 ms target; CoDel would keep dropping on a queue of one or two. Implementations don't drop when the queue holds no more than about one MTU of bytes (CoDel's "queue tiny" check), and on very slow links operators raise the target to more than one box-time and shrink the DRR quantum for FQ-CoDel. The ruler is time, but time can't be shorter than one box.

Check yourself — 04 · system design

You run a 10 Gb/s link shared by two customers, A paying for 6 Gb/s and B for 4 Gb/s. Each wants its own calls to stay smooth while its own backups run. Sketch the keeper.

A hierarchy. Top level: two branches, A rate 6G / B rate 4G, both with ceiling 10G so idle share can be borrowed. Inside each branch: a small strict-priority (or EF) class for voice, policed to a few percent of that branch; then FQ-CoDel for everything else, so web clicks don't wait behind the backup. Classify by the customer's DSCP only if you trust it — otherwise by your own rules at the edge, re-marking as you go.

Why did DiffServ, and not IntServ, become how networks do quality of service?

Once you see the bridge's keeper with only three powers — how long the yard is, when to turn a truck away, and who goes next — every acronym here is just a choice about who waits, and choosing well is why a video call can stay smooth while the house uploads a year of photos over the same small road.

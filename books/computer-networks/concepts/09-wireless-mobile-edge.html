Networks Made of Air, Moving People, and Small Batteries

Network people call this: wireless, mobile & edge networks

On a wire, the link has a fixed speed, nobody else can talk on it, and it stays where you put it. In the air, none of that is true. Everyone shares the same space, the speed changes from one moment to the next, the people move, and some of them run on a battery the size of a coin. This sheet is about how networks are built when the link itself is the hard part.

Picture one big room at a party. There is only one rule: if two people talk at once, nobody understands either of them. So people wait for a quiet moment, then talk. Near the speakers it is loud, so you talk slowly and say things twice. Across the room it is quiet, so you can talk fast.

Now add trouble. One guest at the far end, by the door, can only talk very slowly. Every time it is their turn, the whole room waits a long time. Some guests keep walking from this room into the next one, in the middle of a conversation. Out in the garden there is a small sensor that talks once an hour and has to last ten years on one battery. And far above, someone is shouting down from a moving balloon.

The room is the air, called the radio "channel." Every idea in this sheet is a way to make that room work: how fast you can talk in it, how to share turns, how to follow people who walk out, where to put the food so nobody walks far, and how to talk to the sensor and the balloon at all.

How much you can say in a noisy room

The first fact about the air is that it is a shared, noisy, changing link. A radio sends on a slice of frequencies, its spectrum (how wide the slice is: its bandwidth). Your signal gets weaker fast with distance. In open air, every time you double the distance, the power that arrives drops to a quarter (about 6 dB). Through walls, it drops much faster. The signal also bounces off things, and the bounced copies add up or cancel out as you move even a few centimetres. That is called "fading." Meanwhile, other radios on the same frequencies add noise you can't control.

In 1948 Claude Shannon worked out the hard limit for any such link. The most you can send depends on just two things: how wide your slice is, and how loud your signal is next to the noise (the "signal-to-noise ratio", SNR). Width helps a lot. Loudness helps only a little: past a point, doubling your power buys you about one more bit per second for each hertz of slice.

That is why every new Wi-Fi and cellular generation fights for wider slices of spectrum, and why the same phone is three times slower at the far end of the room. Width is bought from regulators, at auctions. Loudness is limited by batteries, by safety rules, and by the fact that your loudness is everyone else's noise.

Talking slower when it gets loud

Since the SNR changes all the time, a radio can't pick one speed. Before each frame it picks a "modulation and coding scheme" (MCS): how many bits to pack into each symbol, and how many extra check bits to add so errors can be fixed. Packed tight, it's fast but breaks easily. Packed loose, it's slow but survives noise. This choice is called link adaptation. Cellular base stations base it on what the phone reports about its signal. Wi-Fi usually learns by trying. Linux's Wi-Fi code uses an algorithm called Minstrel-HT: it keeps success counts for groups of rates, tries a different rate now and then to see if it does better, and drops to fewer streams when the faster settings start failing.

More antennas, more paths

The bouncing that causes fading has a gift in it. With several antennas on each end, each antenna hears a different mix of the bounced copies. With enough bounce paths and a strong signal, the receiver can pull apart separate streams sent at the same time on the same frequencies. With N antennas sending and M receiving, you get up to the smaller of N and M streams. This is MIMO (many antennas in, many antennas out). Without much bouncing, or when the signal is weak, the same antennas are used another way: to aim the signal at one listener (beamforming), or to send copies so one survives. "MU-MIMO" points different streams at different users at once, so the room can hold more than one conversation after all, as long as the listeners stand in different places.

Waiting for a quiet moment

Cellular networks have a boss: the base station tells every phone exactly when and on which frequencies it may talk. Wi-Fi has no boss. It uses CSMA/CA ("listen before you talk, and avoid crashes"). Listen; if the air is quiet, wait a random short time; then send. The receiver sends back a quick "got it" (an ACK). No ACK means a crash or a fade, so wait a longer random time and try again. That resend happens right here in the link, not end to end. Sheet 01 showed why that's worth it: on a link that loses one frame in ten, fixing it close by is much cheaper than making the far end resend.

Listening has a blind spot. Two laptops at opposite sides of an access point can both hear the access point but not each other. Each one hears silence, both talk, and the frames crash at the access point. This is the "hidden station" problem. The fix, RTS/CTS, is to ask first ("can I talk?") and have the access point answer out loud ("yes, A, everyone else hush"). It costs two extra short frames per turn, so it's mostly turned on only for big frames.

A lost frame on the air is usually not a full queue. It's a fade or a crash. Loss-based congestion control (Sheet 04) reads every loss as "slow down," so the link hides most losses with its own fast resends. The price is delay that jumps around: a frame that needed four tries arrives late, and the radio keeps big buffers to cover the gaps. That is where much of the bufferbloat on Wi-Fi and cellular links comes from.

The slow talker who slows down everyone

Back at the party. The rule "everyone gets an equal turn" sounds fair. But in Wi-Fi, a turn is one frame, not one stretch of time. A laptop by the door talking at 1 Mb/s needs ten times as long to send a frame as a laptop near the access point talking at 11 Mb/s. Equal turns mean the slow one holds the air most of the time. In 2003, Heusse and colleagues showed what that does: every station falls to roughly the speed of the slowest one. They called it the "performance anomaly." Lots of people find this maddening once they see it, and that is fair: one old phone in the corner can make a brand-new laptop feel broken.

Worked numbers · two stations, equal turns
One 1500-byte frame
12,000 bits, plus about 0.4 ms of fixed cost per frame (waiting gaps, preamble, the ACK); a rough round number
Fast station at 11 Mb/s
12,000 ÷ 11 + 400 µs ≈ 1.49 ms per frame
Slow station at 1 Mb/s
12,000 ÷ 1 + 400 µs ≈ 12.4 ms per frame
One round, each sends one frame
≈ 13.9 ms, so each gets 12,000 bits per 13.9 ms ≈ 0.86 Mb/s
Fast station alone
12,000 bits per 1.49 ms ≈ 8.1 Mb/s
Equal time instead
fast gets half the air ≈ 4.0 Mb/s; slow gets ≈ 0.48 Mb/s

Equal turns: the fast station loses 90% of what it could do, and the room moves 1.7 Mb/s. Equal time: the fast station keeps half its speed, the slow one gives up a little, and the room moves 4.5 Mb/s. Fair "by frames" and fair "by time" are different promises.

Try it. Add fast stations, change their speed, and move the slow one in and out of the room. Then switch from equal turns to equal time.

In production · fixing the room from one side

Airtime fairness in Linux Wi-Fi, 2016–2017

Høiland-Jørgensen, Kazior, Täht, Hurtig and Brunstrom attacked two Wi-Fi problems at once. First, huge queues inside the Wi-Fi driver and firmware, one shared line for everybody, meant a big download added hundreds of milliseconds of wait to everyone's packets. Second, the slow-talker problem. Their fix lived entirely in the access point. It gave each station its own short queue managed with FQ-CoDel (Sheet 05), and a scheduler that hands out airtime, not frames. Phones and laptops needed no change at all. They reported about ten times lower delay under load, big gains in total throughput with mixed-speed stations, and nearly perfect airtime fairness. The work went into the main Linux kernel, so it now runs in a great many home routers.

The access point is the only party that sees every station's speed and can sort out its turns, so that is where the fix belongs. And the two problems were one problem: who waits in which line, for how long.

How Wi-Fi grew up

Each Wi-Fi generation is one of the tools above, made bigger: wider slices, tighter packing, more streams, and from Wi-Fi 6 on, a better way to share turns. The headline speeds add every stream at the widest slice and tightest packing with a perfect signal. Nobody gets them. Half-duplex air, the per-frame costs and sharing with neighbours take most of it. Read them as "how much bigger the room got," not "what my laptop will do."

Wi-Fi 8's goal is the telling part. After twenty years of chasing peak speed, the standard's own aims are now about the worst moments: throughput at the room's edge, the slowest few percent of packets, loss while moving. That is the lesson of §9.2 written into a standard: on shared air, the average is not what people notice.

The phone network: a room with a boss

A cellular network is the opposite design. The base station (in 5G, the "gNB") is a strict host. It decides who speaks, when, on which frequencies, and how tightly packed, every millisecond or less. That costs a lot of signalling, but it means no crashes, planned turns, and the ability to make promises about delay. Behind the radio sits the "core," which in 5G (3GPP TS 23.501) is split cleanly into Sheet 01's planes.

The core's job is to give a phone a PDU session: a "pipe" from the phone to one data network, with one address, anchored at one UPF. The pipe between the base station and the UPF is a GTP-U tunnel, an envelope over UDP, exactly Sheet 08's letters-inside-letters. This is the most important idea in mobile networks: the phone's address stays fixed at the anchor; only the tunnel's far end moves. Mobility is a tunnel being re-pointed.

The radio side has been cut up too. The base station can be split into a "central unit" (slow, smart, can live in a data centre) and a "distributed unit" (fast, timing-critical, near the antenna). The O-RAN movement pushes for open interfaces between those pieces so one maker's radio can work with another's software. Many early 5G networks ran "non-standalone": 5G radios bolted onto a 4G core. Features like slicing and local edge anchors need the standalone 5G core.

Slices: many networks inside one network

Network slicing is Sheet 08's idea applied to a phone network: give each customer, or each kind of traffic, a network that acts like its own, with its own core functions, its own anchors and its own share of radio. A phone asks for a slice by name, and the name is short.

A promise per slice vs. one shared radio

Slice it hard

Set aside radio time and core capacity per slice, so a factory's robots get their delay promise no matter how many fans are filming a concert. The promise is real because the capacity is kept back.

Share it all

Kept-back capacity is idle capacity, and radio is the scarcest thing in the system. Shannon's limit doesn't care about labels: a slice can't promise more than the air near that tower can carry, and the core side is easy compared with the radio side.

It's Sheet 05's old fight between keeping back (IntServ) and sorting (DiffServ), now with a radio. Most real slices today are "sort and prioritise" plus separate core functions. Hard radio reservation is kept for the few customers who will pay for idle air.

Standards keep moving. 3GPP froze Release 19 in December 2025. Release 20 mixes "5G-Advanced" features with 6G studies, and the first real 6G specifications are expected in Release 21. Expect the radio to change more than the core: the planes and the anchored tunnel are here to stay.

Walking out of the room mid-sentence

Here is what goes wrong. On the Internet, your IP address says where you are (which network), and TCP uses that address to say who you are (which connection). Walk to a new network, get a new address, and every open connection breaks. Every mobility design is a way to pull those two meanings apart. There are three places to do it.

Keep a fixed home and forward the mail

The classic answer is Mobile IP. Your device keeps a "home address" that never changes. A "home agent" on your home network gets your mail. When you move, you tell it your new temporary address (the "care-of address") with a "binding update," and it forwards everything to you in a tunnel. In Mobile IPv4 your replies go straight out, but traffic to you always goes via home, a "triangle." Mobile IPv6 (RFC 6275) adds "route optimisation," which lets the other end learn your care-of address and talk to you directly once it can check you are who you say.

Mobile IP on laptops never really caught on: every host needed new code, and the security for route optimisation was hard. What won was the same idea moved into the network. Proxy Mobile IPv6 (RFC 5213) has the network's own gateways send the binding updates for the device, so the device does nothing. A 5G core is this idea at huge scale: the UPF anchor is the home agent, the GTP-U tunnel is the forwarding, and the device never knows it moved.

Handing a phone to the next tower

Inside one network, moving to the next base station is a handover. The phone measures nearby towers and reports. The current tower decides and prepares the next one over a direct link between them (Xn). It tells the phone to switch, and forwards any packets still in its queue to the new tower. Then the SMF tells the UPF to re-point the tunnel. One detail shows how much care goes into order. After switching, the UPF sends an "end marker" down the old tunnel as its very last packet. The new tower delivers the forwarded packets first, sees the end marker, and only then starts on packets from the new tunnel. Without it, a fast new path would deliver packets ahead of slower forwarded ones, and TCP would see reordering and think there was loss (Sheet 04).

Wi-Fi moves the device-first way, and it shows. The device decides when to leave, and a full re-login with a company's 802.1X security can take half a second to over a second, long enough to drop a voice call. Three add-ons help. With 802.11k, the access point gives a list of neighbours ("where to go"). With 802.11v, it suggests a move ("when to go"), though the device can say no. With 802.11r, "fast transition," the security keys are worked out ahead of time so the move skips the full login. Vendors quote under 50 ms. All three need support on both sides, and turning on 802.11r can lock out some old devices that don't understand it.

Or let the two ends handle it

The third place is the end-to-end answer from Sheet 01: stop using the address as the connection's name. QUIC names a connection by a connection ID, so a phone can move from Wi-Fi to cellular and keep going after checking the new path. MPTCP uses both at once, and Apple shipped it for Siri in 2013 so a request survives walking out of Wi-Fi range (Sheet 04). No anchor, no tunnel, no network help. But only apps using those transports get the benefit, and the other end has to play along.

A fixed anchor vs. a short path

Anchor far away

One central anchor means your address never changes, however far you move. Sessions survive, billing and lawful rules sit in one place, and moving is just re-pointing a tunnel.

Anchor close by

Every packet goes to the anchor and back. If you're in one city and your anchor is 800 km away, a game server in your own city costs you 1600 km of fibre each way. A close anchor means changing it, and your address, when you move far.

5G lets you pick per session: a far, stable anchor for the ordinary Internet, and close anchors that may change for traffic that cares about delay. That choice is what makes the next section possible.

Putting the food near the guests

Some applications care about the time to the server more than about bandwidth: cloud games, AR glasses, machine control, the voice and video calls of Sheet 05. You can't make light faster. In fibre it covers about 200 km per millisecond, so 1 ms of round trip is 100 km of distance. The only thing left to change is where the server stands. Edge computing puts servers in or near the access network: in a city data centre, at a big tower site, at the factory.

Worked numbers · the distance budget
Light in fibre
≈ 5 µs per km one way → 10 µs per km of round trip

Moving the server from 1500 km to 50 km buys about 14 ms, which matters a lot inside a 20 ms budget and not at all inside a 200 ms one. But if the radio hop is already 15 ms because the cell is busy, the edge can't save you. 3GPP's own estimate is that edge cuts delay by a factor of 2 to 10, "depending on assumptions," and the radio is the biggest of those assumptions.

A server near the user is useless if the user's packets still go to a far anchor first. This trip out and back is called "tromboning." So edge in a phone network needs local breakout: let chosen traffic leave the mobile network close by. 5G has two tools. A local anchor (a UPF at the edge) gives a session a nearby exit. An "uplink classifier" is a UPF inserted on the path that looks at each packet and sends only the matching flows, say "anything to the game's edge address," out the local door. Everything else goes on to the far anchor. The ETSI MEC standards describe the edge host and its APIs: tell the app which edge site serves this user, tell it when the user moves.

Edge brings Sheet 08's cloud problems back with less room to solve them. An edge site is small, so it can't hold every app's every server. App state has to follow the user, or the user has to cross back to where their state lives. And instead of a few big regions you now run hundreds of tiny ones, each a place that can fail. The edge that works best is usually metro edge (one site per city), not one at every tower. It gets most of the distance gain at a fraction of the cost.

The sensor in the garden

Now turn every design rule around. The sensor sends a few bytes, a few times a day, from a basement or a field kilometres away, and it must last years on a battery. For it, the most costly thing is not bits. It is time with the radio on, listening included. So these "low-power wide-area networks" (LPWAN) trade nearly everything for range and sleep.

  • LoRaWAN (unlicensed bands, anyone can run a gateway): a chirping signal that can be heard far below the noise. Each step of its "spreading factor," from SF7 up to SF12, roughly doubles the time on air in exchange for more range. In Europe's 868 MHz band, a device may be on the air only 1% of the time. A gateway is bound by the same rule, so answers and acknowledgements going down are scarce.
  • NB-IoT and LTE-M (3GPP Release 13, 2016, on operators' licensed spectrum): stripped-down cellular for things. Both aim at a "maximum coupling loss" of about 164 dB, roughly 20 dB more than an ordinary phone can manage, enough to reach a meter in a basement. "PSM" lets a device sleep for days while staying registered. "eDRX" lets it wake to check for messages only every few minutes or hours.
Worked numbers · the 1% rule
Duty cycle 1%
36 seconds on air per hour, at most
After a send of time T
stay quiet for T × (1/0.01 − 1) = 99 × T
Small message at SF7 (~0.1 s on air)
wait ~10 s → up to ~360 messages per hour
Same message at SF12 (~30× longer, ~1.3 s)
wait ~2 minutes → ~27 messages per hour, with payloads capped near 51 bytes

The devices that most need range pay the most for it, in air time and in battery. A gateway answering 1,000 devices is bound by the same 1%, so design for one-way, unacknowledged reports, and use downlink sparingly.

Read "10-year battery life" as a best case. Operator and vendor material quotes 10 to 15 years for NB-IoT and LTE-M with PSM and eDRX. Careful models at the full 164 dB edge of coverage give a few years for a 50-byte report every two hours, and decades in good coverage. The battery life is set by the worst-placed device, its message rate, and how often the network makes it wake up.

Above the radio, the IETF built a small Internet to fit. IEEE 802.15.4 radios (used by Thread and Zigbee) carry frames of at most 127 bytes, while IPv6 demands that 1280-byte packets get through. 6LoWPAN (RFC 4944, with RFC 6282's compression) squeezes IPv6 headers from 40 bytes down to a few by leaving out what both sides already know, and splits big packets into pieces. SCHC (RFC 8724) goes further for LPWANs: both ends agree ahead of time on a "rule" that describes the whole header, so often only a rule number crosses the air. On top, CoAP (RFC 7252) is a small web for things: GET and PUT on resources like HTTP, but over UDP with a 4-byte fixed header, and its own simple "confirmable" messages to resend when it matters. It is the end-to-end argument in its smallest form: keep the network dumb, and let the ends decide which messages deserve a resend.

Shouting down from a moving balloon

For decades, satellite Internet meant geostationary satellites: 35,786 km up, so they hang still over one spot and one dish can point at one forever. The cost is distance. Up and down once is about 240 ms at the speed of light. A request and its answer cross it twice, so close to half a second of round trip before anything else, enough to break interactive apps and to cripple TCP's early speed-up.

Low-earth-orbit networks like Starlink put thousands of satellites around 550 km up. Up and down is then about 1,100 km, under 4 ms. Starlink reports a US median peak-hour delay of about 26 ms (June 2025). The price is that every satellite crosses your sky in minutes. So the network is always handing over, and its topology is a moving map. Laser links between satellites can carry traffic where there is no ground station below, at the cost of a longer path. 3GPP's Release 17 added "non-terrestrial networks" to 5G, so phones and IoT devices can cope with long, changing delays and the frequency shift of a fast-moving satellite.

Worked numbers · height is delay
GEO, 35,786 km
up + down ≈ 71,600 km ÷ 300,000 km/s ≈ 239 ms one way; ≈ 480 ms round trip
LEO, ~550 km, straight overhead
up + down ≈ 1,100 km ≈ 3.7 ms one way; ≈ 7.3 ms round trip
Window of one TCP connection at 50 Mb/s
GEO: 50 Mb/s × 0.48 s ≈ 3 MB in flight; LEO at 30 ms: ≈ 190 KB

Light in a vacuum is about 50% faster than in glass, so over long distances laser links in orbit can even beat ground fibre. But the path changes every few minutes, and so does the delay.

In production · the 15-second heartbeat

Starlink's synchronised reconfiguration, measured 2023–2024

Users and researchers saw delay spikes and dips in throughput on Starlink that came back like clockwork. The natural guess was "that's a satellite handover." Mohan and colleagues (The Web Conference 2024) combined crowd-sourced speed tests with controlled dishes in two countries, and found the spikes were lined up at 15-second intervals across the whole network, in step everywhere at once, not tied to when a given satellite left a given sky. The best explanation is a central scheduler that re-plans which dish talks to which satellite every 15 seconds. Each re-plan briefly disturbs traffic. Later work treats these re-plans and local handovers as separate, predictable sources of delay and loss.

A moving network has a rhythm, and the rhythm is the control plane's, not physics'. An app or congestion controller that knows the beat can ride it. One that doesn't will read a scheduled hiccup as congestion and back off. It's Sheet 07's lesson in orbit: a central controller makes the network simple to reason about, and makes its own timing everyone's timing.

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01 · capacity calculation

An access point serves four laptops at 300 Mb/s link rate and one old scanner stuck at 6 Mb/s, all sending 1500-byte frames, with 0.1 ms of fixed cost per frame. (a) Under equal turns, what does each device get? (b) Under equal airtime, what does each laptop get? (c) Roughly how much total throughput does airtime fairness win?

Frame times: laptop 12,000 ÷ 300 + 100 µs = 140 µs; scanner 12,000 ÷ 6 + 100 µs = 2,100 µs. (a) One round = 4 × 140 + 2,100 = 2,660 µs, so each device gets 12,000 bits / 2,660 µs ≈ 4.5 Mb/s. Total ≈ 22.6 Mb/s. (b) Each device gets 1/5 of the air: a laptop gets (12,000 / 140 µs) ÷ 5 ≈ 17.1 Mb/s, and the scanner ≈ 1.1 Mb/s. (c) Total ≈ 4 × 17.1 + 1.1 ≈ 69.7 Mb/s, about 3× more. The scanner drops from 4.5 to 1.1 Mb/s, which for a scanner is usually fine.

Check yourself — 02 · system design

An operator puts a GPU edge site in each city for cloud gaming, but players see no change in delay. Their sessions are anchored at a UPF in a national data centre 900 km away. List what has to change, in order, and what new problem each change brings.

(1) Local breakout: an uplink classifier UPF (or a local PDU session anchor) in each city sends game traffic out to the edge site, saving about 18 ms of fibre round trip (2 × 900 km × 10 µs). New problem: the classifier needs current traffic filters for the game's addresses, and a move across cities may change the anchor, and so the address. (2) Make the game able to find and move to the right edge site (MEC-style APIs, or DNS that answers by location). New problem: game state must move with the player, or the player must stay pinned. (3) Check the radio: with busy cells and resends, the radio hop may be bigger than the fibre saved. Measure delay at the edge site per cell before promising anything. (4) Decide which slice or priority the game traffic rides on the radio.

Check yourself — 03 · protocol analysis

A team uses LoRaWAN in Europe at SF12 for parking sensors and asks each sensor to send a confirmed (acknowledged) message every 2 minutes, through 1 gateway serving 2,000 sensors. Why will this fail, and what would you change?

At SF12 a small message takes about 1.3 s on air, so a 1% duty cycle forces about 2 minutes of silence after it. The sensor is right at its limit with nothing to spare for resends. Worse, the gateway must send 2,000 acknowledgements every 2 minutes under its own 1% rule: 1,000 per minute × ~1.3 s each is far more than 0.6 s per minute of allowed air. It is also deaf while it sends. Changes: send only when a spot changes state, not on a timer; use unconfirmed uplinks with an occasional heartbeat; let the network use faster spreading factors for sensors close to gateways (adaptive data rate); add gateways so sensors can use faster rates; or move to NB-IoT/LTE-M, where licensed spectrum has no 1% rule.

In a 5G Xn handover, why does the UPF send an "end marker" down the old tunnel?

You give a Wi-Fi link twice the transmit power. What happens to the Shannon limit, roughly, at high SNR?

Share the room by time, not by turns. Fight for width, not loudness. Keep each person's name fixed at an anchor, and move only the tunnel. Put the food where the guests are, and let the sensor in the garden sleep. Do all that, and a network made of air, moving people and tiny batteries can feel almost as steady as a wire.

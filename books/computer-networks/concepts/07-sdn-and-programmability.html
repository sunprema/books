Taking the Thinking Out of Every Box and Putting It in One Place

Network people call this: software-defined networking & programmability

For forty years every box on the network did two jobs: it worked out where things should go, and it sent them there. This sheet is about pulling those two jobs apart: one room full of computers does the thinking for many boxes, and the boxes just do what they're told, very fast. It sounds like a product you buy. It is really a change in who decides, what they can see, and what happens when the room that decides goes quiet.

Picture a big town where every crossing has its own traffic helper. Each helper watches only their own crossing. They chat with the helpers at the next crossings ("the bridge road is blocked, send people my way") and each one decides alone which way to wave the cars. It works. It even works when half the helpers fall asleep. But nobody in the town can see the whole picture, and if the mayor wants "keep the hospital road clear on Fridays," someone has to walk to four hundred crossings and explain it four hundred times, in each helper's own way of talking.

Now picture a second town. The helpers still stand at every crossing, but they don't decide anything. Each one has a card of simple rules, "cars going north: wave left," and a phone. In a map room at the town hall, planners can see every street at once. They work out the best plan for the whole town and phone each helper a new card. The mayor tells the map room what they want, once.

The second town can do clever things the first can't: fill every road just to the edge, clear one road on purpose, check a plan on paper before anyone moves. It also has new ways to fail. What if the phones go down? What if two helpers get their new cards ten seconds apart? What if the map room is wrong? This sheet is about the second town: why big networks moved there, the phone lines they use, and the new problems they bought.

Splitting the thinking from the doing

Sheet 01 split a network box's work into three "planes." The doing part, the forwarding (data) plane, moves each packet by the rules in its tables, billions of times a second. The thinking part, the control plane, decides what those rules should be. The looking-after part, the management plane, sets the box up, watches it and fixes it. In a normal router all three live inside one metal case, and the thinking is spread across every case in the network, each one talking routing with its neighbours (Sheet 03).

Software-defined networking (SDN) moves the thinking out of the case. A program running on ordinary servers, the "controller," holds the map of the whole network, works out the rules, and writes them into many boxes over a standard line. The boxes keep the doing. The IETF's word list for this (RFC 7426) names the lines between the layers: the line from the controller down to the boxes is the southbound interface, and the line from the controller up to the programs that ask for things is the northbound interface.

Two easy mistakes to avoid. First, SDN is not one wire format. The famous one, OpenFlow, is one southbound line among several; most big real networks mix it with others, and with plain old routing protocols. Second, "logically centralised" does not mean "one computer." It means the network acts as if one mind is deciding, even though that mind is copied across many servers for safety. Making those copies agree with each other, and with what the boxes really hold, is most of the hard work on this sheet.

Every helper decides vs. one map room decides

Spread-out thinking (OSPF, IS-IS, BGP in every box)

No single place can fail and take everything down; every box keeps working with only its neighbours. But each box sees only what its neighbours tell it, so the network finds a good answer, not the best one for everyone together, and a new idea means new software in every box from every maker.

Central thinking (a controller)

One program sees all the roads and all the demand, so it can plan the whole network at once and change behaviour with a software update in one place. But now the map room, and the phone lines to it, are things that can break, lag, or be wrong everywhere at the same moment.

Real networks pick a mix. Google's private network kept ordinary routing running underneath its central planner so it could switch the planner off and fall back. The question is never "central or not" but "what is central, and what still works when the centre goes quiet?"

The phone line down to the boxes

The first phone line: a rule card with fixed boxes to fill in

In 2008 a group of researchers wrote a short paper with a plain wish: let people try new network ideas on the real campus network without asking every switch maker for new software. Their answer, OpenFlow, gives a switch a "flow table." Each line in the table says: if a packet's header looks like this, do that, and count it. The "do that" was one of three things: send it out these ports; wrap it up and send it to the controller; or drop it. A remote controller adds and removes lines over a locked channel.

That bottom line, "ask the controller," is called reactive set-up: rules appear when traffic first shows up. It is lovely on a campus and terrible across a continent. Here is why, in numbers.

Distance turns "ask when you need to" into a tax on every new connection. So wide networks write the rules ahead of time (proactive set-up) and keep the controller out of the per-packet path entirely. The controller plans; it never sits between a packet and its way out.

OpenFlow's deeper limit was the card itself. The switch could only match on header fields the standard listed, and every new kind of header meant a new version of the standard and new chips. The list grew from 12 fields in version 1.0 (2009) to 41 in version 1.4 (2013). The people behind it looked at that growth and said: this will never end. Stop giving the switch a longer form to fill in. Tell the switch what the form is.

The second phone line: send the switch its own form

That idea became P4 (2014), and Sheet 02 showed what it runs on: chips built as a line of match-and-act stages whose tables can be shaped by a program. A P4 program says what headers exist, how to read them in order (a little "parser" state machine), which tables to look up, and what each table's actions do. The program is written once and turned, by a compiler, into whatever a particular chip or network card can do. Its three goals were: change it in the field, tie it to no header format, and tie it to no one chip.

So now there are two separate things to program, and it's worth keeping them apart in your head. SDN changes who fills in the tables. Programmable data planes change what tables exist. You can have either without the other: a fixed-chip switch run by a controller, or a P4 switch run by normal routing software.

The phone line to a P4 switch is P4Runtime, built on gRPC (a general way for programs to call each other over the network). It has a short list of calls. One sends the compiled program, the "pipeline," to the switch. One writes table lines; one reads them back. And one long-open stream carries packets the switch wants to send up to the controller, packets the controller wants to send out, and something you will need in §7.4: the vote for who is in charge.

Several controller copies can talk to one switch at once, but only one may write. Each copy sends a number, its "election ID," and the switch obeys the highest. When a new leader takes over, it sends a higher number, and the old one's writes are refused even if it hasn't noticed it's been replaced. That one rule stops two map rooms from scribbling on the same card.

The third and fourth phone lines: setting up and watching

Forwarding rules are only half of a box. The other half is set-up: port speeds, addresses, which routing sessions to run, who may log in. For decades that meant a person typing commands, each maker's commands different. The modern way is to describe the set-up as structured data, using a shared language for the shape of that data called YANG (RFC 7950). A YANG model says "an interface has a name, a speed, a list of addresses…", so a program can build, check and compare set-ups instead of pasting text. The OpenConfig group publishes YANG models that many makers support, so one program can set up many makers' boxes.

Two protocols carry that data. NETCONF (RFC 6241) is the careful one. It keeps the running set-up apart from a draft copy (the "candidate"), lets you lock it, check it, and commit it all at once, and has a safety net worth learning by heart:

1 edit-config   target=candidate      — write changes into the draft, not the live box
2 validate                          — ask the box: is this draft even legal?
3 commit confirmed timeout=120      — go live, but only on trial for 120 s
4 …can I still reach the box? are routes up?…
5 commit                            — yes: keep it. no reply in time: roll back

Line 3 is the whole trick. If the change cuts off the very line you're using to reach the box, you can't send line 5, the timer runs out, and the box puts itself back how it was. (The standard's default is 600 seconds; dropping the session rolls it back too.) Anyone who has ever locked themselves out of a router 300 km away knows why this exists. There is also RESTCONF (RFC 8040), the same idea over plain web requests.

gNMI is the fast one, from the OpenConfig group, built on gRPC like P4Runtime. It has just four calls: what can you do (Capabilities), get, set, and subscribe. Subscribe is why people love it. Instead of a program asking every box every five minutes "how full is port 7?", it says once "tell me" and the box streams the answer, either on a timer (every 10 s, say) or only when it changes ("tell me the instant port 7 goes down").

The line going up: saying what you want, not how

On the northbound side, the dream is that people say what they want, "the payroll servers may talk only to the bank, over a locked line, with less than 50 ms delay," and the system works out the rules. This is sold as intent-based networking. Be careful: the phrase is used for almost anything. The research group that wrote it down (RFC 9315, 2022) gives a useful test. A real intent system does two jobs. It fulfils: takes the wish in, turns it into set-up, and pushes it out. And it assures: keeps watching the live network, checks the wish is still true, and acts when it isn't. A tool that does only the first is a template engine with a nice name.

Where to put the map room, and what if it burns

Back in the second town. You get to build the map room. Where? If it sits at one edge of town, far crossings wait a long time on the phone. Build several, and they must agree with each other. This is the controller placement problem. A 2012 study put it as two puzzles: put k map rooms where the average phone delay is smallest, or where the worst one is smallest. Across more than a hundred real wide-area maps they found that, for delay alone, one well-placed room is often enough for smaller networks, a few are enough for most, and each extra room helps less than the last. After that, what decides the count is not delay at all. It is what happens when a room fails.

Copies that agree

So the map room is copied, and the copies elect one leader. They must agree on who that is, even when the phone lines between them break. The usual way is a majority vote (algorithms called Paxos and Raft do this). Google's private network, B4, ran a small group of controller copies at each site that used Paxos to pick a leader, with the rest standing by hot. The leader election in P4Runtime (§7.2) is how the switch learns the result.

Odd numbers, small numbers, and spread out, but not so far apart that the vote itself is slow. Put all five copies in one building and one power cut takes all of them; put them on three continents and every decision waits ~100 ms for the vote.

When the phones go dead

The most important design choice in an SDN network is what a box does when it can't hear the controller. The good answer has a name: fail static. Keep forwarding with the last rules you were given. Yesterday's plan is far better than no plan. A controller that can't see a switch is not the same thing as a switch that can't forward. But "keep the last rules" raises the next question: for how long? Routes learned from outside age out, links change, and a box frozen on an old plan slowly drifts from the truth.

In production · the map room switched itself off

Google Cloud, 2 June 2019

An automatic maintenance tool was told to stop some jobs in one area for upkeep. Two set-up mistakes and a software bug meant the network's controller jobs in several places were on its list, and the tool stopped all of their copies at once. The network did what it was built to do: it kept forwarding on its last rules for several minutes. Then, with no control plane to refresh them, capacity fell away, and services in several US regions saw heavy packet loss for between 3 hours 19 minutes and 4 hours 25 minutes. Getting back was slow because the controllers' own set-up data had been lost with them and had to be rebuilt and sent out again.

Every copy of the map room shared one fate: the same tool, the same list, the same moment (Sheet 01's fate sharing, again). Google's fixes went straight at that: keep the controllers' set-up stored so it never has to be rebuilt, and let the network fail static for longer. Fail static buys you minutes, not hours. Plan what happens when the minutes run out.

Changing many boxes without tripping over yourself

Here's the problem most people don't see coming. You have an old plan that's correct and a new plan that's correct. You send the new rules out. For a short time, some boxes have the new rule and some still have the old. A mix of two correct plans can be a broken plan.

Take three boxes. Packets come in at A and leave at E. Old plan: A sends to B, B sends to E. You want to take the B-to-E wire away for repair, so the new plan is: A sends straight to E, and anything at B goes back to A. Both plans deliver every packet. Now suppose B gets its new card before A. A still sends to B; B now sends back to A; A sends to B… The packets go round and round until A's card arrives or they run out of steps (most packets carry a hop limit, often 64) and die. You built a loop out of two plans with no loops in them.

The fix that works for any pair of plans came from a 2012 paper and is called a two-phase update. Each packet gets a small label saying which plan it belongs to. Phase one: add the new plan's rules everywhere, matching only packets labelled "new," and leave the old rules alone. Nothing changes yet, because no packet is labelled "new." Phase two: once every box has confirmed, tell the entry boxes to start labelling packets "new." Each packet now travels wholly under one plan or wholly under the other, never a mix. That promise is called "per-packet consistency." Later, when no old-labelled packets are left, remove the old rules. Try both ways in the figure below.

The price is space and time: for a while every box holds two copies of its rules, and table space on fast chips is small and dear (Sheet 02's TCAM). So real systems mix tricks: two-phase where it's needed, careful ordering where it's enough (in our example, "always update A before B" works fine), and, for links that could overflow mid-change, the idea from Microsoft's SWAN: keep a little spare room on every link so that no order of updates can pour too much onto one wire.

Checking the plan before anyone moves

Software brings a gift and a threat, and they are the same thing. One program can now change a thousand boxes in a minute. It can also break a thousand boxes in a minute. When a person typed commands, mistakes spread at typing speed. Automation removes that brake. So the network has to grow a new brake: check the change before it goes out.

Once the plan is data, you can check it like data. Three ideas show the range.

  • Treat the header as just a row of bits (Header Space Analysis, 2012). Each box becomes a function: "bits like this come in on this port, bits like that go out on that one." Push "every possible header" through the whole network on paper and you can answer, for all packets at once: can anything loop? Can the guest network ever reach the payroll servers? It doesn't care which protocol the bits mean.
  • Check each rule as it goes in (VeriFlow, 2013). Sit between the controller and the boxes; for every rule added, changed or removed, check in real time that the network-wide promises ("no loops," "no black holes") still hold, and stop the rule if they don't.
  • Work out the tables from the set-up files (Batfish, 2015). Most networks still run ordinary routing, so the tables aren't written by anyone; they come out of the configs. Batfish reads the config files, runs the routing protocols on paper, works out the tables they would produce, and checks those. Change one line of config, and see what it would do to reachability before you push it.

A checker proves the plan matches the promises you wrote down. It says nothing about promises you forgot to write, or about a box whose chip doesn't do what its rules say. Pair checking-before with watching-after: §7.6's streamed measurements tell you whether the real network still matches the checked plan.

Where the map room pays for itself

Filling the long wires to the brim

Long-distance links between data centres cost a fortune, and normal practice was to run them at about 30–40% full on average. That leaves room for a failure to push traffic onto other links. But it means paying for two or three times the wire you use. Spread-out routing can't do much better, because no box knows the whole demand.

In production · a central planner on a private WAN

Google B4, described 2013

Google built its data-centre-to-data-centre network from switches it controlled with OpenFlow, with a central traffic planner. The planner knew how much each service wanted and how important it was, split traffic across many paths, and filled links close to full. Many links ran near 100%, and all links averaged about 70% over long periods: two to three times better than normal practice. Two design choices made it safe. Ordinary routing (BGP and IS-IS) kept running underneath. And when the planner misbehaved, operators could switch it off and fall back to plain shortest-path forwarding. The team called that fall-back "invaluable."

The planner worked because the traffic was Google's own: it could be told to slow down, and low-priority copying could wait. That's a very different deal from a public network where you control none of the senders. Same year, same idea: Microsoft's SWAN reported carrying about 60% more traffic than before by centrally steering how much each service sent.

Running the data-centre fabric

Inside a data centre the network is a huge, regular grid of switches (Sheet 08 builds the pretend networks that ride on it). Here the map room's gift is control over a few thousand switches that all look alike. Google's second-generation controller, Orion (described 2021), runs its data-centre fabrics, its campus and B4. It is built as many small programs that never talk to each other directly. Instead they all read and write one shared board, a "network information base." The programs above write what they want; the programs below keep comparing that with what the switches actually report, and fixing the difference, again and again. The paper reports up to 40 times faster recovery and up to 1.16 million network updates per second.

That "keep comparing want with is" loop is the quiet big idea. A controller that fires off commands and assumes they worked will slowly drift from reality. A controller that keeps checking and repairing heals from a lost message, a rebooted switch, or its own restart in the same way: it just runs the loop again.

Running the network on what it tells you

The last use closes the circle. If the boxes stream their state to you (gNMI subscriptions, §7.2), a program can act on it: a link's error count climbs, the program drains traffic off it before it fails, and opens a repair ticket. Programmable chips go further with in-band network telemetry (INT): each switch on the path writes a little note into the packet itself, saying how long the packet waited there and how full the queue was, and the last switch strips the notes off and reports them. Instead of averages every ten seconds, you get the story of a single packet's trip, hop by hop. Sheet 10 picks up what to do with all those notes.

Flexibility vs. knowing what it will do

Program everything

New headers, new tables, new rules from any app, changed in seconds. You can fix a problem the day you find it, and try ideas nobody's chip maker planned for.

Keep it fixed and boring

A network whose behaviour comes from well-tested protocols and slow, reviewed changes is easier to predict. Every new program, table and API is a new place for a bug to hide.

The networks that made SDN work paired the flexibility with brakes: a fall-back to plain routing, fail-static boxes, checking before pushing, staged roll-outs, and a loop that keeps comparing the plan with the real network. Flexibility without those brakes is just a faster way to break things.

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01 · system design

You run a WAN across three continents. Your controller lives in one data centre in Europe. A team proposes reactive set-up: switches send each new flow's first packet to the controller. Give two reasons to say no, and what to do instead.

(1) Delay: from Asia or the US west coast the controller is ~8,000–10,000 km away, ≈ 40–50 ms one way, so every new flow waits ≈ 100 ms or more per switch that has to ask. (2) Fate: if the line to Europe breaks, new flows everywhere stop starting, and a flood of new flows (or an attack) becomes a flood of controller requests. Instead: compute paths proactively and pre-install them; keep routing (or a default path) running underneath for fail-static; place controller copies in each region, with an odd count, elected by majority vote.

Check yourself — 02 · protocol analysis

Two controller copies both believe they are leader after a network split. Copy X sent election ID 7 to switch S an hour ago; copy Y, elected after the split, sends ID 8. Then X tries to write a table line to S. What happens, and why is that the right answer?

S treats the highest election ID it has seen (8, from Y) as primary, so X's write is refused, even though X still thinks it's in charge. That's right because the switch can't know which copy has the true picture; a single rising number gives one simple, local rule that stops two leaders from interleaving writes. It only works if the vote that produced 8 was a real majority, so the election numbers must come from the controllers' agreement protocol, never be made up locally.

Check yourself — 03 · configuration review

An engineer pushes a NETCONF change to a remote edge router that rewrites its management access list, using a plain commit. The change has a typo that blocks their own address. What should the procedure have been?

Edit the candidate, validate, then commit confirmed with a short timeout (say 120 s). Then, from a fresh session, check the router is still reachable and routes are up, and only then send the confirming commit. With the typo, the confirming commit can never be sent, so the router rolls itself back when the timer runs out. Better still: run the new access list through an offline checker first (Batfish-style) and roll it to one router before the rest.

In §7.4's example, which change order is safe without any version tags?

A vendor says their product is "intent-based" because you type a goal and it generates device configs. By RFC 9315's test, what's missing?

Take the thinking out of every box, put it in one well-guarded room that sees the whole town, give the boxes a clear rule card and a way to keep going when the phone goes quiet, and check every new plan before it moves a single car. Then a network stops being four hundred helpers you must persuade one by one and becomes one thing you can plan, test and change, carefully, in an afternoon.

Why the Network Is Built in Layers, and Who Gets to Be Clever

Network people call this: architectures & design principles

Every later sheet in this book is one of a few old choices playing out. Here are the choices, why they were made, and where they have started to crack.

You mail a thick letter to your grandmother: forty pages of family recipes. It passes through a dozen post offices, three trucks, and one plane. Each carrier handles it with care. Each one checks that the envelope is not torn. Every single step says "all good."

Grandma opens it, and page 17 is missing. You left it on the copy machine. No carrier could have caught that. They never looked inside, and they couldn't know what "all forty pages" meant.

This whole sheet happens inside that mail system: who reads which envelope, who has to remember what, who plans the routes, and who has to call whom to say "it came." The same letter goes all the way through.

Envelopes inside envelopes

The mail works because nobody needs to understand the whole job. You write the letter. The local office puts it in a bag marked for a city. The bag goes in a truck marked for a hub. Each worker reads only the outside of the thing they were handed and adds or removes only their own wrapper. Network people call this layering: each part promises a simple service to the part above it, and hides how it does the work from everyone else.

The promise between two layers is the useful part. Engineers call it a service interface. As long as a layer keeps its promise ("hand me a packet with an address and I will try to get it there"), it can change everything inside. The truck can become a train. Copper can become glass, then air. Nothing above has to know.

Lay the Internet's wrappers out by how many kinds sit at each level and you get an hourglass. Many kinds of programs at the top. Many kinds of wire and radio at the bottom. And in the narrow middle there is basically one thing everybody agrees on: the Internet Protocol, IP, the plain "best try, no promises" delivery of addressed packets. The old summary of Internet thinking puts it this way: the goal is connectivity, the tool is IP, and the cleverness lives at the ends, not hidden in the network.

Here is a strange fact about that waist. When researchers built a model where made-up protocols compete and die out over many rounds, the hourglass shape grew back almost no matter how they started it. The protocols that ended up in the waist were the old ones that lots of other things already leaned on, not the best-made ones. The waist is narrow because it is hard to replace, not because it is perfect. Keep that in mind; it comes back at the end of this sheet.

Where the envelopes leak

Real layers peek at each other all the time. A few you will meet again in this book:

  • TCP's check sum covers part of the IP wrapper (the addresses), so the "upper" envelope depends on what is written on the "lower" one.
  • Address-changing boxes in the middle rewrite the outer envelope and reach inside to fix port numbers, so they must understand layers they were never part of.
  • Finding the biggest packet a path can carry means the sender has to learn about the smallest pipe somewhere in the middle — a lower-layer fact leaking up.
  • On radio links, TCP reads lost packets as "the road is crowded" when the real cause is noise, so designers are tempted to let the radio layer tell TCP what really happened.

That last kind of fix is called cross-layer design: letting one layer read or steer another's private state. It can win real speed. It also has a cost that people in wireless learned the hard way: two layers that each adjust to the other make a loop, and loops can swing out of control in ways nobody planned. Do enough of it and the clean stack becomes a bowl of noodles that nobody can change without breaking something far away.

Clean layers vs. reaching across

Keep the walls

Each part can be swapped, tested and reasoned about alone. New wires and new programs show up without asking anyone.

Reach across

The radio can tell TCP "that was noise, not crowding." Fewer bytes, faster recovery, better use of a scarce link.

A good rule: reach across only through a small, written-down hint (a bit, a signal) both sides agree on — never by reading the other layer's insides. ECN, later in this book, is exactly that kind of hint.

An Internet guidelines paper from 2002 even has a section titled "Layering Considered Harmful." Its point is narrower than the title: every extra layer you stack (IP over a circuit layer over another circuit layer) adds its own tuning, its own features, and its own ways to fail, and the layers start fighting over the same job.

Only the two ends can be sure

Back to the missing page 17. The carriers checked every hop, and the letter was still wrong, because the mistake happened where they couldn't see. A 1984 paper turned this into a rule now called the end-to-end argument: if a job can only be done fully and correctly by the two ends — because only they know what "correct" means — then the network in the middle cannot do it for them. At best, the middle can help it go faster.

The paper's own example is copying a file carefully from one computer to another. The disk can go bad after the file is written. A copy program can have a bug while moving bytes through memory. A computer can crash halfway. Making every link perfect fixes none of these. What does fix them is one check at the very end: the receiver reads the file back, adds up a check number, sends it to the sender, and they compare. If the numbers differ, try again. The paper says that making every step extra careful ("do everything three times") costs too much for what it buys.

The end-to-end argument does not say "never help in the middle." It says help in the middle is a speed-up, never a replacement for the check at the ends. People misquote this half constantly.

So when is the speed-up worth it? When the middle is bad enough that a single end-to-end retry is wasteful. If one hop loses lots of packets, every loss there throws away the whole trip so far. Try it:

On clean wires, retrying in the middle buys almost nothing. On one noisy radio hop, it more than halves the work — which is why Wi-Fi resends locally while TCP still checks at the ends.

Keep the memory with the one who cares

Suppose every post office kept a notebook: "letter from you to Grandma, page 23 sent, waiting on page 24." Now one office burns down. Your letter is fine, but its memory is gone, and the trip breaks even though another road was open.

The Internet's designers chose the other way. The memory of a conversation — what was sent, what came back, what to send next — lives in the two computers talking, not in the boxes between them. They called this fate-sharing: it is fine to lose the memory about a thing if you only lose it when the thing itself is lost. If your computer dies, the conversation was over anyway. If a router dies, nothing important went with it, and packets just go another way.

That choice came straight from the list of goals the builders wrote down, in order. The order matters; it explains a lot of what feels odd today.

Fate-sharing has an evil twin: hidden shared fate. Two "separate" fiber paths dug into the same trench, two routers running the same buggy release, or every region taking the same config at once all fail together. Most big outages are this, not a single broken box.

Three kinds of workers, working at three speeds

Look inside a sorting office and you see three very different jobs. The sorters grab each letter and drop it in a slot in a fraction of a second. In a back room, planners work out which slot means which city, and redo it when a road closes. Upstairs, managers hire, set rules, count, and fix things when the numbers look wrong.

Routers and switches are split the same way, and each job is called a "plane." The data plane (also "forwarding plane") handles every packet against a table. The control plane decides what goes in that table — routing protocols talking to neighbors. The management plane watches, configures, and maintains the boxes. A newer list also names an operational plane for each box's own health (fans, ports, memory).

Why keep them apart? Speed, first. A data plane at 400 Gb/s gets about 31 ns for a full-size packet and under 2 ns for the smallest; it cannot stop to think. The control plane can take its time, but it must never be starved by a flood of packets — so routers rate-limit what goes up from the data plane to the control plane, and an attack that floods that path is an attack on the router's brain. Second, change: once the planes talk through a clear interface, you can move the control plane somewhere else entirely. That move is the heart of software-defined networking (Sheet 07). Note what that move is and isn't: splitting the planes is about the interface. A split-out control plane can still be spread over many machines.

Four good things you can't all max out

Every design in this book is pulling on four ropes at once: performance (fast, cheap per bit), scale (still works at ten times the size), reliability (keeps working when parts break), and simplicity (people can understand it and change it safely). Tighten one and another goes slack. A per-hop retry speeds a noisy link and adds memory to the middle. A backup path adds reliability and adds a state machine that only runs on the worst day of the year.

The 2002 guidelines paper argued that the fourth rope is the one people under-pull. It names three rules worth knowing by name:

  • Simplicity: complexity is the main thing that stops a network from growing well.
  • Amplification: very big systems do things small ones never do; in a big enough network, small things can and do cause huge events.
  • Coupling: the more the parts lean on each other, the further one failure spreads.

One old rule has quietly flipped. For decades, protocol builders were told "be careful in what you send and forgiving in what you accept." It made early meetings between different programs go smoothly. Over time it did damage: everyone's sloppy input got accepted, then relied on, and the protocol was stuck with the mess forever. A 2023 note from the Internet's architecture board says it plainly: tolerating strange input hurts in the long run, and the better plan is to keep tending a protocol — fix and reject, on purpose, together.

Forgiving vs. strict

Forgive

Things work today with buggy partners. Faster early growth.

Refuse

Bugs get fixed instead of copied. The protocol can still change in ten years.

Forgiveness is a loan. The bill is a protocol that can't move.

Who pays, and who fights

The mail is not run by one kind person. The sender wants it cheap and private. The carrier wants to charge more for fast service. The town wants to read some mail. Each of them will bend the system toward what they want, and they will never stop.

A well-known 2002 paper calls this the tussle, and says it is the normal state of the Internet, not a bug. Users, network owners, content companies and governments want different things, and those fights get fought inside the design whether the designer planned for them or not. Its advice: don't build in one winner. Leave room for different outcomes in different places, cut the design along the lines where people fight, so a fight over one thing (say, prices) doesn't spill into another (say, naming), and give people real choices. A design too stiff to bend in a fight gets broken instead.

Money explains many "technical" facts in this book. Network owners carry each other's traffic based on business deals, so routing between them is about policy first and shortest paths second (Sheet 03). The middle of the network is paid by people who don't see your data, so they want to see headers and add boxes; the ends want privacy and freedom, so they encrypt. Hold that pull in your head and the next case makes sense.

Case study: why the Internet grew the way it did

Turn 1 · design for many services

One protocol becomes two

TCP and IP started as a single protocol. It was split because some traffic — packet voice was the example — would rather lose a packet than wait for a resend. Splitting gave a bare datagram layer (IP) and let reliable delivery become one choice among several (TCP, UDP).

Keep the waist minimal, and put "extra" promises above it where they can be optional.

Turn 2 · October 1986

The congestion collapse, and a fix at the ends

Between two labs 400 yards apart, throughput fell from 32 kb/s to 40 b/s — about a thousand times worse. Senders kept resending into full queues, so the links carried mostly copies that would be thrown away. The fix (slow start, careful backing off, better timing) went into the hosts' TCP. Routers didn't change at all, and the fix spread by people updating their own machines.

The end-to-end shape let the Internet be fixed without asking the middle for permission. It also means the whole Internet relies on hosts choosing to behave.

Turn 3 · 1990s onward

The middle gets clever anyway

Addresses ran short, firms wanted firewalls, carriers wanted to speed up and shape traffic. So boxes appeared in the path that rewrite addresses and ports, inspect connections, and drop what they don't recognize. Each solved a real problem. Together they broke the "dumb middle" that the end-to-end shape assumed.

The tussle wins over the drawing. Owners will put function where they get paid to.

Turn 4 · measured in 2011

The waist hardens

Measurements across many paths found middle boxes that change TCP's starting sequence numbers, strip or keep unknown options, and re-cut data into different packet sizes. Any new TCP feature (multipath TCP was the big one) had to be designed to survive all of that. People call this ossification: the protocol has turned to bone, because boxes in the middle depend on its current look.

Whatever the middle can see, the middle will depend on. Anything it depends on, you can no longer change.

Turn 5 · the ends fight back

Encrypt what the middle shouldn't touch; split out what it should

QUIC runs on top of UDP and encrypts almost all of its transport header, so middle boxes can't learn to depend on it — the end-to-end argument, enforced with cryptography. The price is that operators lose a view they used for fixing problems. Meanwhile, inside networks the owners do control, the control plane was pulled out of each box behind a clean interface, so one program can plan routes for a whole data center or backbone.

Both moves are the same idea: decide on purpose which parts are allowed to be clever, and make the boundary hard to cross by accident.

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01

A storage team says: "Our data center links have strong error checks and resend locally, so our file copy tool doesn't need its own check sum." Use the end-to-end argument to answer them, and name two failures their link checks can't see.

Link checks only cover bytes while on the wire. They miss memory or copy bugs inside either host, bad disks before or after the copy, a buggy NIC offload, and a crash halfway through. Only an end check (hash the file at the source, hash it at the destination, compare) covers the whole trip. The link checks are a speed-up: they make the end check fail rarely.

Check yourself — 02

A path has 4 wired hops at 0.1% loss and one satellite hop at 20% loss. Roughly how many link sends does one packet cost with ends-only retry, versus resending only on the satellite hop? (Ignore the tiny wired loss.)

Put the satellite hop last (worst case for ends-only). Each try spends 4 wired sends, then 1 satellite send, and works 80% of the time: 5 ÷ 0.8 = 6.25 sends. With local resend on the satellite hop: 4 + 1 ÷ 0.8 = 5.25. If the satellite hop is first, ends-only costs (1 + 0.8 × 4) ÷ 0.8 = 5.25 — same as local. Where the bad hop sits changes how much is wasted; the end check is needed either way.

Check yourself — 03 · design review

A team wants its load balancers to keep a per-connection table in memory, with no sharing to a backup. List what breaks when one load balancer dies, which principle from this sheet it violates, and one way to restore it.

Every connection through that box breaks even though both ends are fine: state lived in the middle without sharing the ends' fate. Fixes: make the choice of backend a function of the packet's own fields (consistent hashing on the address/port five-tuple), so any balancer makes the same choice with no memory; or copy the state to peers; or let the endpoints reconnect cheaply (QUIC connection IDs).

Which statement best matches the original end-to-end argument?

Once you can ask "who has to be sure, who has to remember, and who gets to be clever," every box and protocol in the rest of this book stops being a list to memorize and becomes a choice you can judge.

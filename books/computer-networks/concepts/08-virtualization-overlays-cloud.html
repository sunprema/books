Many Pretend Networks Sharing One Real One

Network people call this: virtualization, overlays & cloud networking

A cloud is thousands of strangers renting the same wires, and every one of them wants a network that feels like their own: their own addresses, their own walls, their own rules. This sheet is about how you build those pretend networks on top of a real one. You put letters inside bigger letters, you keep a book of who lives where, and you put a small switch inside every server. Then it is about the bill: bytes, lost packets, and problems you can't see from inside.

Picture a tall office building with one mail room and one set of halls. Two hundred companies rent rooms on its floors. Each company wants to feel like it owns the place. It wants its own room numbers (two companies both want a "Room 10"), its own mail rules, and no stranger reading its letters.

The building can't give every company its own halls. So it plays a trick. When a letter leaves a company's room, a building worker puts it, unopened, inside a bigger building envelope. On the outside they write only the floor's mail desk and a small company number. The halls and the mail carts only ever read the outside. At the far desk, a worker checks the company number, opens the big envelope, and drops the letter into the right company's tray. The company never sees the trick. To them, Room 10 just got their letter.

This sheet is that building. The halls are the real network. The envelopes-in-envelopes are the pretend networks. And the hard questions are the building's: how does the worker know which desk Room 10 of company 37 sits behind? What stops company 37 from writing "company 52" on the outside? And what happens when the big envelope is just a little too big for the mail cart?

Cutting one wire into many: the old way

The first trick was cheaper than an envelope: a sticker. A VLAN (virtual LAN) adds a 4-byte tag to each Ethernet frame (IEEE 802.1Q), and switches keep frames with different tags apart, as if they ran on different wires. The tag's number field is 12 bits: 4096 values, minus two kept back, so 4094 pretend networks at most. Providers needed more, so 802.1ad ("Q-in-Q") puts a provider sticker outside the customer's sticker. That gives up to 4094 × 4094 pairs, but only if every switch in the path reads both.

Stickers have three problems in a big data centre, and every one of them pushed people toward envelopes:

  • 4094 is a small number. A cloud has hundreds of thousands of customers, and each one may want several networks.
  • The sticker only lives inside one Layer-2 area. To let a pretend network span a whole data centre, every switch must carry every VLAN, and you are back to one giant flat network with its floods of "who has this address?" and its loop-breaking tree that switches off spare paths.
  • The halls must know every company. Each switch needs to learn every customer's machine addresses. A big cloud has millions of them, and switch tables are small (Sheet 02).

What modern data centres want is the opposite: a plain routed "underlay" (the real halls, running IP and ECMP across many equal paths, Sheet 03) that knows nothing about customers, and an "overlay" (the pretend networks) made only at the edges, in the servers.

Letters inside letters

Wrapping one packet inside another is called encapsulation, or tunnelling. The thing at the edge that wraps and unwraps is the "tunnel end point": in VXLAN talk, a VTEP. In a cloud it is usually the small switch inside each server (§8.5). Three wrappers matter.

The thin envelope: GRE

GRE (RFC 2784) is the oldest and simplest. Its own header is 4 bytes: a few flag bits, a version, and "what kind of packet is inside." Put that behind a new IPv4 header and you have added 24 bytes. An extension (RFC 2890) adds an optional 4-byte "key" that people often use as a customer number. GRE is still everywhere in site-to-site links and router-to-router tunnels. Its weakness in a data centre is quiet but big: to the halls, every GRE packet between two servers looks the same, with no ports to hash on. So ECMP puts all of that tunnel's traffic on one path (§8.3 shows why that matters).

The envelope that won the data centre: VXLAN

VXLAN (RFC 7348, 2014) wraps a whole Ethernet frame, not just an IP packet, so the pretend network can act like a plain switched LAN. The frame goes inside an 8-byte VXLAN header, inside UDP, inside IP. The VXLAN header's main job is a 24-bit network number, the "VNI": about 16 million pretend networks instead of 4094. The UDP target port is 4789.

That source port deserves a second look. The standard asks the sending VTEP to work out the UDP source port from a hash of the inner packet's fields. The halls do ECMP by hashing the outer headers. So two different customer conversations between the same two servers get different source ports, and they spread over different paths. One conversation always gets the same port, so its packets stay in order on one path. A field that looks like plumbing is really there for load balancing.

One trap from the real world: Linux's VXLAN code is older than the official port number, so its default is 8472, not 4789. Two makers' VTEPs that don't agree on the port will each say "tunnel up" and never hear each other.

Big packets barely notice the envelope; small ones pay heavily. That is why data-centre underlays run jumbo frames (9000 or more bytes). With them, customers keep a full 1500 and the envelope fits in the spare room.

The envelope with a note pocket: Geneve

VXLAN's header has one useful field. Cloud builders kept wanting to carry more: which security group sent this, which step of a service chain it's on, a trace mark. Geneve (RFC 8926, 2020; UDP port 6081) keeps VXLAN's shape and size, with an 8-byte base header and a 24-bit network number, and adds a pocket of "options," little type-length-value notes in 4-byte steps. One bit per option, "critical," says: if you don't understand this note, drop the packet rather than guess.

The pocket is the point, and also the cost. A header whose length changes is harder for fixed chips to read at line rate (Sheet 02's parser problem). The IETF's own review (RFC 9638) notes that Geneve was chosen on the guess that it would mostly be read by software in servers. That is true in clouds, where the host switch does the wrapping. It is a fair worry where fixed hall switches must look inside.

Who knows where everyone lives?

Back in the building. The wrapping is the easy part. The real question is what to write on the outside. When company 37's Room 10 sends a letter to its Room 22, the worker must know which floor's desk Room 22 is behind today. Machines move (a virtual machine can be moved to another server while running), so the answer changes. Wrapping is the data plane. This lookup is the control plane, and the three ways to do it are the three eras of overlays.

Shout and remember

The first VXLAN standard has no control plane at all. When a VTEP doesn't know where an address lives, it sends the letter to every desk that serves that company, using an IP multicast group for each network number. It also notes the outside address of every letter it receives ("Room 22 of company 37 lives behind desk 10.0.4.7"). This "flood and learn" works, the way old Ethernet worked. But it needs multicast running in the halls, which many operators don't want. It floods every unknown address to every desk. And a newly moved machine stays lost until it speaks.

Tell everyone in advance

The fix the switch world settled on is EVPN: use BGP, the routing protocol from Sheet 03, to announce addresses instead of learning them from floods. When a machine appears behind a VTEP, that VTEP sends a BGP route that says, in effect, "MAC aa:bb:…, IP 192.168.5.22, network 37, reach it through me." Every other VTEP for network 37 installs it ahead of time. RFC 7432 defined EVPN for MPLS networks; RFC 8365 (2018) uses it as the control plane for VXLAN and other IP overlays. It adds things flood-and-learn never had, such as a machine plugged into two switches at once with no loops. Most data-centre switch fabrics today run "VXLAN with an EVPN control plane." Route reflectors (Sheet 03) keep the BGP sessions from growing as n².

Ask the building's own directory

Big clouds don't run BGP to every server. They run their own directory, a "mapping" service, and push its answers into each host switch. That is Sheet 07's map room, aimed at pretend networks. Google's Andromeda (NSDI 2018) shows the choices well. Pushing every mapping for every customer to every server costs too much when a big network has tens of thousands of machines. Asking the directory on every first packet is too slow. Andromeda's answer, "Hoverboard," is a middle road. By default, a host sends packets for addresses it doesn't have to a set of gateway servers that know everything. The control plane watches for conversations that turn out to be heavy and installs a direct host-to-host path just for those. Most conversations are small and short, so a few gateways carry the long tail cheaply. The paper reports that the control plane could set up connectivity for tens of thousands of machines in seconds.

Tell everyone everything vs. ask when you need it

Push every mapping ahead of time

Every packet finds its answer right there in the host, with no first-packet wait and no single place to ask. But the tables and the update traffic grow with (machines × places that might talk to them), and a machine move must reach everyone before packets go astray.

Look it up, or use a default gateway, on a miss

Tables stay small and changes go to one place. But the first packets take a longer road, the directory or gateway is now a thing that must scale and stay up, and a burst of new conversations becomes a burst of lookups.

It's the reactive-vs-proactive choice from Sheet 07 again. The working answer is to push what's cheap, send the rest by a default road, and promote what turns out to be busy.

The halls and the pretend networks, tangled together

People like to say the overlay and underlay are "independent." They are not, and most of the hard operating problems come from where they touch:

  • The halls only see envelopes. Underlay ECMP, queues and QoS markings act on the outer header. If the VTEP doesn't copy the inner packet's priority bits (DSCP, ECN, Sheet 05) to the outside, the halls can't treat a voice call differently from a backup.
  • Shared fate stays hidden. Two pretend links that look separate to a customer may ride the same real fibre. When one hall switch dies, hundreds of customers lose "independent" paths at once (fate sharing, Sheet 01).
  • Each side hides problems from the other. A customer's traceroute shows one hop where the halls had six; the underlay's monitors see healthy links while one customer's tunnel drops every big packet. You need tools that can look at both layers and tie them together.

The walls between companies

Here's the uncomfortable truth about the company number on the envelope: it's just a number, written in plain sight. VXLAN has no lock and no seal; its standard says so and tells you to trust or protect the underlay. If a customer could write their own envelopes, they could write "company 52" and drop letters into another company's trays. Isolation in a cloud comes from who is allowed to hold the pen.

So the isolation boundary sits in the host switch, below the customer's machine. The customer's machine never makes its own envelope. The host switch, run by the provider, takes each packet from a virtual machine and checks it came from that machine's real address (no faking). It applies the customer's firewall rules, which clouds call security groups, and only then wraps it with the network number it knows that machine belongs to. On the way in, it unwraps only for machines it hosts, on the network number they belong to. The halls themselves are a closed, provider-only network. When they cross anything the provider doesn't own, the envelopes ride inside encryption (IPsec or MACsec).

Wherever the pen leaks, the wall leaks. Three classic leaks: a bare-metal or container host where the customer controls the VTEP; a hall that accepts VXLAN packets from outside the building (block UDP 4789/6081 at the edge, or anyone on the Internet can post letters into your trays); and a bug in the host switch itself, which is why clouds treat it as security-critical code and are moving it off the host CPU entirely (§8.5).

Walls for packets are the easy half. Walls for performance are harder. Everyone shares the same real links, switch buffers, host CPUs and network cards. One company's burst can fill the queue another company's packets wait in, a "noisy neighbour." The tools are the ones from Sheet 05, used per tenant and per machine: rate limits at the host switch for each virtual network card, fair queuing between machines on a host, and enough spare room in the halls that the overlay rarely sees a full queue. It's no accident that Andromeda's paper puts "isolation" in its title next to "performance": in a shared host switch, one tenant's flood of tiny packets is also a flood of work for the code that serves everyone.

The switch that lives inside every server

In a cloud, the busiest "switch" isn't in a rack. It's the virtual switch in each server, sitting between the machines on that host and the real network card. It does the security checks, firewall rules, wrapping, load-balancer address rewriting and counting for every packet, and it does them in software, on the very CPUs customers are paying for. That cost drove three waves of design.

Wave one: think once, remember the answer

Open vSwitch (OVS, NSDI 2015) runs the full rule set, many OpenFlow tables deep, in a slow, flexible program. When a packet's answer is worked out, it puts a single flattened rule into a fast cache in the kernel. That rule is a "megaflow": wild about every field the decision didn't depend on, so one entry covers many conversations. A small exact-match cache sits in front of that. The surprise was how small the cache needs to be. Across a day of data from 1,000 servers, the median host had under 107 cache entries; the 99th percentile was about 7,000. The hard part is not lookup speed. It's knowing which cached answers go stale when a rule changes.

Wave two: move it onto the network card

Even a cached software switch spends CPU cores on every packet, and latency jitters when a host is busy. Microsoft built its host switch, VFP, with the same "work it out once, cache the result" design, then moved that fast path into an FPGA on a custom network card (AccelNet, NSDI 2018). The software keeps the thinking. The card runs the cached rules at line rate, and packets go straight to the virtual machine. Microsoft reported it on every new Azure server since late 2015, over a million hosts, with VM-to-VM TCP latency under 15 µs. Other clouds took the same road with their own cards (Sheet 02's SmartNICs and DPUs). The prize is twice over: the cores go back to customers, and the security-critical switch runs where the customer's machine can't reach it.

Wave three: small safe programs in the kernel's packet path

On ordinary Linux hosts, and above all in container platforms, the newer tool is eBPF: small programs, checked for safety by the kernel before they load, that run at hooks in the packet path. The earliest hook, XDP, runs in the network card driver before the kernel has even built its usual packet record. Kubernetes shows why this matters. Its classic service load balancer, kube-proxy in iptables mode, turns every service into chains of rules that a packet walks through in order, so the cost grows with the number of services. Cilium replaces those chains with eBPF programs that look the service up in a hash table, the same cost for ten services or ten thousand.

Line 2 says "anything you don't know where to send, copy to the server at 10.0.0.2": flood-and-learn without multicast, one far desk at a time. Line 3 is the line people forget. It shrinks the pretend network's MTU by the 50-byte envelope so packets never outgrow the real wire.

The boxes that became programs

The same move happened to the middle boxes. In October 2012 a group of phone and network companies published a white paper calling for network functions virtualisation (NFV). Their idea: firewalls, load balancers, carrier gateways and NAT boxes should be software on ordinary servers, not special metal, so they can be started, scaled and moved like any program. It led to ETSI's NFV group, and the paper says plainly that NFV and SDN help each other but neither needs the other. Running traffic through an ordered list of these functions, say firewall then intrusion checker then load balancer, is service chaining. The steering can come from route tables, from a controller, or from a header made for it (NSH, RFC 8300) that carries "which chain, which step" with the packet.

In production · the load balancer that is just servers

Google Maglev, described 2016

Google's network load balancer is not a box; it is software on ordinary Linux servers, serving Google since 2008 and behind its cloud load balancing. Routers spread incoming packets across a group of Maglev machines with ECMP, so adding capacity means adding servers. Each Maglev machine then picks a backend by consistent hashing on the connection, and remembers its choice in a connection table. The hash is what makes it robust. Any Maglev machine picks the same backend for the same connection, so when routers move a flow to a different Maglev, it still lands where it was. When a backend is added or removed, only a small share of connections move. One machine could fill a 10 Gb/s link with small packets.

The pattern is everywhere in clouds now. A "managed" load balancer, NAT gateway or private endpoint is usually a fleet of plain servers behind ECMP, using hashing so that no single one is special. The hard part is keeping connections alive while the fleet changes underneath them.

The cloud's building plan

Everything so far, put together and sold, is what a cloud calls a virtual private cloud (VPC; Google and Microsoft have their own names, but the shape is the same). You pick an address range. You cut it into subnets, each tied to one zone. You write route tables. You attach security rules. Underneath, it's an overlay with a mapping service and a host switch on every server, and you never see an envelope. The parts you do see are the doors.

Two lessons cost people the most. First, address ranges are forever. Every company starts with 10.0.0.0/16 in its first VPC, and so does the company it later buys, and so does the office network. Peering and transit hubs refuse overlapping ranges, and the fixes (NAT between them, or private endpoints for each service) are complex and permanent. Plan your addresses on day one as if every network you own will one day need to talk to every other. Second, connect services, not networks, when you can. A private endpoint exposes one door with one lock. Peering two whole networks joins everything and makes every security rule on both sides part of your wall.

Hybrid and many-cloud

Real companies end up with their own buildings plus two or three clouds. The patterns that hold up are boring on purpose:

  • Hub and spoke. Each cloud gets a transit hub; on-site and cross-cloud links land only on hubs, never spoke to spoke. You get one place for routes, firewalls and logs per cloud.
  • One address plan across everything, with no overlaps, kept in a single address book (IPAM) and checked by automation before a range is ever handed out.
  • Two paths, different kinds. A private line for steady traffic, plus an encrypted VPN over the Internet as backup. Clouds route by BGP over both, so failover is a routing event you can test (Sheet 03).
  • Design to the smallest MTU on any path: 1500 or less wherever a VPN or the Internet is involved, whatever the inside of each cloud allows.
  • Identity over location. Across clouds, addresses mean less and less; the zero-trust ideas of Sheet 06 (check who's calling, every time) travel better than "anything from 10.4/16 is ours."

Fixing what you can't see

Lots of people find troubleshooting overlays maddening, and that is fair. The trick that makes them work, hiding the real network, is the same trick that hides the fault. A few patterns cover most of the pain.

"Ping works, the website hangs"

This is the classic. Small packets fit, so pings, DNS and the TCP handshake all work. Then the first full-size data packet goes out at 1500 bytes. Wrapped, it's 1550, too big for the real wire. If the "don't fragment" bit is set, a router should send back an ICMP "too big" message so the sender can shrink (path MTU discovery, Sheet 04). But that ICMP often comes from a hall router to the VTEP, not to the machine that sent the packet. Or a firewall on the way throws all ICMP away. The big packet just vanishes, again and again. The connection hangs after "connected." This failure is called a black hole, and RFC 4459 catalogues it for every kind of tunnel.

Fixes, from best to worst: make the underlay MTU big enough that the envelope never matters (jumbo frames inside the building); set the pretend network's MTU smaller and tell every machine (clouds do this by handing out the right MTU when the machine starts); have edges rewrite TCP's "largest piece I'll accept" (MSS clamping), which helps TCP only; and use packet-layer MTU probing (RFC 8899), which doesn't need ICMP at all. Never "fix" it by blocking less ICMP in one place and hoping.

In production · the 63-second wait

Kubernetes over VXLAN, Linux kernels before 5.7

Teams running Calico's VXLAN mode reported a strange, exact symptom: some connections from pods to services took about 63 seconds to start, then worked fine. The cause was down in the envelope code. With VXLAN checksum offload turned on, a kernel bug in this path left some wrapped packets with bad UDP checksums, and the far end quietly threw them away. The newer kernel fixed it. Calico now turns VXLAN offload off by default. Why 63? TCP resends a lost SYN after 1 second, then waits twice as long each time: 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 = 63. A connection whose first several attempts are dropped and whose sixth retry gets through waits exactly that long.

An exact, repeating number is a clue: 3, 7, 15, 31 or 63 seconds means "SYNs are being lost." And the loss lived in a layer neither the application team nor the hall-network team was looking at. Overlay problems sit in the gap between teams. Make sure someone owns the gap.

A way to look, layer by layer

  1. Is it the pretend network or the real one? Send the same test inside the overlay (machine to machine) and on the underlay (VTEP to VTEP). If only the inside fails, suspect mapping, security rules or MTU; if both fail, it's the halls.
  2. Probe with sizes, not just pings. Ping with "don't fragment" at 1400, 1450, 1472, 1500 bytes. The size where it stops is your real MTU.
  3. Ask the host switch what it thinks. Its mapping entry for the target, its counters for drops by rule, its flow cache. Most "the network ate it" turns out to be a security rule doing its job.
  4. Capture both envelopes. On the host, capture inside the tunnel and on the real card at once, and match them up. Tools that decode VXLAN/Geneve show the inner packet in the outer one.
  5. Follow the hash. If one conversation is slow and others aren't, its source-port hash may land on a bad underlay path. Change the inner port and see if the problem moves.
Hide the real network vs. see the real network

Hide it

Customers get their own addresses, walls and rules, moved and changed in seconds, with no ticket to the hall team. The underlay stays simple and the same for everyone. This is what makes a cloud possible.

See it

Every hidden layer is a place a fault can hide, a few more bytes and lookups per packet, and a fate shared by tenants who think they are apart. Speed problems and MTU problems cross the line no matter how clean the design.

Hide it from the customer; never hide it from the operator. The overlays that run well publish their envelope sizes, keep the halls bigger than the letters, and build tools that can follow one customer packet through both layers.

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01 · capacity calculation

A fabric runs VXLAN over IPv6 on an underlay whose MTU is 1500. Customers want a 1500-byte MTU inside. (a) Does it fit? (b) What underlay MTU do you need? (c) If you instead give customers the biggest MTU that fits, what is it, and what fraction of a 200-byte packet's wire size is envelope (count from the outer IP header)?

(a) No: VXLAN over IPv6 adds 40 + 8 + 8 + 14 = 70 bytes, so a 1500-byte inner packet is 1570 on the wire. (b) At least 1570; in practice set jumbo frames (9000+) so you never think about it again. (c) 1500 − 70 = 1430 bytes. A 200-byte packet becomes 270, so 70 ÷ 270 ≈ 26% is envelope.

Check yourself — 02 · threat modeling

A team offers bare-metal servers in the same VXLAN fabric as virtual machines, and lets customers run their own VXLAN software on them "for flexibility." List what goes wrong and two ways to fix it.

The VNI is a plain, unsigned number, and isolation depends on the provider holding the pen. A customer who writes their own envelopes can put any VNI and inner address on them and inject packets into other tenants' networks, or flood them. Fixes: (1) Move the VTEP off the customer's reach, onto the top-of-rack switch port, or onto a provider-controlled SmartNIC/DPU in the server, so the customer sends plain frames and the provider wraps them. (2) At the ToR, accept only plain frames from bare-metal ports and drop any UDP 4789/6081 they send toward VTEPs. Also filter tunnel ports at the building edge, and encrypt the underlay where it crosses untrusted links.

Check yourself — 03 · system design

Your company has a VPC at 10.0.0.0/16 in one cloud. You just bought a company whose VPC in another cloud is also 10.0.0.0/16, and both need to call the other's payments API and nothing else. Re-addressing will take a year. What do you build now, and what do you change for the future?

Now: don't join the networks. Peering and transit hubs refuse overlapping ranges anyway, and joining whole networks is more access than needed. Expose each payments API as a service through a private endpoint or a load balancer behind NAT in a small, non-overlapping "shared services" range, reached over an encrypted cross-cloud link landed on each side's hub. For the future: one company-wide address plan in IPAM, no overlaps, checked by automation; hub-and-spoke per cloud; and a plan to re-address the bought company's networks into it over the year.

Why does VXLAN set the outer UDP source port from a hash of the inner packet?

Small requests to a service in a new overlay work, but big downloads hang right after connecting. What's the first thing to check?

Put each letter in a bigger envelope, keep a good book of who lives behind which desk, let only the building hold the pen, and keep the halls a little bigger than the biggest letter. Then two hundred companies can each have a network that feels like their own, built, moved and torn down in seconds, on wires none of them will ever see.

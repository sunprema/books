Watching a Running Network, and What Comes Next

Network people call this: measurement, operations & future networks

The other nine sheets were about how a network is built. This one is about the part that never ends: keeping it well. How do you know it is sick before the users tell you? How much sickness did you promise to allow? Why do most networks break the moment someone tries to help them? How do you check a change before you make it? And what new kinds of network are coming? At the end, four big real networks show every sheet of this book working at once.

Picture a hospital ward at night. Nobody can see inside the patients, so nurses watch numbers: heartbeat, breathing, temperature. Some machines report every second. Some numbers are only checked when a nurse walks by every few hours. Now and then a nurse does a small test on purpose (a finger prick, "squeeze my hand") to see how the patient answers.

Here is what goes wrong. A machine that reports the average heartbeat every hour will miss a heart that stopped for a minute. A ward that promised "no patient is ever unwell" can't keep that promise and stops trying to keep any promise. The ward has exactly enough beds, so one broken bed means people sleep in the hall. And the worst nights are not the ones when a patient gets sick on their own. They are the ones when somebody gives the wrong medicine to every patient at once.

The network is the patient. This sheet is the ward's way of working: what to watch, how often, what to promise, how much spare room to keep, how to try a medicine on a practice dummy first, and what new kinds of patient are coming through the door.

Reading the patient's numbers

There are two ways to learn how a network is doing. You can watch the real traffic go by. This is passive measurement: counters, records of conversations, copies of a few packets. Or you can send your own test traffic and see how it is treated. This is "active measurement" (§10.2). Watching real traffic tells you what users actually got, but only where users are. Sending tests tells you about a path even at 3 a.m. when nobody is on it, but it tells you how your test was treated, which is not always how users are treated.

Asking every five minutes vs. being told right away

The oldest number is the counter: every port on every box keeps a running count of bytes, packets, drops and errors. For decades a central machine asked each box for its counters every five minutes, using SNMP. Subtract two readings, divide by 300 seconds, and you have the average speed. That average is the hourly-heartbeat problem. A port that is full for two seconds and empty for the rest of the five minutes looks less than 1% busy, but packets fell on the floor during those two seconds. These short floods are called "microbursts," and on fast data-centre links they are where much of the loss lives.

The fix turns the asking around. With streaming telemetry, the central machine says once "tell me about these things," and the box pushes updates by itself: every few seconds or less, or whenever a value changes. gNMI's Subscribe call (Sheet 07) does this, and the "things" are named by the same YANG and OpenConfig models used for configuration. So "what is this port's state" and "what did I set this port to" have the same name. Finer numbers cost more to carry and store, so people stream the few numbers that matter quickly and keep slow polling for the rest.

Who was talking to whom

Counters say how much. They don't say who. For that, routers keep flow records: one line per conversation, keyed by the five numbers of Sheet 02 (source, destination, two ports, protocol), with packet and byte counts and start and end times. Cisco's NetFlow started this. Its version 9 became the IETF's IPFIX (RFC 7011). Both send a "template" first, which says which fields will follow and in what order. Then the records themselves are just numbers in that order. A record of every flow costs a great deal of memory on a fast router, so most routers keep records for only one packet in N.

sFlow (RFC 3176) goes further: it takes one packet in N, every time, and sends off the first part of it plus regular counter readings. N runs from about 100 on a 1 Gb/s link to tens of thousands on a box that moves terabits. Lots of people distrust sampled numbers at first, and that is fair: how can one packet in 10,000 tell you anything? The answer is that the error depends on how many samples you got, not on N.

Counters, flows and samples all describe a box from the outside. They can't tell you where in the path a packet waited. In-band Network Telemetry (INT, Sheet 07) lets the packet carry a small notebook: each switch writes its name, how long the packet waited, and how full its queue was. The last switch tears the notebook out and sends it to the collector. Now you can say "the 4 ms of delay was all in switch 17's third queue." The cost is extra bytes in the packet and a flood of notebooks to store, so it is often turned on for a sample of packets, or only when something looks wrong.

Doing a small test on purpose

Ping is the finger prick everyone knows: send a packet, wait for it to come back, time it. It has two problems. Routers answer ping with their slow general-purpose CPU, and many limit how many they answer, so "this hop is slow" often means "this router doesn't care about ping." And ping only gives a round trip, so you can't tell whether the slow half was going or coming back.

The IETF's test packet for this job is STAMP (RFC 8762, 2020), a simpler follow-on to TWAMP (RFC 5357). A "Session-Sender" sends numbered, time-stamped test packets over UDP (port 862 by default) to a "Session-Reflector." The reflector stamps when it got each one and when it sent it back, copies in the sender's number and stamp, and returns it. One packet coming back carries four clock readings, which is enough to measure delay, delay jitter and loss, in both directions. Simple STAMP packets look just like "TWAMP Light" packets on the wire, so old and new boxes can test each other.

Finding the way, without being fooled

Traceroute sends packets that are set to die after 1 hop, then 2, then 3, and each router that kills one sends back a note saying "died here." Line the notes up and you have the path. But Sheet 02's ECMP hashes each packet's header to pick one of several equal paths, and classic traceroute changes a header field (the port) on every test. So test 3 may go left, and test 4 may go right, and the drawing joins routers that aren't joined at all. Paris traceroute keeps every field the hash looks at fixed, so all tests take one path. Change the fixed values on purpose and you can find every path in turn.

In production · every server pricks every other

Pingmesh in Microsoft's data centres (SIGCOMM 2015)

Microsoft wanted to answer one question all the time: "what is the delay between any two servers, right now?" Pingmesh runs a small agent on every server. A central controller hands each agent a list of peers to test with TCP and HTTP pings, drawn at three levels: servers in the same rack, racks across the data centre (one set of pings between each pair of top-of-rack switches), and data centre to data centre. The agents produced tens of terabytes of delay numbers a day. Those numbers settled the oldest fight in operations — "is it the network?" — in minutes instead of days. They tracked a delay promise for each server, rack and data centre, and they found broken switches that dropped packets without telling anyone, by noticing that every path through one switch was losing a little.

Test from where the users are, everywhere, all the time, and the network becomes something you can see instead of something you argue about. The probes were cheap; the value was in covering every pair.

How much sickness did you promise?

"The network should never be down" sounds like a good promise. It is the worst one you can make, because it is never true, so nobody can tell how badly it is being broken. The working answer, from Google's SRE practice, comes in three layers.

  • A service level indicator (SLI) is a number you measure: "the share of test packets between any two racks that came back in under 1 ms," or "the share of DNS answers that worked."
  • A service level objective (SLO) is the goal for that number over a stretch of time: "99.9% over any 30 days."
  • A service level agreement (SLA) is a promise to a customer with a price attached: credits or money back if it is missed. It is set looser than the SLO, so you hear the alarm well before you owe anyone.

The clever part is what is left over. If the goal is 99.9%, then 0.1% of the time you are allowed to be bad. That allowance is the error budget, and it is a budget in the money sense: you spend it. A risky change, a planned upgrade, a test of a new path, all spend a bit of it. While there is budget left, change freely. When it runs out, stop changing things and fix things. The fight between "move fast" and "keep it steady" becomes a number both sides can read.

The "if, and only if" on the last line is where most designs fool themselves: two copies that share a power feed, a software version or a config push are one copy (§10.5).

An alarm that rings at the right speed

When should the alarm wake someone up? "Any bad minute" pages people all night for things that don't matter. "When the budget is gone" is too late. The answer is to watch the burn rate: how fast you are spending budget compared with plan. A burn rate of 1 spends exactly the whole budget in 30 days. A burn rate of 14.4 spends 2% of it in one hour. Google's SRE Workbook suggests three alarms, each with a long window (to ignore blips) and a short one (so the alarm stops once things are fixed).

Keeping spare beds

A ward with exactly enough beds is full every night, and one broken bed puts a patient in the hall. Networks are the same, with two extra rules from earlier sheets. First, Sheet 05's queues: delay doesn't grow evenly with load; it shoots up as a link gets close to full. Second, Sheet 03's routing: when a link fails, its traffic doesn't vanish; it moves onto the links that are left. So capacity planning is not "is there enough for today's traffic?" It is "is there enough for next year's traffic after the worst likely failure, at a load where the queues stay short?"

The spare room you must keep is set by the failure you plan to survive and by how long it takes to buy more, not by today's average. And use a high percentile of traffic (the busy-hour 95th), not the daily mean, or the plan will look good on exactly the days nobody is using the network.

Most harm comes from the medicine

If you ask "what breaks networks?", the honest answer surprises people. It is mostly not cables, and not boxes dying of old age. Two careful studies looked at real failures. Gill, Jain and Nagappan (SIGCOMM 2011) studied a year of failures in a large cloud's data centres. Cheap top-of-rack and aggregation switches were very reliable. The clever middle boxes were not: about one in five load balancers had a failure, mostly short ones, from software bugs, configuration mistakes and hardware faults.

Govindan and colleagues at Google ("Evolve or Die," SIGCOMM 2016) went through more than a hundred serious failures across Google's data centres and two wide-area networks. Failures were spread evenly across Sheet 01's three planes: data, control and management. A large share happened while somebody was making a change. And no single fix would have stopped more than a small part of them. Their advice: layers of defence; keep the planes agreeing with each other; when something very big goes wrong, "fail open" (keep the last good state rather than shut everything); go slowly and in stages; and find the root cause fast. The title is the point: a network you stop changing gets worse, so make changing it safe.

In production · one file, every server

Cloudflare, 18 November 2025

Not an attack, Cloudflare said, though for the first minutes it looked like one. A permission change in a database cluster changed what one query returned, and that query built a "feature file" for bot detection that is pushed to every server every few minutes. The file came out about twice its normal size. The proxy software that reads it had a fixed limit on how many features it would take, went over the limit, and failed. Because the file was rebuilt every few minutes, and only some parts of the cluster had the new permission yet, the network flipped between working and broken. That looked like an attack coming in waves, and slowed the diagnosis. Core traffic came back about three hours later, once the bad file was stopped and a known-good one was pushed everywhere.

The "medicine" doesn't have to be a router config. Any file that reaches every server at once is a change to the whole network, and needs the same care: check it like code, roll it out in stages, and make the reader fail safe on bad input instead of falling over.

Sheet 03 told Meta's October 2021 story (a command to check backbone capacity cut off every data centre, and the check that should have stopped it had a bug) and Sheet 07 told Google's June 2019 one (a change meant for a few machines stopped the network's control jobs in several regions). They share a lesson that belongs here: the tools you fix the network with must not need the network you are fixing. In both cases the way in for engineers, the dashboards, or the badge readers on the doors rode the same network that had just gone dark. Keep a separate, boring way in, an "out-of-band" network, and test it the way you'd test a fire exit.

Change often vs. change rarely

Change rarely

Most failures happen during changes, so make fewer of them. Bundle the work into big, carefully planned windows, and leave a working network alone.

Change often, in small steps

Big rare changes are the riskiest kind: many things change at once, people are out of practice, and you can't tell which part broke. Small daily changes are each easy to check and easy to undo.

Big operators chose "often and small," with the error budget as the brake. That only works with the machinery to make each step safe: staged rollout (one box, one site, one region), automatic checks between stages, and a rollback that is tested, not hoped for.

Trying the medicine on a practice dummy

If changes are where trouble comes from, check each change before you make it. Sheet 07 met the tools; here is how they are used. Network verification means proving a fact about every packet, not testing a few. Data-plane checkers (Header Space Analysis, VeriFlow) take the real forwarding tables and answer questions like "can any packet from the guest network reach the payment servers?" or "is there any loop?" for all possible headers at once. Config checkers like Batfish go one step earlier. They read the configs you are about to push, work out what routes and tables those configs would produce, and check the same questions on the result. The change is tested before any box sees it.

A network digital twin is the full practice dummy: a model of the network, kept up to date from its real telemetry and configs, where you can try "what if this link fails during the busy hour?" or "what if we push this change?" and watch what happens. The IETF's research group on management is still writing down what a twin should be (an Internet-Draft, not yet an RFC, in 2026). The honest limit is in the word "model": a twin built from configs knows what the routers should do, not the bug in one line card that makes it do something else. It is strongest for routing and reachability, and weakest for delay under load and software bugs.

Intent-based networking (RFC 9315) closes the loop. You say what you want in plain outcome words, "the payment servers are reachable only from the shop front, with under 5 ms delay," and the system works out the configs. The important half is "assurance": it keeps checking, with telemetry and verification, that the intent is still true, and says so when it stops being true. Without that half, intent is just a nicer way to write configs.

Let the machine act vs. let it advise

Let it act

"AIOps" means using machine learning on the flood of telemetry to spot odd patterns, group a thousand alarms into one problem, and guess the root cause. At four or five nines, a person is too slow; only automation can react in time.

Let it advise

A learned model can be confidently wrong, can't always explain itself, and its training data has few examples of the rare, huge failure it most needs to handle. An automation that acts on a wrong guess at every site at once is the Cloudflare file all over again.

Let machines act fast on narrow, well-understood fixes (drain a bad link, roll back the last stage) with hard limits on how much they can change at once. Let them advise on everything else, and check their advice against verification before anything is pushed.

New kinds of patient

Locks that a future computer can't pick

The key-swap math in Sheet 06's TLS, SSH and IPsec (RSA and elliptic curves) could be broken by a big enough quantum computer. None exists yet. But someone can record your locked traffic today and open it once one does: "harvest now, decrypt later." So the key swap has to change first. NIST published ML-KEM (FIPS 203) in August 2024, a key swap built on different math (lattices) that quantum computers aren't known to break. Its draft move-over plan (NIST IR 8547) marks the old methods as "use at your own risk" after 2030 and not allowed after 2035. In practice the Internet is already moving: browsers, big CDNs and OpenSSH use a hybrid key swap, old curve plus ML-KEM together, so it is safe if either half holds. Cloudflare Radar shows a large and growing share of its TLS traffic using it.

The first message of a handshake no longer fits in one packet. That is a network problem, not a crypto one: a middlebox that only reads the first packet of a hello, or drops "odd" sized ones, breaks the connection. Sheet 01's warning about boxes in the middle that assume the past, again.

A network that can't copy

A quantum network is not a faster Internet. It moves "qubits," and one law of physics, the "no-cloning" rule, says a qubit can't be copied. So the Internet's oldest trick, read the signal and send a fresh copy (every repeater, every router buffer, every resend), is forbidden. RFC 9340, from the IRTF, says the basic thing a quantum network hands out is not bits but entanglement: a pair of linked particles at two places. Neighbouring nodes make linked pairs across one link; a middle node can "swap" two short links into one long one; and the two ends then use their pair for a job, such as making a shared secret key that can't be secretly read (QKD).

Distance is the hard wall. Light fades in fibre by about 0.16–0.2 dB per km, and with no copying you can't top it up, so fibre links top out around a hundred kilometres. In 2017 China's Micius satellite sent linked photon pairs to two ground stations 1,203 km apart, because most of a satellite's path is empty space. It worked at about one pair per second. Quantum networks today are small, slow, and special-purpose. Note also that QKD and post-quantum locks solve the same worry two ways, and for nearly everyone the software lock (ML-KEM) is the one that's arriving.

Networks for AI, and AI in networks

"AI-native networking" means two different things. One is networks built for AI: training a large model ties thousands of accelerators together, and every step waits for the slowest message. That workload is all-at-once, all-to-all, and hates tail delay — Sheet 04's data-centre transports (DCTCP, Swift) under the hardest load yet. The Ultra Ethernet Consortium's Specification 1.0 (June 2025) is one answer: an Ethernet and IP stack with its own memory-to-memory transport, congestion control and spraying of packets over many paths, meant to scale to very large clusters. The other is AI inside networks: 6G work and the AIOps of §10.6, where learned models help schedule radios and run operations. Read the first as a real change in traffic; read the second with the tension above in mind.

Networks in space

Sheet 09 showed low-orbit satellites as a moving network with a 15-second heartbeat. Go further out and the Internet's basic idea breaks: an end-to-end path to Mars is never all there at once, and a round trip takes many minutes. TCP's handshake can't work. Delay-tolerant networking keeps the message instead of the path. Its Bundle Protocol (version 7, RFC 9171, 2022) is store-and-forward mail: each node holds a "bundle" until the next link appears, then passes it on. A bundle carries everything the far end needs, because there is no time to ask. And the protocol itself doesn't promise delivery, which is the end-to-end argument in its oldest form.

Capstone 1 · hyperscale data centre

Google's Jupiter loses its spine (SIGCOMM 2022)

For years Google's data-centre fabric was a classic Clos: racks, aggregation blocks, and a big "spine" layer joining every block to every other. The spine had to be bought, wired and upgraded all at once, and it held back mixing old and new speeds. Google replaced it with optical circuit switches: mirrors that steer light from one fibre to another, no packets read at all. Aggregation blocks now connect directly to each other, and a central SDN controller sets both the routes and the wiring itself to match the traffic. Google reported 5× more speed and capacity, 30% less spending and 41% less power, with throughput like a Clos on real traffic.

Sheet 02's fabric and Sheet 07's controller merged: when the controller can re-wire the network, the topology itself becomes something you tune with measurements. That only works because the traffic between blocks is measured well enough to plan on.

Capstone 2 · global CDN

One address, hundreds of places

A CDN like Cloudflare's tells BGP the same address block from every site it runs. Sheet 03's route choice then takes each user to a nearby site, with no DNS tricks. The same choice spreads a DDoS flood over hundreds of sites (Sheet 06), so each site soaks up a small part. An overloaded site can withdraw its routes and push users to its neighbours. The catch is that BGP doesn't know about load or delay, and a route change in the middle of a TCP connection can send its packets to a site that has never heard of it, so CDNs put real work into keeping connections steady. And because every site runs the same software and the same config, one bad push reaches all of them, which is the November 2025 story.

Anycast turns "many sites" into "one network," which is its strength against attacks and its weakness against your own mistakes. Measure from the user side (what delay did they get, from which site), because the routers only know what BGP chose, not whether it was good.

Capstone 3 · enterprise zero-trust network

No inside, only checked requests

Sheet 06's BeyondCorp idea, grown into NIST SP 800-207: no network location is trusted, and every request is checked for who the user is, what device it comes from, and what state the device is in, at an access proxy in front of each application. The old private network becomes just a way to carry packets, often SD-WAN tunnels over ordinary Internet links. Operations change the most. "Can Alice reach the payroll app?" is no longer a firewall rule; it is a policy, and the right check is verification against that policy, plus logs of every decision. The common failure is no longer an open port. It is a policy engine or identity provider that goes down and locks everyone out, so it needs its own error budget and its own break-glass way in.

Zero trust moves the single point of failure from the edge firewall to the policy engine. Watch and plan capacity for it like the most important box in the network, because it is.

Capstone 4 · 5G edge deployment

Delay you can sell, if you can measure it

An operator selling "under 20 ms to the edge" for factory robots or cloud games needs every piece of Sheet 09: a standalone 5G core, a local UPF in the city so traffic doesn't trombone, a slice or priority on the radio, and edge servers in a metro site. Then this sheet starts. The promise is per slice and per cell, so it needs probes from inside each cell to the edge site, an SLI like "share of probes under 20 ms in each 5-minute window," and an error budget per customer. Capacity planning is the hardest part, because Shannon (Sheet 09) caps what a busy cell can carry no matter how the slices are labelled, and the edge site is small, so one lost server is a big share of it.

A delay promise is only as real as the measurement behind it. If you can't measure it per cell and per slice, you can't sell it, and you can't tell when you've broken it.

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01 · measurement design

An app team says, "your network drops our packets every afternoon." Your 5-minute SNMP graphs show every link under 30%. Name three measurements you would add, in order, and what each could prove.

(1) Port drop counters streamed at 1 s or finer (gNMI), or queue-depth INT on the suspected path: proves or rules out microbursts, which a 5-minute average under 30% cannot see. (2) Probes from the app's own servers to their peers (Pingmesh-style TCP pings, or STAMP between racks): proves whether loss is in the network at all, and where, by which pairs lose. (3) Flow records or sFlow on the busy ports at those times: shows who fills the queue (a backup job starting at 2 p.m., say). If the probes are clean while the app still sees loss, the problem is likely in the hosts (Sheet 04's receive buffers, NIC drops).

Check yourself — 02 · error budget calculation

A service has a 99.95% SLO over 30 days. On day 3, a bad change makes 2% of requests fail for 40 minutes. (a) What is the whole budget, in full-outage minutes? (b) What share did the incident spend? (c) What was its burn rate, and would the fast (14.4 × over 1 h) page ring? When?

(a) 0.0005 × 43,200 = 21.6 minutes. (b) 0.02 × 40 = 0.8 full-outage minutes, about 3.7% of the budget. (c) Burn rate = 0.02 ÷ 0.0005 = 40×. The fast page needs the 1-hour average to reach 14.4 × 0.0005 = 0.72% bad, i.e. 0.432 bad-minutes inside an hour; at 2% that takes 0.432 ÷ 0.02 ≈ 22 minutes (ignoring background). So it pages about 22 minutes in, during the incident. A good rollout would have caught it sooner, at the first stage.

Check yourself — 03 · failure analysis

Your firm's two "independent" data-centre uplinks, each 99.95% available, went down together twice last year. Your report claimed 99.99998%. List three ways two such links are often not independent, and how you would measure or fix each.

(1) Shared physical path: both fibres in the same trench or duct, cut by one digger. Ask each carrier for route maps and demand separate paths. (2) Shared software or config: both edge routers run the same version and get the same push, so one bug or one bad change hits both. Stage changes so the two never change on the same day, and verify configs first. (3) Shared dependence above them: one BGP session to one provider, one DNS service, or one power feed. Test it: fail one side on purpose during a quiet hour, and count past events where both failed within the same minute; that count is your real joint failure rate, not the product of the two.

Check yourself — 04 · capacity planning

Four 400 Gb/s links carry a busy-hour 95th-percentile load of 900 Gb/s, growing 35% a year, and new links take 6 months to arrive. You want every link under 80% after any single failure. Do you need to order now?

After one failure there are 3 × 400 = 1,200 Gb/s; 80% of that is 960 Gb/s. Today 900 Gb/s fits. In 6 months, at 35% a year: 900 × 1.35^0.5 ≈ 1,046 Gb/s, which is over 960. You cross the line in about log(960/900) ÷ log(1.35) ≈ 0.22 years, under 3 months. So yes, and you are already late: order now, and in the meantime plan a traffic-engineering move (Sheet 03's segment routing) or a temporary lower target.

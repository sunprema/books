How Every Box Learns the Way, and Who Decides Which Ways Are Allowed

Network people call this: routing: algorithms, protocols & policy

Sheet 02 showed the box reading its table in two billionths of a second. This sheet is about who writes that table: boxes that talk to each other, a few ways of talking that each break in their own way, and companies that decide which roads they will carry for whom, and for how much money.

Picture a huge town with no map. At every corner stands a helper. A helper can only talk to the helpers on the next corners, and it has to answer one question all day long: "someone wants to get to the bakery — which street do I send them down?"

One morning a bridge falls down. The helpers who used to send people over it don't know yet. Some of them hear old news from a neighbor ("the bakery is only three streets from me!") and believe it, even though that neighbor was counting on the very bridge that just fell. People walk in circles. And the town is really many towns, each run by a different company, and those companies don't send strangers through their streets for free.

This sheet stays in that town. Everything in it is one of three things: a way for helpers to share what they know, a way for them to stop believing old news, or a rule about whose streets you're allowed to use.

Two ways to learn the way

There are really only two ideas, and every protocol on this sheet is one of them or a mix.

Pass along distances. Each helper tells its neighbors, "here is how far I am from every place I know." You hear those lists, add the length of the street to each neighbor, and keep the shortest. You never see the town; you only see your neighbors' numbers. This is called distance-vector routing: a list ("vector") of distances, worked out the way a 1950s method by Bellman and Ford does it, one neighbor's report at a time.

Pass along the map. Each helper tells everybody, "these are my streets and how long each one is." Every helper collects every one of those notes, draws the whole map, and works out the shortest way to everywhere by itself. This is link-state routing: share the state of your links, then let each box run a shortest-path search (Dijkstra's) on its own copy of the map.

There is a third idea, built for the space between companies, and it sits between the other two. You pass along not just "how far" but "through whom": the whole list of companies the news passed through. That's path-vector routing, and it's how BGP works. We'll get there in 3.4.

Old news that won't die

Go back to the fallen bridge. Four helpers stand in a row: A (the bakery), then B, C, D. B was one street from A, C was two, D three. The A–B bridge falls. B no longer hears from A. But C is still saying "I'm two from the bakery!" — B can't know that C's two went through B itself. So B thinks, "fine, I'll go through C: that's three." C hears B say three and updates to four. B hears four and goes to five. They ping-pong, one step at a time, toward a number that means "nobody can get there."

This is called counting to infinity. RIP, the oldest common distance-vector protocol, makes "infinity" small on purpose: a distance of 16 means unreachable. That caps a RIP network at 15 hops across, which sounds silly until you see it's what stops the ping-pong in finite time.

The fix in the widget has two parts. Split horizon: don't tell a neighbor about a road you learned from that same neighbor. Poisoned reverse: go further and tell it, out loud, "for me that place is 16." It cures loops between two helpers. It does not cure every loop: three helpers in a ring can still pass old news around the ring if their updates arrive at unlucky moments. The deep reason is the one in figure 3.1 — a distance alone doesn't say how it was reached, so nobody can spot that the road goes through themselves.

Real RIP sends a quick update when something changes, so it's usually faster than this. But the shape is the lesson: the worst case grows with how big "infinity" is, so distance-vector protocols must keep infinity small, which keeps the network small.

Everybody draws the same map

Link-state protocols trade gossip for paperwork. When a link changes, the router at its end writes a short, numbered note — "my link to R7 is down" — and floods it: sends it to every neighbor, who sends it to every neighbor, each one keeping only the newest copy by its number. Within a few moments every router has the same pile of notes, draws the same map, and runs the same shortest-path search. Because they all start from the same map, they all reach answers that agree, and loops only appear in the short window while the notes are still spreading.

The two link-state protocols in wide use are OSPF and IS-IS. They are the same idea wearing different clothes, and the clothes matter more than you'd think:

The price of a shared map is that everyone pays for every change. Every note goes everywhere, and every router re-runs the search. That's why both protocols cut a big network into areas: inside an area you see every street; from outside you see only a short summary ("these addresses are over there, about this far"). Areas limit how far a broken link's note travels, at the cost of hiding detail, and hidden detail sometimes means a worse path.

How fast is "a few moments"? A 2005 study timed each step on deployed routers and showed large service-provider networks could settle in under a second without becoming unstable. The slow step usually isn't the shortest-path search, which is fast on modern processors; it's writing hundreds of thousands of changed lines into the hardware table from Sheet 02. That's why good designs work out a backup next step ahead of time and keep it loaded, so the box can swap to it the moment a link dies, long before the map is redrawn.

Trust your neighbors vs. check for yourself

Pass along distances

Tiny memory, tiny messages, each box can have its own idea of cost. But it can believe old news, and it can't see a loop through itself.

Pass along the map

Quick, loop-free once settled, easy to reason about. But every box must hold the whole map and agree on what "cost" means — which only works inside one company.

That last point decides everything: link-state needs everyone to share one map and one idea of "shortest." Between companies, nobody will share their map, and nobody agrees on what "best" means. So the Internet between companies needs something else.

Between companies: pass along the whole trail

The Internet is tens of thousands of separately run networks. Each one is an autonomous system (AS): a network under one owner with one routing policy, known by a number. Between them runs one protocol, BGP ("Border Gateway Protocol"). Two routers in neighboring networks open a long-lived TCP connection on port 179 and, after the first full exchange, send only changes: "you can now reach these addresses through me" or "you can't anymore."

Every "you can reach this through me" carries the trail: the list of AS numbers it has passed through, newest first. This list is the AS_PATH. It does two jobs at once. It stops loops with one simple check — if you see your own number in the trail, throw the news away — with no shared map needed. And it lets you choose by who, not just how far: "I'd rather not send my traffic through that company."

Notice what the trail fixes. Counting to infinity happened because a distance didn't say how it was reached. The trail says exactly that. B would have seen "A is reached through B" in C's news and refused it on the spot.

Money draws the map

Here is the part that surprises people who learned routing as a shortest-path problem: between companies, the shortest way is rarely the point. The roads that get used are the ones somebody is paid to carry. Two kinds of deal cover most of the Internet:

  • Customer and provider. The customer pays; the provider carries the customer's traffic to and from the whole Internet.
  • Peers. Two networks swap traffic between their own customers, usually for no money, because it saves both of them paying a provider. Neither one carries the other's traffic to anyone else.

From those deals come two rules nearly every network follows. Choosing: prefer a road through a customer (they pay you) over one through a peer (free) over one through a provider (you pay). Telling: tell everyone about roads to your customers; tell only your customers about roads you learned from peers or providers. In 2001 Gao and Rexford proved something lovely: if every network follows these rules, and nobody is their own provider's provider in a circle, BGP is guaranteed to settle down — no central planner needed.

Policy vs. getting there fast

Let every network choose

Each company follows its own business. The Internet works with no one in charge, and it stays stable as long as most follow the common rules.

Find the best road

A shortest-path search would find quicker routes. But it needs one shared map and one shared meaning of "best" — neither of which competing companies will give you.

The Internet chose policy. The cost is real: paths that are longer than they need to be, settling that takes minutes not milliseconds (3.8), and safety that rests on everyone keeping to rules nobody enforces (3.9).

How one router picks among many roads

A big router may hear about the same block of addresses from ten neighbors. It picks one by walking down a fixed list of questions and stopping at the first one that separates them. The order is the whole story, so here it is:

#AskWho sets it, and what it's for
1Highest LOCAL_PREFYou, inside your own network. This is where the money rule lives: customer roads get a high number, providers a low one.
2Shortest AS_PATHThe trail. Fewest companies wins. Others can make theirs look longer by repeating their own number ("prepending").
3Lowest ORIGINHow the route first entered BGP. Rarely decides anything.
4Lowest MEDA neighbor's hint: "come in through this door of mine, not that one." Compared only between routes from the same neighbor, unless you say otherwise.
5Outside over insidePrefer a road learned straight from another company over one passed along inside your own.
6Nearest way outLowest inside-the-network cost to the exit. Get the traffic off your network as soon as you can ("hot potato").
7Lowest router IDA plain tie-breaker so the answer is always the same.
 The standard order (RFC 4271), simplified. Router makers add steps of their own — Cisco's "weight" comes before step 1 — so read your vendor's list, not just the standard.

Two things this list tells you. First, step 1 beats step 2: a network will happily send your traffic across three extra companies if the long road is through a paying customer. Second, every step after 1 is a way other people try to steer you, and step 1 is how you ignore them. People find step 4 maddening, and that's fair: whether two MEDs get compared depends on where each route came from, so adding or losing one route can change which of two other routes wins.

Inside one company: spreading the news without a crowd

BGP between companies is half the job. The routes one border router learns must reach every other router in the company, and that's done with BGP too, run inside (iBGP). There's one strict rule: a router does not pass a route it heard from one inside neighbor to another inside neighbor. The trail can't catch loops inside one company (every router has the same AS number), so this rule does instead. The result is that every router must talk directly to every other one.

A 25× cut, and adding a router means touching two configurations, not a hundred. That's why nearly every large network uses route reflectors.

A route reflector is a router allowed to break the rule for its "clients": it takes routes from one inside neighbor and passes them to the others. To keep that from making loops, it stamps two new fields on what it passes along — who first brought this route in (ORIGINATOR_ID), and which reflector groups it has passed through (CLUSTER_LIST) — and anyone who sees themselves in either drops it.

The catch is what the reflector doesn't pass on. It sends its clients only its own single best road, picked from where the reflector sits. A client on the far side of the network might have chosen a different exit if it had seen all of them. It loses backup roads it could have used the instant a link failed. And with MEDs in the mix, two reflectors can end up flipping each other's choice forever: reflector 1's pick changes what reflector 2 hears, which changes reflector 2's pick, which changes what reflector 1 hears. RFC 3345 describes exactly this, and it's the purest example on this sheet of sensible local choices that don't add up. The usual cures are making MED comparison order-independent, or letting reflectors send more than one road (BGP "add-path").

The inside map (OSPF or IS-IS) and BGP must also share some routes, which people do with redistribution: copying routes from one protocol into another. The costs don't translate — a BGP road has no OSPF distance — so you have to make one up. And copying both ways at two different routers is a classic loop: a route goes out into BGP at one point, comes back into OSPF at another looking new, and gets copied out again. Tag what you copy, and refuse anything already carrying your tag.

Slow to settle: why BGP takes minutes

When a road disappears in a link-state network, every router learns the plain fact and redraws. In BGP, a router that loses its best road just picks its next-best — which may be a road that also went through the broken spot, but nobody's told it yet. It announces that one, neighbors switch to it, then it vanishes too, and they try another. The network walks through many dead roads, one by one, before it gives up or finds a live one. This is called path exploration.

To keep that from becoming a storm of messages, BGP holds back repeat news to each neighbor for a while: the suggested gap between announcements is 30 seconds to outside neighbors. So each wrong guess can cost tens of seconds. In 2000, Labovitz and colleagues injected hundreds of thousands of fake failures into the real Internet from exchange points and measured the result: after a road was withdrawn, the Internet took minutes to agree, passing through many short-lived paths on the way, with lost and looping packets the whole time.

Inside map, tuned (OSPF/IS-IS)

That's why careful networks keep failures inside their own map where possible, and why a lost link between two big companies is felt far longer than a lost link inside one.

There's a deeper instability too. Because each network sets its own preferences, it's possible to write rules that have no stable answer at all: A prefers going through B, B prefers going through C, C prefers going through A, and they chase each other forever. That's why Gao and Rexford's result matters so much — it's not that BGP is safe; it's that BGP is safe if preferences follow the money rule from 3.5.

When someone slips, or lies

BGP was built for a few hundred networks that knew each other. By design, a router believes what its neighbors tell it. Two kinds of failure follow, and they look alike from outside.

A route leak is real news sent to the wrong people: a network passes along roads it should only have used itself, breaking the telling rule from 3.5 — the valley in figure 3.3. A hijack is false news: a network claims to be where an address block lives, when it isn't. Either one can pull huge amounts of traffic the wrong way, and longest-prefix match (Sheet 02) makes it worse — a more exact claim wins over a less exact one, no matter who's telling the truth.

Case · 24 February 2008

A country blocks a video site and takes it off the whole Internet

Pakistan's government ordered the video site YouTube blocked. Pakistan Telecom (AS17557) made a route for 208.65.153.0/24, a slice of YouTube's 208.65.152.0/22, so it could send that traffic nowhere inside the country. The route got out to its provider, PCCW (AS3491), which passed it on without checking. Everywhere it spread, the /24 beat YouTube's own /22 by longest match, and YouTube traffic from around the world headed to Pakistan. YouTube struck back by announcing even more exact /25s. The hijack ended about two hours after it began, when PCCW withdrew Pakistan Telecom's routes.

A local filtering decision became a global outage because one provider didn't check what its customer was allowed to announce. The defense is boring: providers must filter customers.

Case · 24 June 2019

A small "optimizer" and a big provider that didn't filter

DQE (AS33154) ran a tool that makes more-exact routes inside a network to steer traffic. Those routes reached its customer, Allegheny Technologies (AS396531), which passed them up to its other provider, Verizon (AS701). Verizon accepted them and told the world. Thousands of networks' traffic, including large parts of Cloudflare's, now squeezed through a small steel company's links. At worst, Cloudflare lost about 15% of its global traffic.

A textbook valley, made worse by more-exact routes. Two filters would have stopped it: Allegheny refusing to send provider routes up, and Verizon refusing customer routes it had no reason to expect.

Signed slips of paper: who may announce what

The main fix in use today is a set of signed records, the RPKI ("resource public key infrastructure"). The regional bodies that hand out addresses also sign certificates saying who holds which blocks. A holder then signs a small record, a ROA ("route origin authorization"): "this block, up to this exactness, may be announced by this AS." Routers download a checked list of these and sort every incoming route into one of three bins:

BinMeaningUsual action
ValidA record covers this block, and the origin AS and the exactness match.accept
InvalidA record covers this block, but the origin AS or the exactness doesn't match any record.drop
NotFoundNo record covers this block at all.accept (for now)
 Route origin validation (RFC 6811). Only "drop Invalid" adds safety; most of the Internet still has to accept NotFound.

Had YouTube held a ROA saying "only our AS, nothing more exact than /22," Pakistan Telecom's /24 would have been Invalid twice over. By August 2025 about 56% of routed IPv4 space and 59% of IPv6 space was covered by ROAs — real progress, and still a long way from all of it.

Be honest about what it doesn't do. It checks only the last name on the trail, the origin. A liar can just put the true owner's number at the end of a made-up trail and pass. And it does nothing about leaks, where every route is truthfully originated and simply sent to the wrong people. One more trap: a ROA that says "up to /24" when you only announce the /22 leaves the /24s open to exactly that made-up-trail trick, which is why the current best practice (RFC 9319) is to avoid the "up to" field except where you really need it.

Two newer pieces aim at leaks. BGP roles (RFC 9234, 2022) let two neighbors state their deal when they connect — "you're my provider," "we're peers" — and mark routes "only send this down to customers" with a new field, so a leak is caught even several networks away. ASPA lets each network sign the list of its providers, so a receiver can check that a trail is a hill, not a valley. As of late 2026 ASPA is still a draft, not yet a standard.

Case · 4 October 2021

Taking yourself off the map

During routine work, a command meant to check capacity on Facebook's backbone cut every link in it, and a checking tool that should have stopped the command had a bug. Facebook's name servers were built to stop announcing their addresses in BGP if they couldn't reach the data centers — a good rule for one sick site. With the whole backbone gone, every site followed it at once. The routes vanished, nobody could look up Facebook's names, and the tools engineers would use to fix it ran on the same missing network. It took about six hours.

Withdrawing a route is a powerful health check and a dangerous one. A rule that's safe for one site can be fatal when every site obeys it at the same moment.

Writing the route on the packet

Everything so far picks one "best" road per destination, and every packet to that place takes it. Sometimes you want something else: send this customer's traffic the long way round to keep a busy link free; keep two copies of a stream on paths that share no link. That's traffic engineering, and it means steering traffic onto roads the shortest-path search wouldn't pick.

The classic tool is MPLS ("multiprotocol label switching"): the first router sticks a short number, a label, on the packet, and each router after that looks up only the label — "label 42 in, label 17 out, through port 3" — not the address. Add RSVP-TE and you can set up a labeled tunnel along any path you pick. But setting it up means walking the path and asking every router on it to remember the tunnel. Every router in the middle holds a line for every tunnel through it, and refreshes it.

Segment routing turns this around. The first router writes the whole set of directions on the packet as a short list, and each router in the middle just does the top instruction and pops it. There are two main kinds of instruction: "go to router X by the normal shortest way" (a node segment, which also spreads across equal roads) and "leave by this exact link" (an adjacency segment). The inside map protocol (IS-IS or OSPF) hands out the numbers for these, so no separate signaling protocol is needed. The list can be MPLS labels (SR-MPLS) or IPv6 addresses (SRv6).

Worked numbers · what the middle routers must remember
50 edge routers, a tunnel between every pair
50 × 49 = 2,450 one-way tunnels
RSVP-TE, a middle router on many of them
a line per tunnel through it, refreshed — can be thousands
Segment routing, same middle router
one line per router (node segment) + one per own link — about the size of the network, whatever the tunnels

Remembering grows with the size of the network, not with how many paths people ask for. The price moves to the packet: every instruction rides along, and SRv6 instructions are 16 bytes each.

Remember it in the network vs. carry it on the packet

Tunnels kept in every router

Each router can hold bandwidth for a tunnel and refuse new ones when full. Small headers. But lots of state to set up, refresh and repair after a failure.

Directions on the packet

Nothing per-flow in the middle; a central planner can push new paths by talking only to the first router. But no hop-by-hop holding of bandwidth, and longer headers that Sheet 02's readers must reach into.

Same choice as Sheet 01's end-to-end argument, one layer down: keep the middle simple and put the knowledge at the edge. Many networks pair segment routing with a central controller that does the bandwidth sums (Sheet 07).

Check yourself

Check yourself — 01 · trace the old news

Three routers in a ring, X–Y, Y–Z, Z–X, all cost 1. X is also connected to the place P (cost 1), and Y and Z both reach P through X. The X–P link breaks. Why might split horizon not save you here?

Y learned P from X, so it won't tell X — but it will tell Z "P: 2." If X's "P is gone" reaches Z before Y's old "P: 2" does, Z believes Y and routes via Y, and tells X "P: 3." X now believes Z. The news chases itself around the ring and counts up to 16. Split horizon only blocks telling a road back to the one you heard it from; it can't see a loop of three.

Check yourself — 02 · which road wins

Your router hears 203.0.113.0/24 two ways. Route 1: from a customer, LOCAL_PREF 200, trail 64500 64501 64502 64503. Route 2: from a peer, LOCAL_PREF 100, trail 64510, with a lower MED. Which wins, and what one change would flip it?

Route 1. LOCAL_PREF is asked first, and 200 beats 100; the trail length and MED are never looked at. To flip it, you would have to change LOCAL_PREF (set the peer route at or above 200, or the customer route below 100). Nothing the peer does with MED or a short trail can override your local preference.

Check yourself — 03 · configuration review

A regional network has two providers and sends each one everything in its BGP table. Its address block has a ROA. A colleague says, "We're safe from leaks — we have RPKI." Find the mistake and the fix.

Sending provider A's routes to provider B is the leak — a valley. The ROA only proves who may originate the network's own block; it says nothing about passing along other people's routes, which are all truthfully originated. Fix: export only your own and your customers' routes to providers and peers (tag routes by where they were learned and filter on the tag), and turn on BGP roles where your router supports them.

Check yourself — 04 · design

You run 300 routers that need full BGP routes. How many inside sessions does a full mesh need, and how many with two route reflectors that peer with each other and with every other router? Name one thing you lose.

Full mesh: 300 × 299 ÷ 2 = 44,850. Reflectors: 298 clients × 2 + 1 = 597. You lose path variety: each client sees only the reflector's best road, chosen from the reflector's spot in the network, so exits can be worse than hot-potato and backup roads aren't pre-loaded. Add-path, or placing reflectors with the network's shape in mind, wins some of it back.

Why can't the Internet just run one link-state protocol between all networks?

Once you see that routing is half shortest-path math and half who-pays-whom, the strange parts — the slow settling, the leaks, the signed slips of paper, the directions written on the packet — stop looking like bugs and start looking like the price of a network with no one in charge that still mostly gets you to the bakery.

1. Introduction to Data Pipelines and Event Streaming

Let's start with the problem, not the tool. Say you're building a system with an orders service, a billing service, and a shipping service. The orders service knows the moment an order is placed. Billing needs to know about it. So does shipping. So, eventually, does the analytics warehouse someone builds six months from now.

The easy first move is: orders calls billing's API directly, and orders calls shipping's API directly. It works, for a while. Then someone adds a fourth consumer, and a fifth, and now the orders service is holding a growing list of "everyone who needs to know when something happens" — and every one of those calls can fail, time out, or need retrying independently. Orders has become a switchboard operator for a problem it never signed up for.

The core move Stop calling consumers directly. Publish what happened once, to one place, and let anyone who cares read it from there — on their own schedule, at their own pace.

That's event streaming: instead of services calling each other, they publish events — "order placed", "payment captured", "package shipped" — to a durable log, and anyone interested reads from that log. Apache Kafka is the piece of infrastructure that plays that role at scale: a distributed event streaming platform built for exactly this "publish once, consume by many, independently" shape.

Notice what actually changed: not the data, but who is coupled to whom. Orders now depends on nothing downstream. Billing and shipping each depend only on the event log — not on each other, not on orders being available right this second.

The data-contract problem hiding underneath

Decoupling the calls is the easy 80%. Here's the part that bites teams later: what if the orders service adds a field to its event, or renames one, or changes a number's type? Every consumer reading that event — billing, shipping, the warehouse job nobody remembers writing — has an implicit assumption about the event's shape. Break that assumption and things fail, but not where you'd want: not at build time, in a compiler error, but at 2am, in a consumer that just crashed on a field it expected to be there.

What's missing is a data contract: an explicit, checked agreement about the shape of an event, shared between whoever writes it and whoever reads it. Without one, "the schema" is just whatever the last person who touched the producer code happened to write — an oral tradition, not a contract.

Pitfall "It's just JSON, we'll figure out the fields as we go" is not a data contract. It's a promise nobody's checking — and nobody finds out it was broken until a consumer throws in production.

That's the gap the rest of this book fills. Kafka gets events from producers to consumers, reliably and at scale — that's chapter 2. Avro gives an event an explicit, typed shape — chapter 3. And the Schema Registry is what turns "we should really document the schema somewhere" into something Kafka itself enforces — chapters 4 and 5. By the end you'll have a small, real producer and consumer wired through all three.
